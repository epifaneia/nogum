1.4 The Control Panel (the Tone Cards)

Forget memorising tables. As a NOGUM game master your job is to carry the pace, not the maths. Every rule of each mode is condensed into tone cards. Print the sheet or keep it on screen. When the scene changes, look at the card that applies: that is the only rule that exists right now.

How to read any card

  1. Always roll both d10s, one red and one blue. The card tells you which number you use: the red one only, the sum, the higher or the lower.
  2. Look at that number before adding anything: if it falls in the fumble band, it is a fumble; if it falls in the critical band, it is a critical. The bands take priority over everything else and do not depend on the bonus. ED-1 ED-18
  3. If there is no fumble and no critical, add your total bonus (skill + difficulty + gear + effects, always between −2 and +4; see chapter 4) and compare it with the card’s TN.
  4. On the cards that carry it, just making it costs you: if the total equals the TN or beats it by one, you get there and the GM picks the price. It never turns a failure into a success: it only charges a toll on what had already worked. ED-23

It works out as often as on First Gear up to +3 (at +4, 80 % against 90 %), but nothing is safe and nothing is free: fumble, cost and critical all sit near 20 % throughout. It is not a ball growing towards something: it is a leaf in the wind fighting to land anywhere at all and never managing it. Each single toll looks like nothing much, and three scenes later nobody can say when it went wrong. It is the one that asks most of the GM: four rolls in ten bring something to invent.

And it is called comedy on purpose. A world like this frustrates from two directions: because nothing comes off clean, and because what lies underneath is usually ugly. The card carries one of them, the heavier one. The darkness does not come from the die: it comes from Lethal health and from whatever you put underneath. That is why a grim cyberpunk game and a comedy of errors use the same card without contradicting each other.

A warning: chaos frustrates

The three cards in the chaotic column only work if the table has agreed that what you are chasing is the story, not success, because much of the time you will not get there. On Black comedy the GM has to invent something on four rolls in ten, and on Nightmare one action in five crashes whatever the sheet says. Nightmare is built for one dirty night or a short scene inside a campaign; a long campaign on it wears people out. Say so before you start and nobody will feel punished.

Where the oppressive row comes from

It is not a theoretical corner of the map: it is the emulation of a family of games that exists and gets played. Look at what Anguish does —9 % at −2, 25 % at 0, 49 % at +2 and 81 % at +4— and compare it with Call of the antediluvian octopuses, where an untrained skill sits around 5–20 %, an ordinary investigator around 25–35 % and a specialist in their field around 70–80 %. It is the same distribution. What is stored in the character sheet there is stored in the card here, which is why you can step in and out of that tone without rewriting a character. The difference is the spread: there the specialist has their corner where they do land things, and here the roll is equally hard for everyone. That is why it works better in short form —one night, one act, one scene— just like those one-night horror games where the candles go out one by one or blocks come out of a tower until it falls, which get to the same place by another road. Say so before you start.

A recipe apart: Tragedy

The quadrant never touches the difficulty, but a recipe may, if it says so clearly and holds for the whole campaign. Tragedy is the only one in the rulebook: the Realist’s sum with the bar set at 14. The same clockwork world, with hope out of reach. Its table is in Appendix D.

The quadrant: where your game falls

The two questions in the questionnaire place your game on a quadrant. The row says which side the universe is on; the column says at what price. Do not think in closed boxes: think of a map where your game lands somewhere. If it lands near an edge it is a hybrid, and you can keep one cell’s reading and borrow only part of its neighbour.

The two gaps are real. There is no optimistic or oppressive card in the habit column, and that is not an oversight: with a flat die, the only way to tilt the tone would be to move the difficulty, and moving the difficulty is exactly the friction this game promises to take away. If you find the rule that fills that gap without touching the TN, you have written a new card. Write it in the margin: that is what the margin is for.

Design note. Once you print this you will see that redundancy is your friend. You do not have to remember that “on the sum, success is 11”: you look at the Realist and there it is. Switch to Anguish and your eyes go to that card and see that success is 6. Zero memory. A hundred per cent narrative.

In the app: every card can bring its own music. You drag your own audio files in, assign one to each card, and there it stays, with a speaker on every square of the Quadrant so it plays on a loop. We upload nothing and hand you no list: the music at your table is yours to choose, your files stay on your machine and nobody redistributes them. What the app does do is remember which one goes with which.

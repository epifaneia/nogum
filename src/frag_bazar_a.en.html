8.3 The Multiverse Bazaar

This appendix is reference material, not study material. It is NOGUM's gear porn: a shop window of weapons, armour, powers and gadgets from ten settings, plus a social arsenal. Nobody has to memorise it. Open it when you need a weapon for an NPC, when a player asks “so what does this do?” or when you want to hear how your genre sounds in numbers.

The stat blocks are teaching crutches: they convey tone better than theory does. A Energy Sword carrying Soak: None and a Plasma Rifle carrying Soak: Max 1 explains space opera faster than three pages of essay. A weapon is not defined by its price, but by how it bends the rules: can it be dodged? Is armour any use against it? Can it reach someone higher up the Grid?

The sweep is done: there is no registered trademark left in the catalogue. The names you see are descriptive on purpose —the blade nobody can lift, the cloud of the pure of heart, the slow-time implant— and the proof that they work is that you know exactly which object I mean.

How to read a stat block

Each item uses explicit tags to describe what it does with damage, dodging, soak and the Grid. There are no named “types”. Summary of Chapter 5 (5.3):

Dmg 0–3
Points the blow crosses off if it lands clean: 1 = light (“A&E”), 2 = serious (“ICU”), 3 = massive (“pushing up daisies”). Specials: Dmg: Massive, Dmg: Absolute, Dmg: Infinite, Mental Dmg N and Social Dmg N (under ED-11, social damage goes to the Mental track unless the campaign creates a track of its own). If the attacker rolls a critical, +1 to damage.
Dodge
What the dodge reaction is allowed to do. Full: the reduction can bring damage down to 0. Partial (area, bursts, fire): even if you mitigate, you take at least 1 point. None: it cannot be dodged. Hard (−2) and Easy (+2) modify the dodge roll (ED-10).
Soak
What armour is allowed to do. Full: it can cancel all the damage. Max N: it reduces at most N points. None: armour is useless. Specials: Mind, Will and Reputation, resisted with the mental or social track or skill.
Grid
Relationship with the Power Grid. Standard: it does not affect higher levels; this is the default when the stat block says nothing. Key LN: it can harm up to level N. Level N: the item operates at that level (vehicles, power armour; if you pilot it, Synchronisation). Ignores all: only the impossible bladeine.
Access N · N Keys
What it costs you to have it. Access is the level of Resources (Social → Hierarchy → Resources) you need for that object to be within your reach: 0 everyday, 1 serious weapon, 2 military hardware, 3 what should not exist. And if the object breaks the Grid, it also costs Keys from your wallet (4.3); the ones in this catalogue are worked out for a level 1 bearer. The Access values in this catalogue were derived from damage and rarity in a single pass: the Director can move any of them one step without breaking anything.

The power tables carry no Access. The Force, ki, blood disciplines, superpowers, toon powers and the social arsenal are not bought with money: they describe what you already are, according to your Grid and your sheet. That is why in those tables the last column only talks about levels and effects. And an object the fiction hands you as scenery —the family car, the village horse— is scenery: it only costs Keys the day you buy it as the levelled weapon it is.

Procedure reminder (5.2): the attacker rolls against the TN of the active card. If the attack connects, the defender makes one reaction (dodge or soak, whichever the weapon allows) and the two result levels are compared: if the defender matches or beats the attacker, there is no damage; if they succeed but stay below, −1; if they fail, full damage. Always within the limits of the tag. The “Grid / Effect” column also holds usage tags: Permission (makes the impossible possible), Fractal (heals or affects a track), Effect, Immunity and Key: creature (a narrative Key against one kind of being). A “—” means the entry does not apply or the source does not give it.

Editorial decisions in this appendix

  • ED-B1 The tags dropped in T68 (Type A/B/C, Dirty/Lethal/Absolute, [FAST], [LETHAL], [AREA], [INEVITABLE]) are converted to the explicit format: Type A → Dodge: Full Soak: Full; Type B → Soak: Max 1; Type C / “Inevitable” → Dodge: None Soak: None. “Turn Dirty damage into Aggravated” becomes “the damage becomes Soak: None”.
  • ED-B2 “[+1 Die]” does not exist in the canon: NOGUM never adds dice. It is converted to “the roll uses Best of 2d10 (High Optimism)” for whatever action or period the item specifies. The other [Engine] tags are converted the same way: “Engine: Best of 2d10” is that same reading; “Engine: High Chaos” means using the Black comedy card; “Engine: Extra Action” is rewritten as an effect (one more action per turn).
  • ED-B3 No item kills by the rules (5.5). Where the source said “if you die”, “kills” or “dies”, read “if the character leaves the story” or “is out of the fight”. Death only happens if it is agreed narratively.
  • Duplicates and variants: the most recent approved version is kept, and the other is noted under “Notes”. Merged items only count once, so some settings drop below 30.
  • Values outside the legend ([Dodge: Surprise], [Dodge: Full (Slow)], [Soak: None (Metal)], [Soak: None in gaps], [Dmg: Fall], [Return Dmg], [Key: creature]…) are kept as they are, and the “Notes” column explains how to read them.
  • The idea bank from the first catalogue (T67) is not included, because its tag format was replaced in T68.

The workshop: how to build what is not on the list

No list of objects is ever enough, least of all in a universal rulebook. So the factory comes before the catalogue: with these four pages you can price anything your table dreams up, and the catalogue becomes what it really is, a table of equivalences where you look up what something similar costs and extrapolate.

Three currencies, three different things

This is the only thing you have to understand. An object can ask you for up to three payments, and each one comes from a different place: ED-36

CurrencyPays forComes fromExample
Resources
Access		Having it. Everything the object is: its damage, whether it can be dodged, whether armour helps, its reachYour Resources level and the fictionThe sword, the rifle, the car
Points
skill points		Making you better. Any bonus to a roll: defending better, hitting betterThe sheet’s 12 or 14 pointsThe chainmail, the master-forged sword
KeysLetting you do the impossible. Permissions and Grid levelsThe separate pot and the walletThe X-ray goggles, the powered armour

There is nothing extraordinary about chainmail in its own world: there is no permission to ask for. It has a price in money and it makes you defend better, so it is paid with Resources and points, and it does not touch Keys. The railgun, on the other hand, lets you punch a hole in a tank: no skill does that, and that is why it costs Keys.

The sum

  1. Describe the object in one sentence, as if you were telling the table about it.
  2. Tag it: Dmg N, Dodge, Soak, Grid. They are free: they describe what the object is, and they are paid for with Access.
  3. Count the bonuses. Every +1 to a roll costs 1 point, exactly as in the pyramid. An object that gives +2 costs 2 points.
  4. Count the permissions. Everything no skill can do —flying, seeing through walls, breathing where you cannot— costs 1 Key.
  5. Count the Grid. If the object makes you operate above your level, it costs x² Keys for every Discipline it raises, where x is the number of levels it lifts you (for a level 1 character: level 2 = 1, level 3 = 4). If it makes you inherit the level across a whole sphere while you use it —vehicles, exoskeletons, powered armour: Synchronisation (2.4)—, it is not a Key but Grid: 5·x² Keys, nearly always Limited, because it only works while you have it (level 2 = 3, level 3 = 10). And never more than the whole sphere’s Grid: if it raises five Disciplines or more, pay the pot’s 5·x² and take all eight. It is the exchange unit of 2.3. Weapons come cheap in Keys; the world manages them, through Access and permits (4.3).
  6. Adjust whatever the sum failed to say, and you are done.

The two tests that prevent 90 % of the mistakes

  • If a skill already does it, it is not a power: it is a bonus. “Sensing when you are being lied to” is not a permission, it is Empathy; “shrugging off pain” is Fortitude; “running fast” is Athletics. Buying it as a power means paying twice for something the pyramid already sells.
  • If it makes you beat things a level above you, it is not a permission: it is Grid. Seeing through a wall is a 1-Key permission. Being undetectable to a whole building full of guards is a Grid level against detection, and it is paid for squared.

The Adjustment: what the sum cannot say

Some powers refuse to be itemised. The sum says 1 Key and at the table they solve the whole session; or the sum says 9 and it turns out the gadget only works while you are wearing it and can be stolen. That is what the Adjustment is for: you look at what the power does that the sum has not paid for, and you multiply. ED-37

TagWhat it meansPoints and Keys
LimitedWorth less than the sum says because it comes with strings: only while worn, once per scene, only in daylight, it can be taken from you÷2
CompleteThe sum is the whole truth×1
EnhancedIt does what it says and also something the sum does not cover×2
UnleashedThe sum falls far short: no conditions, and not even the fiction takes it away×3

The Adjustment multiplies points and Keys, never Access. Upwards it rounds up: the sum for an Enhanced or Unleashed power never comes out short.

The free rung: one scene

When Limited takes the price below 1 —the sum said one point, or one Key— it is not rounded up to 1: it is free. But free carries one mandatory strap, and it is the shortest one there is: it lasts a single scene. The grenade, the brew, the single-use patch, the gadget that burns out as you use it.

That is why the invisibility cloak below still costs 1 Key instead of coming free: you can take it off and put it back on all campaign long. If you want it to last longer than one scene, the minimum is 1 again.

The Adjustment only pays for what the sum has not paid already

This is the mistake you are going to make, so here it is in bold. If you have already itemised the power —given it its Grid level, its permissions and its bonuses—, do not charge for it again with the tag. The Adjustment lives exactly where the system does not reach, and it withdraws the moment the system reaches.

So that two different tables do not price the same thing differently, one calibration question: how many scenes does this solve that you have not already paid for? None, and with strings attached: Limited. What the sum says: Complete. A whole scene of its own: Enhanced. It changes how the campaign is played: Unleashed.

It is agreed when bought and written next to the power. If play proves it was mispriced, re-tag it and adjust: a subjective thing that multiplies by three needs a way out. And if something asks for more than ×3, it is not a power: it is a Grid level you have not spotted, or it is scenery.

Invisibility is the perfect example, because it is one word naming three different things depending on the table.

The four categories

Everything you can build fits in four drawers, and each one is made of different parts. (Whatever fits in none of them —a rope, a map, a first-aid kit— is a tool: it costs Access and nothing else.)

What you can afford: the six drawers of Resources

An object’s Access is the level of Resources you need in order to have it (Social → Hierarchy → Resources). Your bonus in that Discipline says which drawer you live in:

The Resources pull

“With effort” means once per arc, and it is never free: you get hold of something one step above your drawer and the table writes down the price, which is not money. A favour that will be called in, a debt, a trail, somebody who now knows your name. It is the same menu as the fumble’s (5.2), and it turns “I want a helicopter” into a plot instead of an argument.

And remember what you already know: anything stolen, borrowed or obtained in the fiction works free for a whole scene. Access says what you can have, not what you can use tonight.

Warning: equipment eats into your points

If your campaign is about powers —superheroes, mechs, wizards with artefacts, chrome to the eyeballs—, the twelve points evaporate into the equipment list and the characters turn up knowing how to do very little. The tool is obvious: raise the budget. Sixteen, eighteen, twenty. The rulebook is not going to tell you how many, because it depends on how much hardware you want on the table.

What it will tell you is the price of raising it, and it is worth knowing beforehand: the more points you hand out, the less specialisation shows. The Anchor cuts off at +4, so the only thing that grows is breadth. With twelve there are two Grand Masters and a hole that hurts; with twenty, everybody can do everything and nobody is the one who can do something. Neither is wrong: the first makes teams of specialists who need each other, the second makes complete comic-book heroes. Choose knowingly.

Chapter 6 · The Opposition (Building sheets)

In film, the lead is not lit the same way as the waiter walking past in the background. In NOGUM, the hero's sheet should not take as long to make as the sheet for the guard who dies in minute 5. To keep the pace up, the system uses three levels of resolution for its actors.

6.2 Scenery sheets and the Style Rule

The Director declares what is scenery, and it is settled with the Style Rule, like anything below your level. Whatever sits far above you —the gods of 6.1— is scenery only as long as it is not coming for you: you do not roll against it, you go around it, you run, or you look for a Key. If it comes for you, it already has a sheet. T176 T183

6.3 Quick sheets: how and when to use them

In the app: the fifth form, The cast, builds these two sheets —scenery and quick— in three taps, and tells you along the way whether the Style Rule (6.2) applies against that NPC, whether it is a real fight, or whether there is nothing to roll at all. Not the nemesis: that one is made on a sheet, with the same rules as a player character.

The Game Master should never use a full sheet for an NPC who is not crucial. To improvise, use the quick sheet (the card), which takes ten seconds to make by answering two questions:

  1. What level is it? (Power Grid 1–5). This decides whether the PCs can use the Style Rule against it or have to fight for real. Careful: the number says nothing on its own. You read it next to yours. A Grid 2 can be the chrome psycho who wrecks you tonight or, three arcs later, the same monster you no longer pay attention to. Do not raise its Grid when you raise yours: leave it where it is and let it fall behind. That is half the point.
  2. Which Sphere is it good in? One whole Sphere of the five, not a single skill: Physical, Mental, Social, Technical or Arcane. Golden rule: inside that Sphere it rolls at +2; outside it, at +0. A whole Sphere is a lot, and that is on purpose: a quick NPC does not defend itself with nuance, it defends itself with one clear idea —the muscle, the one who knows books, the one who talks you round, the one who fixes machines, the one who does magic—. If you need it good in two, that is a full sheet. The +0 outside is a GM simplification for quick sheets: it saves the sums. An NPC with a full sheet follows the Clumsy and Null rules like anyone else (4.2). T176

Health: use the fractal, but do not pick its health according to its “real” strength. Ask yourself how long you want the fight to last. By default, three boxes: healthy, wounded and out.

6.4 Full sheets: the system's recommendation

This is the sheet we taught you to build in chapter 3: complex and detailed. Recommendation: the GM should only have one full sheet on the table, the one for the main antagonist. Treating the villain under the same rules as the heroes (Signatures at +4, Style, 27-point fractal health) is what turns the final fight into a memorable event rather than a piñata of hit points. The draft mentioned “luck points” for the villain: they do not exist. The antagonist spends Keys like everybody else, and if you want them to survive the scene, give them Keys and let it show in the fiction. Author’s decision

For everything else, simplify. NOGUM is designed so that the camera focuses on the protagonists; the rest of the world can be slightly out of focus.

Chapter 7 · The Mixing Desk (Storytelling advice)

In NOGUM, the Game Master is not an impartial physics simulator: they are the sound engineer at a mixing desk. Their job is not to apply the rules blindly, but to choose which rules apply in order to achieve the emotion they are after. If the previous chapters were the science, this one is the art. And art is about feelings, not maths: breathe.

The black box

This system is demanding for the GM, and only for the GM. The player does not even need to read the rulebook. T65

The player declares (narrative) → the GM checks the card, the Power Grid and the stat block (hidden mechanics) → the GM narrates the consequence

“You declare the action and roll. I'll tell you what happens.” The GM is explicitly encouraged to interpret everything and speak only in narrative terms. The curious player can peek behind the curtain as far as they like.

It asks a lot of the GM? That is a virtue, not a flaw. Other games are a point-and-shoot camera: anyone can take a decent photo, but they all look alike. NOGUM is an SLR on manual. You can mess it up, sure, but it is the only way to take exactly the photo you have in your head. T72

7.2 Default tone (the safety net)

What if you are not sure? What if your players just want to “play an RPG” without philosophising about narrative? NOGUM recommends a standard setup that works for the vast majority of adventures.

“Classic Adventure” mode (First Gear)

  • Medium optimism. We only pay attention to one die. There is no noticeable curve. It is as simple as it gets.
  • Medium chaos. No curves and no extra rules: the search for tone goes unnoticed. Ideal for “I don't want to stop and think about tone”.
  • Level 2 damage (9 boxes). We want them to take damage, but not to drop from a single blow.

Tip: if you get stuck, put the desk in this mode. It is the autopilot. There is no rush: start exploring whenever you feel like it.

The right not to switch

If you do not like modularity, this rulebook is still your home. Do not switch mid-game if you do not need to. It is perfectly valid to play a whole campaign in Epic, or always adding the dice together. NOGUM helps you find your tone: you set it up once at the start and, if you never touch the knobs, it runs like clockwork. Use it to emulate the genre you love, not the one the rulebook imposes on you. T60

Who is this for? For anyone who finds that their usual system does not give them the tone of their favourite saga, or that some other system has weird dice. Here, every choice is acceptable. T60

7.4 Ludonarrative dissonance (the invisible enemy)

The greatest danger in roleplaying is when what the system says contradicts what the table feels. NOGUM gives you tools to fix it.

Symptom A · Comfortable laughter in horror
Problem: you narrate a horrific scene, but the players crack jokes and roll the dice cheerfully. Diagnosis: the system is too kind and they feel safe. Solution: lower the optimism and announce the switch to the worst of 2d10. When a professional (+2) sees their success rate drop from 91% to 49%, the laughter will stop. Mechanical fear breeds narrative fear. ED-14
Symptom B · Boredom in combat
Problem: they have been slugging it out with the boss for an hour, have only knocked off half its health, and are looking at their phones. Diagnosis: you used the War zoom (27 points) when you did not need to, or chaos is too low and the same thing keeps happening. Solution: raise the chaos and the lethality. Switch to a smaller zoom so that the next blow, whoever lands it, ends the fight.
Symptom C · The table is tired (the human factor)
Problem: you have a dense, serious political investigation ready (low chaos), but it is Friday night, it has been a hard week and all they want is beer and explosions. Diagnosis: the tone of the universe clashes with the mood at the table. Golden rule: the table rules. Do not force drama if they are asking for comedy. Turn the dials: make the investigation a car chase (raise the chaos) and the debate a bar brawl. NOGUM lets you save the session by adapting the game's physics to your friends' energy.

ED-14 The draft said “from 80% to 30%”. The exact figures for a +2 against TN 6 are 91% with the best of 2d10 and 49% with the worst (Appendix D).

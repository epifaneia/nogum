Chapter 3 · The Protagonists (Character creation)

At NOGUM we love finding unexpected ways out. The rulebooks we have used as references pick a side: either very generic, narrative skills, or very precise skills that turn choosing them into an ordeal. We want, or at least that is the intention, to keep the best of both worlds.

That is why we have designed four levels of “zoom”: skills go from more open to more specific. NOGUM’s thesis is that a general skill covers the basics: knowing what the thing is about. Take History. Someone who knows history in general probably knows the date of American independence, but to master the details of Korea’s Three Kingdoms period (and, within it, the rise and fall of the Gaya confederacy) we are talking about someone specialised: in that place, in that period, or in both. In the rules, this means the bonus of a general skill is capped. To shine, you must specialise. So the system is a pyramid: you buy the general skill as a base so you can specialise in the next step up.

There are no attributes

In NOGUM there is no Strength, Dexterity, Reflexes or Intelligence. The broad fields of the pyramid are big enough to replace them. T47–T48 Everything on the sheet goes straight to bonuses, from −2 to +4.

3.1 The pyramid (the four zoom levels)

Think of a satellite map: the Sphere is the continent, the Domain the city, the Discipline the street and the Signature the house number. The closer you zoom in, the more power you have and the narrower your view.

Null and the cascading safety net

The Null and Clumsy Rule

If you have not spent a single point in a Sphere, you are Null in it: you do not roll at +0, you roll at −2.

As soon as you buy anything within that sphere, Null disappears —but not entirely—. Buying anything means buying at least one Domain, so your column, the Domain you have touched, works normally: you roll with whatever you have in it, +1 at the very least. Whatever falls in the sphere’s other column you roll at −1: you are clumsy. T106 T131 T170

Clumsy is not Null. It is better than Null: the sphere is not foreign to you, you know about nearby things and can at least tell what would be a silly move. But it is worse than having the column, and for a specific reason: you have the sphere, but with the wrong half of it. You are pure Vigour and you are asked to tumble. You are pure Erudition and you are asked to read a street. You are not short of muscle or of brains: you are short of footing.

And that is why the sheet matters. Five Spheres of two Domains each make ten columns (eight without Arcane), and covering them all eats almost the whole sheet. You are going to be clumsy at something, and that is the decision. A broad character is almost never clumsy and almost never shines; a deep one shines at their own thing and drags everywhere else. Both sheets are legal and the game rewards neither: it rewards the table knowing which one each player made, and splitting the work accordingly.

For each action you use the highest applicable level (bonuses do not stack across levels). Example: defusing a nuclear bomb.

  1. Do you have the Explosives Signature? You roll at +4.
  2. If not, do you have the Security Discipline? You roll at +2.
  3. If not, do you have the Operations Domain? You roll at +1.
  4. If not, have you bought anything in the Technical sphere (the other Domain, Artifice)? You roll at −1: you are clumsy.
  5. If your Technical sphere is empty, you roll at −2: you will probably cut the red wire because it is prettier.

If a broad skill on your sheet is close enough to what you are attempting, you negotiate it with the GM on an equal footing. T82

Knowing is not doing

3.3 Signatures

  • They are broad, like the skills of other games: Firearms rather than Sniper; Swordmaster rather than a specific model of sword. T49, T98, T100
  • They hang logically from a Discipline. Examples: Weapons → Historical Fencing; Systems → Cryptography; Biotech → Neurosurgery; Sciences → Chemistry; Acrobatics → Parkour (which only works for moving around the city, not for swimming).
  • Golden rule: to be a Grand Master (+4) in something, you must be able to explain exactly what you are doing.
  • A catalogue of two hundred Signatures in section 8.2, ordered by Discipline: five for each, which is how they get looked up at the table.

The accordion: mastery folds upwards

If you are a Grand Master with the pistol, the rifle and the sword, you are no longer a specialist in three things: you are a Grand Master of Weapons. And if you are one in three Disciplines of Vigour, you are a Grand Master of the whole of Vigour. Mastery does not pile up: it folds. ED-38

When you have…It folds intoAnd then you roll
3 Signatures in the same DisciplineThe whole Discipline+4 at anything in that Discipline, Signature or not

It is the other way to grow, and the only one left to points once you have your +4: the Anchor still does not move —nobody ever goes past +4— but the +4 keeps widening until it covers everything. A character who has folded the Physical sphere is no stronger than one with a single Signature: it is just that there is no longer anything physical they cannot do.

And it is genuinely expensive, which is what makes it a campaign goal rather than a creation trick:

With twelve points at creation, the most that fits is one folded Discipline and little else, so nobody arrives at the table as the best in the world at a complete field. Folding a Sphere is, literally, the end of a long campaign: that is where points go when there is nothing left to learn.

If three feels like too many or too few at your table, it is the only number this accordion has: raise it to four to make total mastery nearly unreachable, or drop it to two for campaigns of competent heroes from the start.

3.4 Style, magic and aspects

Style (+2)
Every full sheet has a Style section outside the pyramid, with a fixed value of +2. It is a gift from the system and a NOGUM signature. It is used with the Style Rule (4.5). T113
Magic is just another skill
Throwing fire is bought just like wielding a sword (Arcane → Gift → Projection). If the magic in your universe has requirements (mana, components, faith), the field is paid at the normal price and the requirement is noted as a flaw of that field. T54
Positive and negative aspects (gifts and flaws)
Permanent traits that are not skills: night vision, an implant, a phobia… This is also where the [Slow] tag goes for skills that are not quick in combat in your universe (see 5.1). T87 Gifts and flaws have no point cost, because they are not bought: a flaw is the scar of a filled block (5.5, ED-35). And the [Slow] tag now has a rule: a skill marked that way cannot be used to roll initiative. Either you enter with a different stance, or you go last. Author’s decision

3.5 The economy of scarcity

NOGUM hands out 12 skill points, or 14 if the universe has an Arcane sphere. The number is not a whim: without magic there are four Spheres, eight Domains and thirty-two Disciplines — forty slots; with magic there are fifty. Twelve out of forty and fourteen out of fifty are the same proportion, so the sheet feels equally scarce wherever you play. ED-31

Twelve points force you to manage scarcity: if you want to stand out at something, they practically force you to be Null somewhere. More points open the door to sheets with no weaknesses, which are usually less interesting. But playing NOGUM is a gentlemen’s agreement: this rulebook will not devote a single line to building firewalls against foul play. The kind of characters the story calls for is agreed at the table, like adults. That said, hand out as many points as you like: the rulebook is there to fit your campaign. Draft, T107

More starting points, knowingly. A table may raise the starting points, and the app has the option. But make it a decision and not an oversight, knowing it goes against the idea of this rulebook: more points give stronger characters with fewer reasons to go looking for Keys, and looking for them is a good part of the game. NOGUM allows it. T170

The separate pot

On top of the points, every character gets a pot that does not mix: either +1 Grid level —in any Sphere whose ceiling, in your universe, reaches 2 or more (2.4)—, or 5 own Keys. There is no exchange between the two pots in either direction: points do not buy Keys and Keys do not buy points.

The reason is a table reason, not a mathematical one. If they could be exchanged, you would get the Grid hero with no trade and no background: someone enormous who cannot do anything, the most boring character anyone can bring to a game. By forcing all twelve points into skills, everybody turns up with a trade underneath the power.

And the choice is not neutral. One Grid level covers a whole Sphere: its eight Disciplines, eight slots for five coins. A Key covers one Discipline for one coin. Breadth is cheap, but the universe sets its ceiling for you; freedom is expensive, but you choose it and it points at what matters to you. Freedom costs 60 % more, and that ratio, five eighths, holds all the way up the ladder (2.3). Torn between the two? Take the test in 2.4: does it beat anything in that sphere, or just one thing?

And starting at 3? That is two levels above: twenty Keys, four pots. The count says what is fair: if one character starts at 3, the others get four pots each, in Keys or in Grid. The cleanest, nearly always, is for the table to raise its starting level (2.4) and everyone jumps from there. T173

Twelve points force you to choose between being a competent generalist or a flawed specialist.

Case B · The survivor (generalist)

Wants to be ready for anything.

  • Raises nine Disciplines to +2 (9 points), all hanging from the Domains already paid for: Fortitude and Brawling (Vigour); Alertness, Survival and Investigation (Wits); Persuasion (Influence); Mechanics and Biotech (Artifice); and Projection (Gift).
  • Total: 5 + 9 = 14 points.

To shake off the −1 as well they would have to buy the five columns they are missing: all ten are ten points out of fourteen, and then only four would be left for depth. That is the third possible sheet, the fully broad one, and it is the one that fails least and shines least. T170

The two tolls, and the card decides what they cost

There are two tolls, and they are worth keeping apart.

The expensive one, the −2. To avoid being Null anywhere, one Domain in each Sphere is enough: four points in a universe without magic, five with it. And a Grand Master hanging from a Domain already paid for only adds three (Discipline 1 + Signature 2). Without magic, your back covered and two Grand Masters come to 4 + 3 + 3 = 10 points out of twelve, with two to spare; the third would need 13, so it only appears if you give up a whole Sphere and sit at −2 there (3 + 3 + 3 + 3 = 12). With Arcane the sum is exact: 5 + 3 + 3 + 3 = 14, three masteries without a single Null, and not one point more. The system charges the toll by itself, with nothing to forbid: the price of Signatures is being Null, or spending down to the last point. T170

The cheap one, the −1. Shaking off Clumsy is another matter: it is eight columns without Arcane and ten with it, one per Domain, and covering them all costs eight points out of twelve, or ten out of fourteen. Hardly anybody does it, because it leaves four for depth: one Grand Master and little else. So almost every sheet is clumsy somewhere. That is not a flaw in the sheet: it is its shape.

What that toll costs you at the table is not decided by the sheet. It is decided by the card:

CardWhat your −2 still pulls off
Epic · Unleashed Pulp51 %

In an epic campaign, leaving a Sphere empty barely hurts: you still pull off half of what you attempt with it. In Anguish it is a death sentence. The very same three-mastery character is a bargain in one place and suicide in another, and that is decided by the tone, not by a creation rule.

The system works. You decide where to put the focus.

What points cannot buy

The two currencies buy skill and permission, and the sheet is a closed list. The world is not. A camera drone that feeds the image to your goggles, a cloak that hides you, a flight pack: none of the three is a skill, and none of them is on the list.

The easy way out would be a thousand rules: a section for drones, another for cloaks, a table of flying devices. NOGUM does the opposite. Everything is bought with the same two coins, and whatever the coins cannot say is said by a tag. ED-37

The sum is four steps. Every +1 to a roll costs 1 point, just as on the pyramid. Everything no skill can do —fly, see through, breathe where you cannot— is a permission: 1 Key. If it also operates above your level, that is Grid: x² Keys for every Discipline it raises, where x is the levels it lifts you (2.3). And then you adjust whatever the sum has failed to say, multiplying by ÷2 · ×1 · ×2 · ×3 according to whether it comes with strings, is fairly priced, does something extra, or leaves the sum far short.

The drone

It carries a camera and feeds the image to your goggles: +2 to whatever you look at through it. The sum is immediate: two bonuses, two points, and not one Key, because seeing is not a permission —there is already a Discipline for that—.

But the drone can be seen. It can be shot down. It is one more body in the scene and it is yours: the day somebody downs it, you lose the +2 and you have made noise as well. None of that is in the sum, and all of it works against you. Limited: one point.

Notice what was not needed. No drone rules, no range, no battery, no table of flying devices. The drawback is charged by the fiction, which is what knows how to charge it; the system only had to decide that it is worth half.

And the same the other way. Flight is a one-Key permission, and in a rooftop campaign it does not solve a scene: it solves a third of the campaign —the chases, the heights, the escapes—. The sum has not paid for that. Unleashed: three Keys. The tag is not a power dial: it is the corrector for what the itemising failed to charge, and it withdraws by itself the moment the sum learns to describe the power.

And below one, free. If Limited takes the price below 1, it is not rounded up: it comes free, in exchange for the shortest strap there is, which is that it lasts a single scene. The grenade, the brew, the single-use patch. If you want it to last longer, the minimum is 1 again.

So how much of this can you pay on day one? All of it. The Adjustment is not a luxury of late campaigns, because it multiplies the same twelve points and the same side pot: a +3 Unleashed power is nine points out of twelve, and you have three left. It is a legal sheet and it is a hard sheet —you have bought something enormous and you have almost no trade underneath—, and that is how the system charges instead of forbidding. It is the same sentence as the rest of this section: the price of Signatures is being Null somewhere, and the price of the impossible is having no trade.

The full procedure —the two tests that avoid 90 % of mistakes, the calibration question and the four categories of what can be built— is in the workshop of the Bazaar of the Multiverse. Here it is enough to know that it exists, because that is where you spend the points that do not fit on the pyramid.

3.6 Progression: the two wallets

Almost every game throws three completely different things into the same bag of points, and it shows: the level fifteen fighter is at once more skilled, more powerful in the world and more scarred, because there is only one counter. Here they are separated, and each one has a different owner.

What changesWho owns itHow it moves
The world · the GridThe DirectorThe plot opens it: it rises when the facts justify it, agreed at the table and against the universe’s ceiling. Every jump has its price in Keys
The scars · gifts and flawsThe fictionNot bought: they happen in play
Competence · points and KeysThe tableTwo wallets, at a pace agreed in session zero

Two bags that grow at different rates

Experience is not one counter: it is two independent wallets. ED-33

  • Skill points. They buy pyramid: Domains, Disciplines and Signatures, at the same prices as at creation.
  • Keys. They buy own Keys, climb the square ladder against the same thing, and pay for equipment that breaks the Grid (4.3). And, with the plot’s permission, Grid jumps.

The permission belongs to the story; the price, to the rule

“The Grid is not bought” means it is not bought without the plot’s permission: a jump cannot break the campaign’s tone or levels. It does not mean it has no price. Every jump is worth an exact number of Keys (2.3), and that gives the table a tool for fairness in both directions:

  • The story raises the Grid and you do not want to rise. You collect the jump in Keys: five for staying one level below the table, and fifteen more if the table rises again and you stay where you were, up to the twenty that two levels are worth. You can be small and very specific, on purpose.
  • You want to rise sooner, or where the others do not. You save Keys and pay for the jump, at the same price, as soon as the story gives permission: a milestone, a time skip, a table agreement.

The normal thing is still to agree on it. The count is there for when it needs to be fair. T173

It is the same question asked twice, and it is worth asking twice, because the two taps do not do the same thing:

The Key wallet is the campaign’s power dial

Skill points only make you wider: the Anchor cuts them off at +4, so however many you hand out, nobody gets taller — only good at more things. Keys, on the other hand, open up equipment and break Grid ceilings, and that never saturates: there is always another Discipline, another scene, another object to buy.

That is why the default pace for Keys is slow: one per session, and it is worth being stingy with them, because many are spent on equipment. If you want the campaign to escalate, open the Key tap. If what you want is for the characters to know how to do more things, open the points tap. They are not interchangeable and should not be opened together without thinking about it.

The same question, again, for Keys. By default: one per session, and if the campaign is about equipment —cyberpunk, looting, gadget superheroes— keep an eye on it, because there the Key wallet is the shop.

Question 2 · Who grows?

Only if the previous answer is not “still”: everyone equally, or whoever earns it in the story. And a warning that comes from many tables: handing out purely on merit makes the strong stronger and makes the table argue about who deserved it.

The healthy shape is for merit to add on top of a base, not to replace it. Everyone gets what the pace says, and whoever had a big moment takes +1 extra. That way the split never punishes the shy player.

What gets rewarded

NOGUM is a narrative rulebook and it rewards narrative. This is the short list:

  1. Resolving the arc’s central conflict.
  2. Playing a flaw against your own interest when it was inconvenient.
  3. Finding a Key, or giving one to someone else.
  4. Paying a heavy cost for the group.
  5. Closing a thread of the character’s personal story.
  6. Failing memorably and owning the consequences.
  7. Surviving a night on a harder card than the campaign’s.

The sixth has an illustrious parent: there are games that give you experience precisely when you fail, and it is an excellent idea. Others reward using a skill until you master it, or winning fights. Those are legitimate approaches and this rulebook does not carry them because it measures something else; if your table wants them, turn them into one more entry on this list and be done with it.

Experience cannot break the maths

No roll goes past +4, so experience makes nobody taller: only wider. At +1 per session, twenty sessions in, a character has tripled their sheet and still does not go past +4. Growing means being good at more things, not being a god at one.

And there a circuit closes with the Grid: the characters widen, the Director raises the world, and the challenge comes back on its own without anybody touching a table. The warning that comes with it: in a fast-growth campaign the characters will live at +2 and +4 in their own field, and several cards lose the clean failure up there. The answer is not to cut points, it is to move the Grid. If the world does not rise, the game turns into a stroll.

Where points go when there is nothing left to learn

The Anchor cuts off at +4 and that is not negotiable, so past a certain point skill points only buy breadth. What gives them a goal is the accordion (3.3): three Signatures fold the whole Discipline, three Disciplines fold the Domain, both Domains fold the Sphere. Eight points, twenty-two and forty-four.

That way each wallet has its own long ladder and they look nothing alike: Keys climb outwards —every rung multiplies your reach against one specific thing by a hundred— and points climb inwards, widening the +4 until there is nothing in that field you cannot do. One makes you touch more world; the other makes you never fail in your own.

Gifts and flaws: not bought, they happen

Advantages and disadvantages are in no pot. They appear in play, and they have a concrete mechanical origin: a flaw is the scar of a filled block. When a block of your Mental or Spiritual bar fills up and you later heal, the −2 goes away but something named stays behind: the insomnia, the debt, the tic, the fear of water. The gift works the same way from the other side, when the fiction owes you one. The details, with their thresholds and examples, are in 5.5. ED-35

The author’s thesis: fast growth, a bonus for good narrative play and fixed experience at campaign milestones. Milestones work as incentives; experience is there to manage the campaign’s expectations. T37

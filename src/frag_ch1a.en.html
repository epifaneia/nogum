Chapter 1 · The Engine (The physics of emotion)

Now we get down to business. This chapter gives shape to the concept from the introduction: how dice statistics influence the emotion of the roll, and how that mood when the dice are thrown influences the feel of the whole table.

In the real world, gravity is a constant: 9.8 m/s². If you drop an apple, it falls. Always. In fiction, gravity is an opinion. If an action hero jumps off a building, he lands on an awning and survives. If a supporting character in a horror film does the same, he breaks his neck.

The physics hasn't changed: the universe's intention has. NOGUM does not simulate the physics of objects; it simulates the physics of the script. To that end, before you touch a single die, I am handing you control of the two invisible engines that govern that reality.

1.1 The two axes of reality

Imagine you have a mixing desk in front of you with two main faders. By moving them, you change how it “feels” to exist in your world.

Y axis · Optimism (the Will)

It answers the question: how much does the universe want the protagonists to triumph?

  • High optimism (The Chosen One). The universe is your accomplice. Coincidences are happy ones: the guard looks the other way just as you pass, enemy shots only graze shoulder pads. Example: The one with the hat and the whip leaping onto a moving lorry. He knows he won't miss the jump; the only tension is seeing how great it looks.
  • Neutral optimism (Indifference). The universe is deaf and mute. It doesn't hate you, but it won't save you. Things are fair. Example: real life, or a serious historical novel.
  • Low optimism (Hostility). The universe is an active enemy. Murphy's law rules: if something can go wrong, it will go worse. The floor creaks just as you try to be stealthy. Example: the last survivor on the ship trying to escape; everything she touches breaks, jams or betrays her.

X axis · Chaos (Predictability)

It answers the question: how stable is the relationship between cause and effect?

  • Low chaos (Order / Predictable). Reality is a Swiss watch. If you are good at something, you do it well almost every time. There are no absurd surprises and consequences are logical. Example: The consultant from Baker Street. Everything has a deductive explanation; if you shoot, the bullet flies straight.
  • Medium chaos (Standard). There is room for plot twists and surprise, but within a coherent frame.
  • High chaos (the unpredictable). Reality is liquid and the improbable happens constantly. A shot can ricochet three times and hit you in the foot, or you can hit an impossible target with your eyes closed. Example: el mercenario bocazas or Alice in Wonderland: physical logic bows to the joke or the madness of the moment.

Pessimism is not chaos

Things going wrong and things going weird look alike, but they are not the same. The “why does the plan fail?” test T60:

  • Low optimism: the heist fails because the enemy is smart, competent and cruel (one guard too many, a state-of-the-art alarm). Feeling: oppression. Things go consistently wrong.
  • High chaos: the heist fails because of something absurd (a cat jumps on the panel, a sneeze, an expired explosive that goes “pff”). Feeling: loss of control. Things go unexpectedly weird.

And real life is neutral optimism with low chaos: fair and predictable, not chaotic. T4

The two gaps are real. In the habit column the die is flat, and on a flat die the only way to tilt the tone would be to move the difficulty: exactly the friction this game promises to take away (the why is in 1.3). If your game lands there, take a neighbour: the predictable one if you want the world to behave, the chaotic one if you want it to charge you. And if you find the rule that fills the gap without touching the difficulty, you have written a new card.

All seven have their rules, tables and percentages in 1.4. There are no numbers here yet: only the place where you want your players to live, or where you want them to live in this particular scene.

The grid is not a fixed menu: tones are potentially infinite and every GM is invited to create their own T3. In section 1.3 we lift the bonnet to look at the mathematical engine that makes this work.

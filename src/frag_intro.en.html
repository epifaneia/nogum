What is roleplaying? (According to game shops)

It looks like an obvious question, but we decided to put it at the start of the manifesto to remove any barrier to entry, and also as a chance to define how we understand this hobby.

Roleplaying is a ludic-narrative activity in which a group of friends gathers around a table to create a story together. There are so many roleplaying rulebooks that almost any list of their defining features will run into an exception somewhere.

Usually the table is clearly split between the players and the Game Master: the person who writes the skeleton of the story and the players who will navigate it. Some games blur that difference.

It is also very common to have character sheets, which describe everything the characters can do, and rules that define when an action succeeds. A degree of chance is added to make events uncertain, so that the narration is not an imposition but a request for permission.

The rules, the dice, the hierarchy with the GM… every piece has a different flavour depending on which parts of the process of narrative creation the game designer finds most interesting. We are no exception. We chose which parts of the narrative matter most to us, and this manual has a very strong personality built around highlighting those elements.

What is roleplaying? (For NOGUM)

NOGUM was written to be a universal rulebook, but it is anything but naive in pursuing that goal. We have designed a manual that fits any narrative universe (from a detective novel in the Hercule Poirot series to a space opera like Krull). But it does not fit every way of roleplaying. Quite the opposite: the manual has a very clear personality stamped on it. Below we describe which points are non-negotiable if you want to enjoy a game with this manual. If any of them clashes head-on with your philosophy of play, it may be time to look for other options that resonate more with you.

The most famous dichotomy is the one separating tactical, simulationist games (old school) from narrative games. This game is clearly narrative. But that definition is a little vague. Let's bring it down to earth:

The non-negotiable pillars

Story over dice
The rules exist to improve the narrative. If they go against the grain, they are ignored. We borrow the maxim: “roll only if every possible result is interesting”. Not positive, not desirable: interesting, at least as a possibility that gives merit and meaning to success T97. Your health is just a number: no player will die because of a bad roll. A protagonist's death must be narrative, decided and agreed with arc-level reasons, and not the product of luck.
Story over fairness
The above is about the rules, but it does not mean they can always be bent in the players' favour. Sometimes it is the exact opposite: a dirty universe hits the players in ways the rule does not cover, and an epic universe makes concessions the rule does not cover either. This game is a gentlemen's agreement. The players accept the ups and downs of the story, and the GM manages them always with everyone's fun in mind, never using their position to make anyone uncomfortable. It takes sportsmanship to accept in advance what you are signing up for, backbone to keep the commitment when the results don't go your way, and maturity to talk it through and steer the experience together. You can negotiate whatever you like to make the game more fun or the narrative more powerful, but never to demand “fairer” rules or more respect for the dice, because that is not what this manual is about. What you can argue is that the way the rules have been ignored is not fun or does not help the story.
Emotion as the pulse of the narrative
When we say everything is subordinate to the narrative, we mean something very specific: emotion. It is fun to point out that the opera of stars game should not have a Colt, but a DH-17 blaster pistol. But what really matters is whether the game feels like an epic, pulpy space opera, whether you can breathe the fun and optimism of the genre at every step. For this manual, the emotion of a genre must show in the way you narrate, in the rules… and in the GM's liberties.
The pace must flow
Roleplaying, as this system understands it, is improvisation. Any quick solution to a problem will be better at the table than a far more nuanced, brainy and balanced one that forces the pace to break. The narrator must know this, allow and encourage quick decisions, and accept that a hasty decision is not always the wisest. We came here to play, not to sit an exam. An idea that is “brilliant for something improvised” must be rewarded in the game as a brilliant idea in that universe. An idea brought ready-made from home, or the result of 30 minutes of debate that interrupt the session, must be treated with all the ruthless rigour the GM can muster. Non-play is discouraged.
Grown-up table, liquid rules, zero combos
Players who are adults (mentally, not on their ID card) and who collaborate with the GM instead of contesting every rule, because everyone's goal is a coherent narrative, not a mathematically fair duel. The rules are liquid: they change according to what the scene asks for, and they are anchored by definition to a statistic that makes rolling dice meaningful. There are no combos or stacking bonuses to exploit by juggling at home. T96–T97

This is a filter of interest, not of skill. Nobody is a worse player for preferring old school: we just point the spotlight here, and you point it there. T41

What is this manual?

This book is not a finished product: it is a question. Or rather, it is the answer to a discomfort that has followed me for years as a designer and storyteller.

Over the last decade we have seen the rise of “universal narrative” systems. Games like The aspect-driven narrative games, The aspect-driven narrative games or the los sistemas de movimientos (The black comedy systems) philosophy promised us the master key: a single ruleset to play any story. And they lied to us. Or at least they told us a half-truth.

These systems are universal in form (you can use them to fire a laser or swing a sword), but they are incredibly rigid in subguard. Without you noticing, they bring their own tone from home.

The default-tone trap

Try playing a purist game of Call of the antediluvian octopuses with The aspect-driven narrative games. You can't. The system is mathematically designed so that characters are competent, proactive and able to overcome fate. However horrible the monsters you describe, the mechanics push towards pulp and adventure.

The opposite example, and the one that gave birth to this project, is The black comedy systems. Its “success at a cost” mechanic is brilliant for generating gritty drama and constant consequences. But try using it for a Silver Age superhero chronicle.

Picture the scene: you are a Sun God, a paragon of virtue and infinite power, stopping a mere bank robber. Silver Age fiction demands perfection: it is a formality, a clean display of superiority. But the system makes you roll, and the statistics say “partial success”. Suddenly the Sun God trips. Or the robber breaks his nose. Or a building collapses by mistake.

If we were playing The Boys, that result would be perfect. But in a classic heroic fantasy, the system had just destroyed the immersion. The rule was working against the narration.

Deconstructing tone

This manual tries to distil the elements that give a system its emotion. It was built to squeeze every one we found and isolate it, so that it only applies in a universe that shares the emotion that rule heightens. The manual offers a complete game (if you want the quick, ready-to-play, somewhat simplified rules, the accelerated version with all the recipes is at the end). But its main reason for existing is to give the GM tools to design their own rules and to start seeing their games from a new angle: tone.

Our first finding was that dice probability imprints very different moods. It is not just a matter of more or fewer successes, but also of how predictable the rolls are. If you have a high chance of success, it seems obvious that your result is predictable; but the nuance between optimism when rolling and certainty is not obvious at all. Let's describe both.

  1. The Optimism axis: does the universe conspire to make you shine or to crush you?
  2. The Chaos axis: do actions have predictable consequences, or does absurd randomness reign?

Picture a fun but heroic universe, like that of the one with the hat and the whip. Indy is never in real danger, but his actions sometimes cause unexpected tangles that take the plot to unforeseen and entertaining places: trouble you know he will get out of, but that adds an interesting twist. That universe calls for rules with high optimism and high chaos.

Now picture Lovecraft's novels: an existential terror that chills the blood. There are no wild twists, tangles or unpredictability here. Only the suffocating atmosphere that constantly reminds you how fragile a human being is among deities that exist beyond time and space. When you hold a book of arcane magic, perhaps you should brace for the unexpected; the rest of the time, your steps will be predictably oppressive. Low chaos and low optimism.

This manual squeezes those two sensations and creates rules that increase the optimism or chaos felt in each setting.

We also explore how lethality completely changes the way characters face challenges, and how to adapt the total amount of health to the tone of the game. And the importance of numerically anchoring the world so that the roll never loses its meaning: NOGUM supports very different power levels with a numerical system that always stays coherent.

Finally, the manual aims to keep things simple and to avoid at all costs letting the rules steal the spotlight from the game. It gives the GM absolute freedom with an agile, intuitive modular scheme that demands minimal cognitive effort to navigate the tables. Not to make the game simple (this manual is not), but so that the GM can focus their efforts on what matters: discerning the tone and working out which set of rules the atmosphere calls for, not how to apply them.

The happy accident

This manual was first conceived as a modular toolkit that would let every GM design their campaigns with the right tools, whatever the tone of their universe. But by designing rule changes to be so simple and fluid, we made a revolutionary accidental discovery. Not only can you adapt the tone for each campaign: you can play an entire session with a different tone from the rest of the campaign (comic relief, a relaxed interlude between dark arcs). And the capacity for change is such that an advanced GM can adjust the tone in every scene… and even give different tones to each character at the same table. T7

These are the questions this manual asks. I found my answers; perhaps yours will be better. But at the very least, we hope we have helped you ask your games questions you had never asked them before.

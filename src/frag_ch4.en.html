Chapter 4 · Dynamics (Resolution rules)

With the axes (the universe) and the characters (the actors) defined, we need a protocol for resolving conflict. This chapter describes how to roll, how to apply bonuses and what they mean. Combat adds a few rules of its own, so it has its own chapter.

The roll

Dice reading (according to the Tone Card) + total bonus ≥ the card’s TN → success

Total bonus = skill (highest applicable level) + difficulty + equipment + effects. Always between −2 and +4.

4.1 Roll difficulty (the complexity filter)

Difficulty does not depend on the character’s skill, but on the intrinsic complexity of the task. The target number (TN) is set by the active Tone Card (6 on single-die or best/worst cards, and 11 on the Realist). The task’s difficulty is expressed as a modifier:

ModifierDifficultyExamples
+2EasyRoutine tasks under stress.
0StandardThe default challenge: shooting someone who is moving, hacking a commercial server.
−2HardRequires professional competence: a headshot, open-heart surgery.
−4HeroicMathematically unlikely for an ordinary human. Requires stacked bonuses.

The success rule and the numerical limits

  • Add up all modifiers: skill, difficulty, equipment and effects.
  • If the total ends up below −2, the GM decides whether to allow the roll (at −2) or narrate an automatic failure. The penalty never goes below −2.
  • If the total ends up above +4, the GM decides whether to allow the roll (at +4) or narrate an automatic success; NOGUM recommends the Style roll. The bonus never exceeds +4.
  • No combos: two effects from the same source do not stack; the best one counts.

ED-5 The TN scale of 4/6/9/12/15 and the +6 ceiling that appeared in an earlier draft are withdrawn. The author’s draft sets difficulty as a modifier and the clamp at [−2, +4].

Your Grand Master is going to hit 99% of the time in Epic? Perfect: that is what the genre asks for. We don’t inflate difficulty to compensate. If the task is mundane, don’t roll. And if the player wants to show off, let them raise the difficulty themselves: one hand behind the back. T21–T22

Fumbles, criticals and partial successes depend on the active Tone Card (1.4).

4.2 Skill: the weight of competence

What what you wrote on your sheet (chapter 3) means mathematically. Figures with the First Gear card (red die against TN 6):

  • Null (−2): the dead weight. With the sphere empty you suffer −2. Your chance of success drops from 50% to 30%. With the lower of 2d10 (Anguish card) it falls to 9%: you are practically a walking corpse. Null turns routine tasks into mortal dangers.
  • Competence (+0 / +1): the citizen. Having something in the sphere, or its Domain, lets you roll without a penalty or with a slight nudge. You are functional.
  • The discipline (+2): the professional. You are reliable. A +2 turns a TN 6 into a virtual TN 4 and drastically reduces the weight of bad luck (70%).
  • The Grand Master (+4): certainty. With +4, on a standard task you only fail on a natural 1, which is the fumble (90% success). If the tone is optimistic (Epic card), you are mathematically infallible at mundane things (99%).

4.3 Equipment: modifiers, keys and effects

In NOGUM, equipment is not just for adding numbers: it is for enabling actions.

  1. Quality modifier (+1 / +2). Only equipment of exceptional make gives numerical bonuses. A standard sniper rifle gives +0; a custom rifle from a master gunsmith gives +1.
  2. Narrative permission (the Key). Normally, a piece of equipment operates at the same Power Grid level as its bearer: a punch affects one person, and so does a shotgun (level 1). They differ in damage and in how it is soaked. But some equipment breaks that barrier: a bazooka can wreck an entire office (level 2).
    • Without equipment: with a punch you cannot attack infrastructure or a medium-sized group of people. Impossible.
    • With equipment: you have a bazooka. Now you can blow up a cabin in the woods and reduce it to ashes. You roll against the standard TN.
    Weapons are described with their spec sheet (5.3).
  3. Effects (the catch-all). Like any good universal rulebook, a piece of equipment can do… other things. Whatever you want. If something is worth writing on the sheet but is neither a key nor a bonus, describe it here: from a cursed weapon that increases your strength but corrupts your soul (it gradually deals you spiritual damage) to a substance that speeds up your reflexes but changes how you perceive danger (euphoria). Effects can describe a mechanic or give roleplaying advice (or impositions). The only limit is your imagination.

The three locks on equipment

Getting something written on your sheet is not only a question of money. It is three permissions, and each object’s spec sheet (8.3) says which ones it asks of you: ED-34

  1. The money, which here is your level in Resources (Social → Hierarchy → Resources). Every object carries its Access N: everyday things ask for 0, a serious weapon 1, military hardware 2, and what should not exist at all 3. If you fall short it is not that it is expensive: it is out of your reach, and getting hold of it is a plot.
  2. Narrative permission: that it makes sense for this to be in your hands here and now.
  3. The permission of pace: if the object breaks the Grid —it carries Grid: Key Nx— it also costs x² Keys from your wallet, the same square ladder as in 2.3. A railgun, which is Key N3, costs nine Keys.

The third lock closes a classic hole. Without it, a catalogue pistol hands out for free what a character pays nine Keys for. With it, the railgun is still brutal and whoever paid for theirs does not feel cheated.

Looted gear works for one scene

A weapon stolen, borrowed or obtained in the fiction can be used for a whole scene without paying anything. You take the railgun off the dead merc, you use it, and when the scene ends you decide: either you spend the Keys and it is yours, or you leave it on the floor.

That turns looting back into a decision instead of an escalation, which is the classic problem of every game with an equipment catalogue. And it fits the distinction between Keys: the equipment Key is the one that can be lost —it lives in the object, it can be taken, spent or broken—; your own Key is the one that is you.

4.4 Temporary and permanent modifiers

Temporary (they last a scene, a few turns or until a condition is met)
Stunned, Inspired, Drugged, Knocked Down… They can be any of the three types above: bonuses (numerical, they modify your roll), keys (narrative, they modify your Grid level) or effects (mechanical or narrative). Always define the condition under which they go away (end of scene, healing, ritual, number of turns…). Any effect that comes from a piece of equipment is temporary, because it disappears if you are not carrying it.
Permanent (they last an arc or a campaign)
These are changes to the sheet: scars, traumas, corruption, prostheses. They need no condition to be there: they simply are. They can only be removed or modified with experience points (or, in the case of lasting injuries, with another significant arc; see 5.6).

4.5 The Grid and the Style Rule

This rule overrides everything above. Before calculating anything, the Game Master checks the Grid (chapter 2). For teaching purposes it comes at the end of the chapter, but at the table it is the first filter.

A · The power gap rule

Compare the Grid level of the actor and that of the challenge in the relevant sphere. One level of difference is enough. T57

  • If the PC is above (level 2 hero against a level 1 thug): automatic success. You don’t roll to see whether you win. It is narrated, or you roll Style (rule B).
  • If the PC is below (level 1 human against a level 2 tank): automatic failure / immunity. Don’t roll: your blows do nothing to it. Find a Key or change your approach. Suggested narration: “It’s like punching a mountain. It doesn’t even flinch.”
  • Same level: roll the dice using rules 4.1–4.4.

ED-6 The draft titled this rule “(+/−2)”, a leftover from a version in which two levels were needed. The author settled that one is enough.

B · The Style Rule (handling mooks)

It kicks in when you face enemies below your Grid level or who are clearly scenery (mooks with no narrative importance).

  1. The premise: there is no doubt you are going to win. You are a level 3 swordmaster and they are level 1 tavern thugs. Rolling attack and damage is a waste of time.
  2. The action: you don’t roll to see whether you hit. It is taken for granted that you hit and take them out. No hit points are subtracted from the mooks.
  3. The Style roll: Style never costs: on the cards with success at a cost, that band reads here as a plain success, because you roll Style precisely when you can no longer fail. ED-26 You roll once with the active Tone Card + Style (+2), to see how you win.
ResultWhat happens
FumbleYou get there, but you look ridiculous. You win anyway: that was never in doubt. What you drop is the pose.
FailureYou get there in the most forgettable, unremarkable way. It works, that is all, and maybe nobody was even watching.
SuccessYou look magnificent. Effective and clean, with pirouettes and astonished faces.
CriticalShow off. You describe the scene, and make it unforgettable: movie choreography, several enemies in one fluid action, rumours born that night. If it fits what is happening, the GM may also hand you a temporary advantage.

ED-7 The draft had two versions (4.5, “difference of 0 or 1”, with four results; and 6.2, “below your level”, with three). They are merged: the trigger is the one from 6.2, which matches rule A and the author’s correction T113, and the table keeps the four results. A Style critical does not heal. Recovering health with a flourish makes no sense; stress can be negotiated at the table, but never automatically, and the specific effect is decided by the Director together with the player. Author’s decision

Tone warning

This rulebook is universal, and this rule does not suit every universe: few things feel more out of place in a cosmic horror game. The author advises against it in very serious universes. T113

  • Where it shines: anime, wuxia, space opera, action films.
  • Where it breaks immersion: realistic horror, war drama, survival. In Saving Private Ryan, killing an extra costs sweat and fear, and even the last mook rolls dice.

That is why Style sits apart on the sheet, like a sixth sphere that does not depend on the pyramid.

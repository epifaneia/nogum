Narrativa Ontológica General Universal y Metafísica

Yes, the "official" title is in Spanish, purely to leave you slightly lost. The real one is underneath. Click it: there is more than one.

A universal rulebook for universes that is, above all, a universal rulebook for emotions. This is the introduction: the idea holding it up, the strategies that came out of it, and what each of the four pieces does.

1 · What this is about

NOGUM is a universal system. It works for a sword, for a laser and for a spell, exactly as the others do. That is not the interesting part, because five or six excellent systems already do it, and they have been doing it well for decades.

What NOGUM is after is something else: to be a universal rulebook for emotions as well. Not for settings or genres, which in the end are scenery. For what the people around the table feel when the dice land.

What tone is, and why it is not genre

Picture a jump. A character runs along a rooftop and has to clear a three-metre gap. That action, exactly that one, can produce three completely different emotions.

It can produce elation: the jump is a formality, they clear it easily, and the roll exists so the table can see how well they do it. It can produce tension: it is close, it could go wrong, and everybody goes quiet while the die rolls. Or it can produce dread: they probably will not make it, and if they do not, what comes next is not a scratch.

None of that is decided by the genre. The rooftop is the same rooftop in a superhero story, in a thriller and in a nightmare. It is decided by the rules, because the rules are what says how many times out of ten you make it, what happens when you do not, and whether making it costs you anything.

That is what we call tone: the emotion a system produces by default, whoever is rolling and whatever they are playing. It is not a setting note. It is not an illustration. It is what the rules are already doing to the table, whether anybody decided it or not.

Every rulebook carries a tone inside, even when it does not say so

Here is the discomfort all of this came from, and it is not a theoretical one: this manual is the answer to years of discomfort as a designer and as a storyteller, running games where the system and the story pulled in different directions and knowing it was nobody at the table’s fault. Universal systems are universal in form —they give you a sheet that works for anything— and rigid underneath: the tone comes factory-fitted.

Black-comedy systems, with their "success at a cost", are magnificent for grimy drama and constant consequences. But try playing the Silver Age with them. You are a sun god, a paragon of virtue and infinite power, stopping a bank robber, and the system hands you a partial success: you do it, but you stumble. In The Boys that result is perfect. In classic heroic fantasy it wrecks the scene, and not because the player did anything wrong.

The same thing in reverse. Aspect-driven systems push towards competent, proactive characters, which is the exact opposite of what purist cosmic horror needs, where the whole point is to be small and unable.

In both cases the same thing happens, and it is the part that stings: the rule was working against the story. Not because the rule was bad —they are excellent rules— but because it carried an emotion inside that nobody had declared and that could not be switched off.

The thesis

NOGUM is a ludo-narrative thesis about how rules amplify emotion at the table. If the rules are what decide what people feel, then emotion cannot be the last thing you consult: it has to be the first thing you choose, and the rules have to come afterwards, in its service.

Specific strategies came out of that thesis. Not a new rulebook from scratch, but a handful of small changes that change the whole emotion: how two dice are read, how much health a body has, what happens when you only just make it.

And the promise they try to keep is exactly this: that one single rulebook genuinely serves both the most unleashed epic and the most suffocating horror. Not with a different bestiary, not by swapping the creatures or the scenery, but by changing what the table feels.

The happy accident

This began as a modularity that would let each GM design their campaigns with the right tools, whatever the tone of their universe. One decision per campaign, at the start, and then play.

But by designing the rule changes to be that simple and that fluid, something unplanned turned up. If turning the tone costs so little, there is no reason at all to turn it only once.

It is not only that the tone can be adapted for each campaign: you can play an entire session in a different tone from the rest of the campaign —a comic relief, a relaxed interlude between dark arcs. And the capacity for change is such that an advanced GM can adjust the tone in every scene… and even give different tones to different characters at the same table.

That is what this introduction is here to argue, and it is what no other universal rulebook knows how to do: not that the system works for everything, but that it works for different things within the same afternoon.

2 · The mixing desk

If emotion is something you choose, you need something to choose it with. NOGUM does not hand you a genre switch with "fantasy / cyberpunk / horror" written beside it. It hands you a mixing desk: a few independent controls you can raise and lower separately, which combined give a great many places to live.

There are few of them on purpose. Nobody uses a mixing desk with forty faders.

Optimism: whose side is the universe on?

The first control asks whether the world pushes with you or against you. An optimistic universe is one where things tend to work out: not because the characters are better, but because reality is on their side. An oppressive universe is one where things tend to go wrong even when you do everything right.

This is not difficulty. Difficulty is set by the scene —that door is harder than this one. Optimism is a property of the entire universe, constant, there before any door shows up.

Chaos: how predictable is what is about to happen?

The second control is independent of the first, and that is what makes it interesting. A world can be optimistic and predictable —classic epic, where you win and you win cleanly— or optimistic and chaotic —pulp, where you win but always the hard way and something always happens.

Chaos does not decide whether you win. It decides what the thing that happens to you is made of: whether results come clean or with an invoice, whether extremes are rare or constant, whether the table can plan or can only react.

Health: how much can a body take?

The third control is the one fewest people expect, and the one that prints the most fear. In NOGUM, how much damage a person can take is not a property of anatomy: it is a tone control.

With a short health track, a knife kills you and every fight is frightening. With a long one, a fight is an action sequence that lasts a whole session. It is the same person in both cases. What changed is how long the story wants to last.

And the controls you do not touch mid-game

There are others —the scale of the conflict, the rate at which people grow, how many optional rules are switched on— but those are chosen when you set the table up and then they stay put. The three above are the ones that can move on the fly, and they are what makes the happy accident possible.

3 · Rigour: the promise can be checked

Everything above sounds good and is worth nothing unless it turns into numbers. Anyone can sign "tone matters". The hard part is making a turn of a control move the statistics in a specific, measurable, written-down way.

In NOGUM you always roll the same thing: a red ten-sided die and a blue one. The only thing that changes between tones is what you do with them, and every way of reading them has an effect you can calculate.

Keeping the higher of the two lifts the average from 5.5 to 7.15 and, on top of that, narrows the spread: it does not just come out better, it comes out better more reliably. Keeping the lower drops it to 3.85, with the mirror effect. Adding them draws a bell that crushes the extremes down to 3%: a clockwork world, where surprises barely exist. Reading the red die alone is a straight line where every face is worth exactly 10%: neutral chaos, the hobby's habit.

Crossing those readings with the width of the fumble and critical bands gives seven tone cards, laid out on a three-by-three grid: optimism against chaos. There are two gaps, and they are not an oversight: with a flat die there is no way to tilt the tone without touching the difficulty, and we would rather not touch it.

The probabilities for all seven, at every level of competence, are not estimates or impressions. They are the exact count of all one hundred possible combinations, calculated and verified, and printed in the manual so that anybody can argue with them.

And that is where they are, because they do not fit here. If you would rather check it than take our word for it, the Complete Game has a whole chapter for exactly that: the Engine Room, with the seven curves drawn, each card’s outcome split bonus by bonus, and what every tone really costs the table. Fair warning: it is the densest chapter in the book, and going in is at your own risk. But this chapter’s promise is kept there, down to the last figure.

4 · Genuinely modular

"Modular" is one of those words that appears on the back cover of everything and almost never means anything. Here it means something very specific: the core, with nothing added to it, is already a complete game.

Tone, competence, damage, health and the currency of the impossible. You can run an entire campaign on that without opening another page. Everything else —and there is plenty— lives in a chest of sixteen optional rules that come on one at a time, each with its switch, its attention cost declared in writing, and its warning about when it gets in the way.

None is compulsory. And there is one house rule that matters more than any of them: if pulling out a module means explaining it for ten minutes, do not pull it out. The story has to flow. Less is more, and when it is not, the manual says so.

Modularity also runs downward, which is the part almost nobody offers. The same book serves a table that enjoys tracking numbers and one that wants to track none: some questions, answered a certain way, switch off everything that makes you write things down. A system that boasts of being universal and makes you use all of it is not universal. It is big.

5 · The strategies, grouped

These are the families of decisions that came out of the thesis. They are not all the rules in the manual: they are the ones that exist because emotion rules, and that would fall with the thesis if it fell.

How to print fear or elation

This is where the two controls live that decide whether the table leaves the session elated or hunched.

The first is what you do with the dice. Keeping the higher, keeping the lower, adding them or looking at one alone are not four decorative variants: they are four different universes with four different averages and four different ways of handing out surprises. Choosing the reading is choosing the emotional genre.

The second is lethality, which most systems treat as a realism detail when it is a first-order tone control. A short health track does not make the world more realistic: it makes it more terrifying. It makes people hide, negotiate, run. A long one does not make the characters stronger: it lets fights have three acts. When you want to print fear, shorten the health track before touching anything else.

How to administer chaos

The chaos family covers everything that decides how much chance rules over plan: how wide the bands of disaster and of glory are, and whether only just making it comes free or charges a toll.

Two ideas govern it. The first is that disaster and glory are read on the dice before anything is added: a natural one is a natural one at any level of competence, which means the world does not bend to your sheet however high you climb. The second is stranger than it sounds: raising chaos does not make you fail more. It changes the texture of everything that happens, not its frequency.

Why the statistics never break

All the promises above come with a condition: if the numbers could grow without limit, they would stop meaning anything. A curve calculated for a range of six rungs turns into rubbish the moment somebody reaches rung forty.

That is why competence in NOGUM is anchored: it runs from one end to the other in six rungs and stops there, always, for everyone, forever. No inflation, no redoing tables halfway through a campaign, and —the important part— the charts in this manual are still true in session one hundred. Tone still rules when the characters are veterans, which is exactly when other systems stop being able to promise anything.

But an anchor creates a problem: if nobody goes past six rungs, how do you express the difference between a villager and a dragon? Not with bonuses, because they do not fit. With the Grid: a second dimension, of scale levels, saying how far away the camera is. Within one level of the Grid, everyone competes on the same six rungs and the charts hold. Between different levels, the question is no longer who rolls better: it is whether the scene allows a roll at all. That settles impossible confrontations without inventing a single new number.

The rate at which people grow

And there is one last decision that is almost never treated as what it is. The speed at which characters progress, and the power they start with, print as much personality as any of the rules above.

A campaign where you grow quickly feels like adventure and promise: the best is yet to come. One where you grow slowly, or barely at all, feels like survival and craft: what you have is what there is. And starting powerful is not the same as becoming powerful, even when the final number is identical.

So progression is not fixed: it is chosen according to what the tone asks for, exactly like the reading of the dice or the length of the health track. It is one more control on the mixing desk, and one of the most noticeable.

6 · The problem with endless lists

Universal rulebooks have a well-known disease: in order to serve everything, they end up listing everything. Forty disciplines. Two hundred specialities. Hundred-page equipment catalogues covering everything from the short sword to the neural implant.

Those lists do not get read. They frighten on the page, get consulted twice and are abandoned, and they make a light system look heavy before anyone has tried it. But removing them does not work either, because then the manual stops being specific and hands the work back to you: "make up your own cyberpunk gear, mate".

NOGUM refuses to choose between the two. The lists are there, complete and specific. And there is an app that shows you only the slice your world uses.

You set the table up with forms that ask nothing technical: they describe situations and you pick the one that sounds like your game. Out of that come tone, scale, lethality and the optional rules, without anyone having had to understand a curve.

And from that moment the catalogue narrows itself. If your world has no magic, you never see the arcane disciplines: they are not hidden, they do not exist in your game. If it has cyberware, the search finds it by what it does, not by what it is called. The sheet prints, equipment comes off what that character can actually pay, supporting cast is built in ten seconds, and every tone card brings its own music.

The best of both worlds: a manual that is fast to use and specific at the same time. The book keeps everything that might be needed; the app decides which part you get to see today.

7 · The version for the impatient

There is a short version, NOGUM Accelerated, and it is not a timid summary: it is the thesis underlined. It keeps what is most recognisable and throws out the rest.

Four modes instead of seven cards, and each changes one thing and one only. Optimistic changes the die: you roll two and keep the higher. Neutral changes nothing, it is the reference. Chaos changes what the result is made of: same die, wider bands and successes that charge a toll. Terror changes what you can take: the same die as Neutral, but three health boxes instead of nine. One difficulty, no chest, no dials to operate.

And something happens there that entry-level rulebooks rarely do: it teaches the thesis with its own dice. Chaos and Neutral bring exactly the same number of rolls home. Chaos does not make you fail more; it changes what happens to you when you roll. Where Neutral deals clean successes and clean failures, Chaos deals twice the disasters, twice the glories, and wins that come with an invoice.

And Terror pulls the other trick: the darkness does not come from the roll, it comes from the health track. You hit just as often as in Neutral; what changes is that two serious hits take you off the scene. Those three boxes are anchored to the mode rather than being a switch you have to remember, so the table gets a look at the lethality control without having to operate it.

Best of all, it is not a separate game. All four modes are legal configurations of the Complete Game. When it starts to feel small you do not begin from scratch: you already know four of the seven cards.

8 · Four pieces, one thing

NOGUM is published as four pieces that read separately and are used together. It is not a collection: each one does a job the others cannot do, and it is the combination that does not exist anywhere else.

The Idea

What you are reading. It answers why: where the idea comes from, what strategies came out of it, and what the system promises. It reads in one sitting and contains not a single rule. If somebody wants to know what is different about NOGUM without committing to anything, they start and finish here.

The Complete Game

The actual manual. The engine, character creation, danger, equipment, the chest of optional rules, twenty ready-to-play world recipes and the appendices with every exact figure, in case you want to argue with them. It answers how, and answers it all the way down: every rule says where it came from and what tone it prints.

Accelerated

The same game in fifteen pages and four modes. It answers now: you can start this very afternoon without having read anything else. And since its four modes are real configurations of the complete game, playing it is not a demo: it is having learned four sevenths of the grid without noticing.

The App

The piece no other universal system has. It answers with you: it interviews you with situations instead of technical questions, sets the table up, narrows the catalogue to your world, builds the sheets and the supporting cast, keeps track of what everything costs and puts music to every tone. It turns the classic problem of universal roleplaying —"universal means you have to decide everything"— into twenty questions that answer themselves.

And why together they are something else

A normal universal rulebook gives you a book and wishes you luck. The load of deciding, narrowing and remembering falls entirely on the GM, which is why "universal" ends up meaning, in practice, "set aside a weekend".

Here that weight is shared out. The book keeps everything that might be needed. The app decides which part you see today. Accelerated lets you feel it before reading anything. And this introduction tells you why all of the above is the way it is.

The result is a system that does not ask you to choose between deep and playable, and a table that can change emotion between two scenes without changing rulebook. That is the wager, and all of it is on the table.

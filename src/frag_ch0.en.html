Chapter 0 · Session Zero

Before writing the campaign, before choosing the module, before making a single sheet, the table sits down to talk. That conversation is session zero, and in NOGUM it is not a formality: it is where the gentlemen’s agreement of the introduction gets signed. Everything that comes after —the tone, the numbers, the pace at which you grow— comes out of here. T184

NOGUM is a proposal, and it is worth saying it out loud before you start. The rules are vague on purpose: they leave gaps for the table to fill with judgement. The focus is on emotion, not on the fairness of the dice. And the rules work for you: they are set aside when they get in the way of what the story asks for. If someone at the table is after something else —a tactical duel, an exact simulation—, this is the moment to find out, not session five.

0.1 Everyone’s red lines

Nothing is imposed that makes anyone feel bad

Nothing that could make someone at the table genuinely feel bad goes into the game. It is not a vote, nor a list of forbidden topics: each person has their own red lines, and one is enough for it to be respected. It needs no justification, and it is not up for debate.

Lines are not only what is usually called strong content. They can be:

  • explicit sex or violence;
  • a specific subject that hits close to home for someone, or a phobia: spiders, enclosed spaces, deep water;
  • an oppressive atmosphere, or a campaign where nothing is ever going to go well;
  • or something as seemingly harmless as a long philosophical exploration of the meaning of your actions, which some find fascinating and others unbearably tedious.

They are all worth the same. That you love something does not oblige anyone to live through it.

Some lines are not said in front of everyone. They can be told privately to the Director, and that is enough: nobody has to explain to the table where each change comes from.

Some show up late. There are things you do not know will get to you until they come up. When that happens, they are removed, without asking permission and without explanations, and the game goes on.

What weighs is asked for first. Oppressing, frustrating, a plot that gives nobody a break: all of that can be a great game, but it is asked at session zero. And if it crosses a line for someone, it does not happen, even if everyone else wants it. Nobody drags anybody into an experience they do not want to live.

0.2 What we are going to feel

The second conversation is the one that looks most like choosing a genre, but it is not about genres: it is about emotions. Saying “horror” is not enough: you need to know which horror.

  • What is going to be felt at the table. Anguish, euphoria, laughter, melancholy, the vertigo of the epic.
  • What you want to explore, and what bores you. “I want existential dread explored.” “I want epic, and don’t bore me with family dramas.”
  • Which areas of the plot are respected. A past a player does not want touched, a relationship that does not break, a place that is sacred to the story.
  • What kind of solutions the genre asks for. Are things solved here by investigating, fighting, talking, running? Does the fairground trick count? In a pulp game, the shark repellent pulled from the utility belt is a worthy scene and a good laugh. In a campaign that asked you to find out how to hurt the final enemy, getting rid of them with a cheap trick is something else.

With this the Director already knows which card to pick (chapter 1) and how far to take it. What is agreed is a range, not each scene: if the campaign can reach Nightmare, the Director moves within what was agreed without asking every time (7.3). Whatever goes beyond that goes back to the table.

0.3 Who proposes

It depends on the table and on how flexible its Director is. Usually the Director arrives with an idea —a world, a tone, a couple of images— and the table adjusts it. It can be the other way round, and the conversation can start from a blank page.

What you are after is consensus, always bearing in mind who does the hard work. The Director is going to write, prepare and sustain the campaign for months, and will not write well a story they do not feel like telling, however much the table asks for it. Their appetite counts as much as everyone else’s red lines. The players do not impose a campaign on the Director, and the Director does not impose on anyone what they do not want to live through.

0.4 “That doesn’t count”

This rulebook does not devote a line to firewalls against foul play. It is designed for adults, and it is not afraid of the cheap combo: what stops it is the table. When someone says “that doesn’t count”, they are not attacking anyone nor arguing a rule: they are reminding everyone of what you agreed at session zero.

That is why that conversation has to exist, however short. Without it, “that doesn’t count” is one person’s opinion against another’s. With it, it is everyone’s word. It is said without drama, the scene is redone if needed, and the game goes on.

0.5 Going back to session zero

The agreement is not a closed contract. Anyone can ask to stop and go over it, and it is worth doing when an arc changes, when the tone changes or when someone has been left out of the story. It takes maturity to talk it through and steer the experience together; session zero is where that conversation already began.

0.6 And the numbers come out of that

Only after all of the above do you touch the mechanics, and almost everything follows on its own. Each thing is explained in its place; this is the list, so you forget none:

  • The tone: card, length of the life bar and how far the campaign can go (chapter 1, 7.3).
  • The world’s ceiling: the highest whole-sphere Grid a player can have in each sphere. The recipes bring a proposal, but the question is yours: is it possible in your world? (2.4).
  • How the Grid reads: tangible, as in the real world, or narrative, the hero the horde does not touch (2.2). T188
  • What you start with: 1·1·1·1·2 at most, or starting experience, or a pacted jump (2.4, 3.5).
  • The pace of the two wallets: how many points and how many Keys, and who grows (3.6).
  • The bars: which ones exist in your world, and which can retire a sheet, if any; a protagonist’s death is always pacted (5.5).
  • The Rules Chest modules you want to use (8.1).

The rules work for you

We give you tools so that progress and tone stay under control. Use them while they serve you, and ignore them when they stop you from doing something the plot asks for.

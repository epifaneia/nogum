Chapter 2 · The Anchor (The Power Grid)

In the previous chapter we broke reality. We gave you the tools to turn a serious story into an absurd comedy or a lethal nightmare simply by changing the way the dice are read. But if the world is so volatile… what stops the character from dissolving? What defines who you are if physics keeps changing?

We need an anchor. We need to separate, once and for all, “how good you are” from “how big your feats are”.

Seven ideas that explain everything

Almost every doubt in this chapter, and in the next one, is settled by one of these seven. If a rule looks odd, come back here: the rule comes from the idea, not the other way round. The hard cases, solved one by one, are in 2.6. T179

  1. The Grid is scale, not skill, and it is almost never human. Every level multiplies your reach a hundredfold across a whole sphere, and it is something nobody can resist: nearly always supernatural, or a technology that looks like it. That is why ones are plentiful, however big the game.
  2. If something grants a permission or breaks the Grid, it has Keys. By definition. Whether it comes from a shop, a workshop or a 3D printer.
  3. The Reaper is a Key. Without a Key you can at most harm what stands at your height; the Reaper reaches whoever stands above. That is exactly what a Key does.
  4. What is lent or stolen lasts one scene. Whoever uses it, and even if nobody does. Passing it around resets nothing.
  5. “Against everything” is the full purchase; “against one thing” is Limited. “Weapons against vampires” is not a different kind of purchase: it is the usual one, on a leash.
  6. What a Key does is what the Key says. A Key puts you one level above, also over what stood at your height, but only in what it covers. The bazooka is a Key to damage: it blows you apart if it hits, but it has to hit.
  7. Nothing is invoked outside the table. The Keys of a moment —the hacker club, twenty mercenaries firing at once, your sister— are set up by the fiction, and they never skip progression. Your contact may be on holiday.

2.1 Immutable competence (the “who”)

Let's start with a statistical truth: being a master means not failing. In most roleplaying games, levelling up means inflating your numbers to the point of absurdity. In NOGUM, competence is an absolute, closed value. We define mastery not by how much damage you deal, but by the reliability with which you carry out your will.

This number (+4) is your anchor. And here is the key to the system: it never changes, whatever genre you are playing. The rubber pirate is a Grand Master (+4) in Brawling. The hitman who avenges dogs is a Grand Master (+4) in Brawling. A neighbourhood boxer in a realist film is a Grand Master (+4) in Brawling. They all have the same modifier, roll the same two dice and have the same chance of hitting their opponent (in Epic, practically certain). The statistics are identical because their degree of mastery is identical: they are the best at what they do.

But if they all have the same number… why does the rubber pirate's punch shatter an island while the hitman who avenges dogs's only shatters a jaw?

That's why NOGUM never adds dice as power goes up. In games where you roll fistfuls of dice, the fumble probability breaks. Here we anchor the statistics, and what moves is the world. T26

To assign the power level of a character, a weapon or a spell, don't look at its stats. Look at its stage and ask yourself: if this gets completely out of hand, what makes the news?

How to use it at the table (the logic of the Key)

To know whether a character needs to roll dice, the GM compares levels:

  • You are at level 1 (pistol) and facing level 3 (tank): you don't roll. Your scale is “room” and theirs is “building”. You can't affect it.
  • You are at level 1 (hacker) and using a level 3 tool (military virus): you use the Key. Now you operate at level 3 and can launch an attack against an entire corporate building.

The complete rule, in both directions, is in 4.5.

2.3 Explaining the Key

The Key is any narrative explanation that lets you interact with a higher level of the Grid. Without it, the feat on the next rung is impossible. With it, you roll with your sheet and with the campaign's tone, with no penalty at all: you are still the best at what you do. T28

  • The barbarian of the steppe is an epic hero. Even without superpowers, the comics make it clear he can fight hundreds of “normal” men at once. A rank-and-file soldier has no right, on paper, to so much as scratch him (just as we'll never see it in any comic). But he is mortal, and we can design a scenario in which he is vulnerable: after a battle, he celebrates, drinks mead and collapses into a sleep as deep as the bowels of the Earth. Something that simple can be the Key: a rank-and-file soldier catches him in his bed, and the system lets him harm someone who, without that narrative key, would be out of his reach.
  • The vigilante millionaire versus the alien in tights and trunks. The vigilante millionaire is a mortal and the alien in tights and trunks is a god, but the alien in tights and trunks has the most famous weak spot in comics: the stone from his own world. If the vigilante millionaire manages to use it, they can face each other as equals. It is the Key that unlocks the feat of fighting a god.
  • A hacker can black out an entire building or a stadium: they are mortal, but technology allows them level 3 feats. On their own they are lethal; as part of a team they can destabilise nations. If they summon every hacker in a large anonymous collective and coordinate an attack on the country's financial centres, that attack is a Key to reach level 5. The effects are far greater, but the roll is the usual one, with the full bonus.

What one Key covers

A Key is one Discipline against its target for ever, or a whole Sphere against its target for one scene. You pick the shape when you buy it. ED-27

  • Narrow and permanent. One of the Sphere’s eight Disciplines against its target, chosen by you and yours for good. It works against what it has written on it: “Weapons against vampires” works against vampires, not against a werewolf. Always a whole Discipline, never a Signature: what narrows it is the target, not the skill. That is why covering a Sphere by hand takes eight Keys, one per Discipline, and the count below adds up. It is stingy on purpose. A sword gives you no cover against the dragon’s breath, because surviving the breath is Fortitude and you bought Weapons. T176
  • Wide and scene-long. If the Key is very specific, it covers the whole Sphere against its target, but only for one scene —the duel, or the assault on the castle with its allies inside—, and it is crossed off when the scene ends. T176

The wide Key aims at a target, but it does not stay with it: it lives for one scene. A monster that comes back once you know how to kill it has stopped being an antagonist; and if it comes back with something new, it is not the same creature and your Key does not work on it. The scene is this game’s unit of time, as it already was for tone and for Style.

Going higher against the same thing

One Key puts you one level above in what it covers, also over what stood at your height. What limits it is what it says: the bazooka is a Key to damage, not to hitting (2.6). To climb further against the same thing the ladder is square: going up n levels costs n² Keys. ED-32

Optional rule · The flat ladder

This is a universal rulebook and sooner or later someone will want to reach godhood. If you would rather the price did not explode, use 3n−2: 1, 4, 7, 10, 13. It agrees with the square ladder on the first two rungs —where almost every table plays— and only parts company when someone goes for divinity. The square one says “divinity is conquered in play”; the flat one says “divinity is bought in instalments”. Both are defensible; the square one is the default, because n² is remembered without a table.

The exchange unit: Keys and Grid

The Grid and Keys measure the same thing —levels above— in two sizes. A Key raises one Discipline. A Grid level raises the whole Sphere: its eight Disciplines, against everything. That is why the Grid is counted in pots of five Keys and climbs the same square ladder: being n levels above in a Sphere is worth 5·n² Keys. T173

The ratio is always five eighths, on every rung, and it is not new: it is the creation pot’s, five Keys or one Grid level (3.5). The Grid is the bundle: the whole Sphere for five eighths of what it would cost piece by piece. And that is what makes the count fair: Keys are never worth more than the Grid they replace. Whoever turns down a jump collects five eighths of what covering the Sphere by hand would cost, never more, so turning it down never beats rising. With the flat ladder the ratio is the same.

What it is for: the Grid is opened by the plot (2.4), but every jump has a price, and that makes it exchangeable. Whoever does not want to rise collects its value; whoever wants to rise sooner, or where the others do not, pays it when the story gives permission (3.6).

The Key, from start to finish

What happens to a Key from the moment you buy it to the moment you lose it. T176

  • The ceiling. Above the universe’s ceiling (2.4) only Keys with a target count. Nothing bought with Keys rises “against everything” above the ceiling: a power that rises against everything —Room-strength, Building-strength— is Grid and respects the ceiling.
  • Turning down a jump. The Keys you collect for not rising can only be spent in that sphere.
  • Numbers are a Key of the moment. Numerical superiority puts you one level above in what it covers while the fiction holds it up, like any Key. It never skips progression.
  • The Reaper above your level breaks the Grid and is paid like any weapon: x² Keys, where x is the levels above whoever carries it. A Reaper 2 in the hands of a level 1 costs 1 Key; a Reaper 3, 4.
  • Anything Limited below one Key comes free for one scene and is used up. To have it again you must get it again in the fiction, never from the sheet (3.5).
  • Synchronisation is Limited only if it can really be lost at the table. A chrome body or a suit you never take off pays the full Grid: 5·x².
  • What the plot lends you —the robot the mission assigns you— synchronises for free for as long as the plot lends it.
  • Equipment can always be given back. When you are no longer going to use it —you sell it, it breaks, you leave it—, its points and its Keys become free again for something else. What lasts one scene —what is lent, what is stolen, the mission’s robot— cost nothing, so it gives nothing back. If you hand it over, whoever releases it does not get their Keys back and whoever receives it pays them (below). T181
  • Grid jumps, for everyone or their equivalent. The Director decides. A Grid level is so big that, if only one character feels it, it feels unfair. So, by default, when one rises, the others get something of the same weight: their own jump, its value in Keys, or whatever the story gives them. Nobody has to do the same thing; nobody should feel cheated. The Director can stagger it —you today, you in three weeks— or hand out small rewards for good sessions. The price is counted per sphere, and whoever turns down a jump they are offered collects its value in Keys, in that sphere. T181

Own Keys and lent Keys

Own Key
Bought at creation or with the experience wallet (3.6). It is permanent if it is narrow —the wide one is crossed off when its scene ends—, written on the sheet with its target and its level, and agreed with the Director before play starts. Nobody can take it from you. If it is equipment and you are no longer going to use it —you sell it, it breaks, you leave it—, its Keys become free again for something else. T176 T181
Lent Key
Given by the Director for the plot or to reward a good idea: “it occurs to you to use the holy water”. It is not paid for and it does not go up a level. It goes on the sheet, but in pencil: it lives in the scene and leaves with it. ED-28

With that distinction the Director can keep handing out ideas without inflating the character, and whoever paid for theirs knows it is theirs.

Lending and handing over

A single-use Key is simply declared: you give the silver bullet to whoever shoots best and that is that. A permanent one is handed over: the owner releases it —and, like anything you give up, gets it back free— and the other pays for it out of their own wallet. It is a transaction and not a loan, which forces the group to decide who it serves best, instead of passing the amulet from hand to hand with nobody ever paying. T176

And one line settles almost every table case: what you carry in your hand can be lent; what you carry in your head cannot. The holy water changes owner. Knowing the true name does not.

Equipment that breaks the Grid —the railgun that punches through a tank— is also bought with Keys, and has its three locks in 4.3.

2.4 Categorising power levels: the Grid (the narrative zoom)

To handle all this without going mad with maths, we introduce the Grid, a conceptual tool that tells the Game Master where the camera is. Imagine a zoom lens: when it is closed (level 1), we see the dirty details of the street and a knife wound is a deadly problem. When it opens (level 5), we see empires and gods; a knife wound is irrelevant, because here people throw mountains.

This character is a cyberpunk rocker: an ordinary human, with everything at level 1. Their fame does not raise their Grid: it gives them Keys, tied to their status and to what their name can summon. With four —two rungs up, 2.3— they bring thousands of fans out onto the streets, as a level 3 would. But only for that: their voice does not intimidate a whole building just because, nor does it sway a crowd that has no idea who they are.

Raising the Grid is a serious thing. In a neon city, the only one who goes past 1 is the chrome job, with a 2 in Physical. A 4 in Social is no longer a rocker: it is the living symbol of a city. It sounds like a comic because it is one.

Grid or Key?

One question: does your character beat anything in that sphere, or just one thing? If anything, it is Grid. If just one thing —their fans, their computer, vampires, holy water—, it is a Key.

The temptation is to give high Grid to everything that sounds huge in the fiction. It almost never is: fame, talent or the special gadget point at something specific, and those are Keys. High Grid is for whoever is huge against everything.

Would a hacker able to disable the defences of an entire building be a character with a 3 in Technical? No. With a 3 in Technical you can build or repair anything the size of a building. The hacker has level 1 in Technical, and their computer is a Key to affect level 3. As in the earlier example, they can take another Key (more powerful software, a team of hackers) and launch an even bigger attack.

One level is two zeros

Every rung of the Grid multiplies your reach by a hundred: how many people you get to, how big a problem you touch, how much world you move at once. ED-29

LevelReachThe image
1×1One person
2×100A full room
3×10,000A building, a district
4×1,000,000A city
5×100,000,000A country

The rungs were already there —person, room, building, city, country—; the number just puts itself in front of them. And with the number in front, this chapter’s hard rule stops looking arbitrary: when someone has a level on you they are not “better than you”, they are playing with two more zeros. That is why there is no roll.

It is reach, not literal truth. A Physical 2 does not lift a hundred times more weight or run a hundred times faster: they face a hundred times more world at once. The number is an image, not a conversion table. The moment someone asks “so how much does that weigh?”, the answer is “whatever the scene needs”.

And it makes the Key more dramatic: what you buy with it is fighting a hundred times above your weight, against one specific thing.

There is no ×10 version. A tenfold jump is exactly what already separates a −2 from a +4: the pyramid buys that, not the Grid. ×100 sounds like comics because it is comics, and if it gets out of hand in your campaign the answer is not to shrink the scale: it is to leave everybody at Grid 1 and play with skill points. For everything in between there is the small Key: a computer with a connection lets you shut down a whole building without being level 3.

Being is not knowing

  • There is no level 0. Level 1 is an ordinary human. The bonus (−2 to +4) is what you know; the Grid is what you are. You can be at level 1 and be the best in the world in that sphere. T91
  • Ontology beats competence. The best fighter in the world has no right to a roll against the weakest enhanced human (level 2): The perfect soldier will wipe the floor with you in the contact sport of your choice, even if he has never practised it.
  • The Grid doesn't measure brains or muscle: it measures starting tools and scale. It is not about doing amazing things (the bonus gives you that), but things on another level, which would require an entire team the size of that level. T133
  • To have a level in a sphere you must be competent across the whole sphere. A person of ordinary intellect who only excels at chemistry does not have mental level 2: they have a Key.
  • Physically, characters are “naked”: a human body against something much bigger. Technology lets ordinary humans break those ceilings, and tools have their own level (a grenade, level 2; an armoured combat suit, level 3). T92

Synchronisation: inheriting the scale

Anyone who pilots a mecha, wears an exoskeleton or a suit, or fuses with an entity inherits its sheet and its level for as long as the bond lasts. It is the rule of anime’s giant robots and of heroes who transform and combine. In other games, a mecha has a separate sheet. Here you just move your position on the Grid: the GM can even change the damage zoom mid-session (the pilot climbs into the giant robot and now has 27 health points, with the same sheet). T36 If the suit shuts down, you return to your own level. T181

An ordinary vehicle inherits nothing. Inside a vehicle they can shoot the vehicle —which has its own health— or you. What hits you takes points off you wherever you are, if bullets can get in through an ordinary window; an armoured one may have to be damaged before anyone can reach whoever is inside. The scene decides, and common sense. T181

What level do characters start at?

Characters start at level 1 in every sphere, plus the pot: 1·1·1·1·2 at most, in any universe and however big its scale. The game’s scale is set by the GM according to the genre, and it says how big the deeds and the threats are; it does not raise the characters’ spheres. If a table wants to start higher —established superheroes—, that is an agreed jump from session zero, sphere by sphere and at its price (2.3), or starting experience: whatever points and Keys the table wants, before play (3.5). Both work; the table chooses. T176

Ones are everywhere

However big the game, almost all of a character’s spheres are at 1. A super has at most two spheres above 1, and exceptionally three. A Grid level is ×100 in a whole sphere: far more powerful than a Key, and that is why it is far scarcer. The alien in tights and trunks is a 5 in Physical and a 1 in Social. T176

  • The pot can give you a 2. The +1 Grid from the separate pot (3.5) goes in any sphere whose ceiling, in your universe, reaches 2 or more (8.4). In cyberpunk only Physical gets there, so that 2 is the chrome job’s; in a game of courts it can be the Social, or any other the recipe lets climb. If you do not want that 2, you take 5 Keys instead: either you are big where your world lets you be big, or you are small and very specific at whatever you choose. And watch out, because this reads wrong easily: that 2 is not an obligation to place one, it is a scale your universe lets you reach. There are worlds that allow no 2 at all —a realist one, a zombie one, anything where nobody lifts a car— and their recipe shows five ones. There is nothing to choose there: everybody starts at [1,1,1,1,1] and takes the 5 Keys, which is how you buy being big in a place where being big does not come as standard. Author’s decision T91 T170
  • Levelling up costs no points, and the plot opens it. Grid level rises with the campaign, through narrative milestones, arc changes or time skips, and the Director decides it. But every jump is worth an exact number of Keys (2.3), and that makes it exchangeable: whoever does not want to rise collects its value, and whoever wants to adjust their rise has the means. The permission belongs to the story; the price, to the rule.   • The Grid changes the universe, not your competence. Levelling up changes the scale at which you operate and what the world lets you touch, and it changes the narration; it does not make you more skilled. What improves your competence are the modifiers (the pyramid), gifts and flaws.

The ceiling of the universe

The starting level is the same everywhere; the ceiling is set by the universe. And they have nothing to do with each other: the ceiling is how far you can get over a campaign, often 4 or 5, and it does not change the fact that everybody starts at 1 with, at most, the pot’s 2. In a cyberpunk game there is no such thing as a Mental 2, however long the campaign runs: there is no wits-times-a-hundred. What there is are chrome jobs on the edge of psychosis, who reach Physical 2, and there the genre ends. Each of the twenty recipes (8.4) carries its ceiling per sphere, and that ceiling is what makes a universe feel like that universe — more than tone, more than equipment. ED-30

The Grid still rises with the story, but against that ceiling. And the Key is the agreed exception: in cyberpunk you cannot have wits times a hundred, but you can have the exploit that works against that AI. A ceiling with no exceptions is boring; an exception with no ceiling breaks the game. Here the ceiling is permanent and the exception is bought. That is why, above the ceiling, only Keys with a target count: nothing bought with Keys rises “against everything” beyond it (2.3). T176

2.5 The synthesis: the Anchor and the Sail

This is how NOGUM manages to be universal without being generic.

  • The bonus (+4) is your anchor. It tells you that you are competent, that you don't make silly mistakes and that your hands are steady. It keeps the character internally consistent, and the statistics always keep the emotion of the dice anchored to the tone of the game (or of the scene).
  • The Grid (level 1–5) is your sail. It tells you how far the wind of your actions reaches. It defines whether your perfect success “opens a door” or “opens a dimensional portal”.

2.6 Untangling: the Grid, the Key and what things cost

This section is dense on purpose. The questions are real: they came from readers and from table tests. Every one is answered by one of the seven ideas (the box at the start of the chapter), but it is not always clear at first sight, and that is why they are here, solved one by one. There is no need to read it in one go. It is there to come back to when, at the table, someone says “so then…?”. T179

What a Grid level is

Before the cases, a ladder, because an example is worth more than a definition. The Social one:

LevelWhat it is
1Any human, however charismatic. The rock star who fills stadiums is here.
2The vampire’s presence. It walks into the room and the whole room is spellbound. A human gets no roll.
3Odysseus’ sirens. Everyone who hears them, over a huge radius. Whole fleets throw themselves on the rocks.
4Orpheus’ song. A voice that changes the course of history: a whole city turns its revolution around.
5The gods of madness. Dream itself.

Notice what is missing: no human gets past 1, however famous. What the rock star has is not Grid, it is Keys: access to the masses, to donations, to an angry mob that will do what they ask. The Grid is what cannot be resisted.

The Grid

“Why doesn’t my superhero start with a 5 in everything?”
Idea 1. The alien in tights and briefs is a 5 in Physical and a 1 in Social. A superhero game is not a game of cosmic geniuses at everything: its scale sets the size of the threats, it does not lift your spheres. Nearly any super has at most two spheres above 1; three, at the very most.
“A rock star sways millions. Isn’t that Social 3?”
Idea 1. They are human: level 1. What makes them huge are their Keys —access to the masses, the mob, the donations—, and a Key has a target and can be lost. Grid 3 is the sirens: those are not disobeyed.
“Can I put the pot’s +1 in Social without a single point in Social?”
You can. You will win without rolling against any human. But the moment you peer into the world above —another vampire, another siren— you are at −2 in everything, because you cannot do anything in that sphere. No need to forbid it: it is a badly built sheet that wastes its best ability.
“In cyberpunk, why doesn’t the computer genius have Mental 2?”
Idea 1. Nobody thinks a hundred times faster than a human: in that world the only Grid above 1 is the perfect soldier’s, pure chrome, in Physical. The computer genius has something else: the exploit that works against that AI. A Key with a target, which is exactly what the universe’s ceiling lets through.
“I want to start with established heroes.”
You always start at 1·1·1·1·2 at most. To start higher there are two tools: starting experience (the points and Keys the table wants, before the first session, 3.5) or a pacted jump at session zero. Both are paid with the same count, and both are a conscious decision.

The Key

“I have ‘Weapons against vampires’. Does it work against the werewolf?”
Idea 5. No. A Key is the usual purchase on a leash: it carries its target written down, and its target is vampires. Against the werewolf you need another.
“And to dodge the vampire’s swipe?”
Not that either. It covers its Discipline, Weapons, and dodging is Acrobatics. To defend yourself from something above you, the Key has to be on the skill you defend with, or the wide one for the whole sphere for one scene.
“Can I put the Key on my Signature?”
No. A Key always goes on a whole Discipline. What narrows it is its target, not the skill. If we sliced it any finer, a sphere would stop being worth eight Keys and the count of the exchange unit would stop adding up.
“I am level 2 and the enemy is a level 1 human with a sheet.”
Idea 1: it is a power jump. Your blow goes in without a roll, and Style says how; he, against you, needs a Key. Whether he is scenery or not is the Director’s call; having a sheet does not stop him being below you. T181
“‘Weapons against zombies’: do I hit a zombie of my level without rolling?”
Idea 6. Yes. Against zombies, that Key puts you one level above, and the one above does not roll to succeed: you roll Style to see how. That is what you paid for. What limits it is what it says: only zombies, and only with Weapons. If it were a Key to damage only, like a bazooka, the damage would go in, but you would have to hit.
“We are twenty mercenaries firing at once.”
Idea 7. They count as one, and that one carries a Key of the moment: one level above in what it covers, the volume of fire. Against a lone man of their level, that is being above him. But the fiction holds it up: if tomorrow there are three of you, the Key is gone.
“My hacker club, my sister, my contact in the police.”
Idea 7. They are your excuse and your ability, not buttons. On your own you hack a room; with your club, a building. But the club has to be there, in the fiction and within reason, and the Director can tell you your contact is on holiday. None of these Keys skips progression.
“My world does not allow Mental 2. What about with Keys?”
Above the universe’s ceiling only Keys with a target count. The exploit against that AI, yes; “Mental 2 against everything”, no. A ceiling with no exceptions is dull; an exception with no ceiling breaks things.

Equipment and the Reaper

“I have a bazooka. Do I surely hit the tank?”
Idea 6. The bazooka is a Key to damage, not to hitting. If you hit, the tank feels it even if you are level 1; and if it hits you, it blows you apart, because that cannot be taken on the chest. But it has to hit: the missile can miss, and the target can take cover. A homing missile with your face in its memory would be something else: it covers damage and hitting, and it cannot be stopped or dodged. To survive it you need another Key, and it can be a narrative one: your hacker friend cuts its guidance for a second, and in that second you can dodge because she gave you the Key. That is why the bazooka is cheap: a single Discipline, and Limited to damage.
“The grenade carries a Reaper. Why does it cost Keys, if I pay for it with money?”
Idea 3. The Reaper takes anyone below its level out of the fight. If that level is above yours, you are reaching someone you could not: that is a Key, and it is paid with Keys. On top of the money. It is never free.
“Why does a brutal weapon cost so few Keys?”
Because it raises one Discipline, and for damage. The count is fair. What makes it rare is not the Keys: it is the world. It is under lock and key, it is sold by someone who has to be persuaded, it leaves a trail. The price of weapons is managed by the world, through Access and permits (4.3).
“This catalogue item grants a permission and lists no Keys.”
Idea 2. It is a typo, not a rule: if it grants a permission or breaks the Grid, it has Keys. Count them like any other purchase (2.3) and let the author know.
“We stole the C4 at the bank, and we keep passing it around.”
Idea 4. What is stolen lasts one scene, whoever uses it and even if nobody does. Passing it around resets nothing. When you leave the bank, the C4 is gone, or you pay for it.
“The exoskeleton: is it Grid or a Key? And how much?”
It puts you at level 3 across the whole Physical sphere while you wear it: that is Grid, Synchronisation. It is paid with the exchange-unit table: from 1 to 3 is twenty Keys; if you were already at 2, the difference, fifteen. If at the table it can really be taken from you, it is Limited, half. A suit you never take off is not Limited: it is your body.
“The giant robot is assigned to me by the mission.”
What the plot lends synchronises for free while the plot lends it. The day the robot is yours, it is paid for.

Points, Keys and limitations

“How much does it cost to raise a Discipline one level?”
Idea 5. Against everything, the full purchase: 2·x² Keys. Against one thing, Limited: x². The whole sphere, the Grid: 5·x². You never pay more than that: from three Disciplines against everything up, buy the Grid and take all eight.
“Something comes out at less than one Key.”
It is free for one scene, and it is used up. The grenade, the brew, the one-use patch. To have it again you have to get it again in the fiction; from the sheet, never.
“My character is a werewolf.”
Transforming is paid for like whatever it raises. If it turns you into a beast across the whole Physical sphere, it is Grid: five Keys to go from 1 to 2. At will, the full price. If only at full moon, without control, or if it costs you health, it is Limited: three. Never free.
“The table raises the Grid and I don’t want to rise.”
You collect the jump in Keys, five for one level, and you only spend them in that sphere. If you could collect them in spheres you do not use and pour them all into one, scarcity would be over.
“I want to start with a 3 in Physical.”
That is two levels above: twenty Keys, four pots. Either the table gives everyone the same, with starting experience, or it is a pacted jump at session zero. The count only says what is fair; what gets done is up to the table.

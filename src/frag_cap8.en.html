Chapter 8 · Appendices (The Toolbox)

So far you have read the theory: why the dice have emotions, why the +4 never moves and why health is a Russian doll. This chapter is the toolbox. It is not meant to be read straight through: it is meant to be consulted.

The order is no accident. It goes from the general to the specific and, finally, to the application:

  1. 8.1 Rules Chest: optional modules borrowed from other rulebooks (the general).
  2. 8.2 Skills list: the complete pyramid and the catalogue of Signatures (the specific).
  3. 8.3 Equipment lists: the Multiverse Bazaar, with its stat block (the specific).
  4. 8.4 Rulebook Emulation: twenty recipes that emulate famous universes (the application).
  5. 8.5 NOGUM Accelerated: the complete quick-play rulebook, for reference at the table (the summary).

The rules and the lists come before the emulations for a reason: no smoke and mirrors. The twenty recipes in 8.4 have been built solely from pieces that appear in this rulebook: Tone Cards, damage zoom, Power Grid, Keys, tags and the modules in the chest. If a recipe needs something, that something is in these pages. T117

And a warning of principle: here we emulate lore, not engines. You will not find recipes for playing “in the style of” another universal system. We emulate worlds, stories and feelings; we do not emulate the emulators. T125

Why is there no recipe for “how to play it like the aspect-driven narrative games” or “like an old-school simulator”? Because they already have their own rulebooks, and because NOGUM is not just another universal system: it is the machine that explains why each of them sounds the way it does. If you are coming from one of them, at the end of 8.4 there is a Rosetta Stone so you don't get lost.

Section 8.3 (the Multiverse Bazaar) has its own section.

Progress clocks

Progress clock

Draw a circle split into 4, 6 or 8 segments depending on how long the goal is (an infiltration, a chase, the end of the world). Each success on a roll that pushes towards the goal fills 1 segment; each critical, 2. Each failure on a risky roll fills a segment of the opposing clock (the villains' one: the alarm, the ritual, the tide). Whoever fills their clock first wins. Approved by the author T117

  • Travel clock. It measures the road, not the fight: each leg of the journey fills segments and each setback (storm, ambush, despair) costs them. It is the heart of recipe 3. T127
  • Supply clock. It replaces bookkeeping of arrows and rations. It only comes out when scarcity is the theme of the scene (lost in the desert); the rest of the time, the heroes have packed sandwiches. Approved by the author T119

The core already uses a clock without calling it one: the arterial bleeding at threshold 2 (5.6) is a 3-turn clock.

Recipes: 3 (“The Melancholy Stroll”), 15 (“Thieves in the Fog”). T176

Flashbacks

Retroactive planning

A player can interrupt the present to narrate a scene from the past that solves the current problem (“I don't have the key… but yesterday I bribed the guard”). The flashback is not paid for: it is rolled.

  • Roll whatever skill the plan calls for against the active card’s TN.
  • Success: the flashback happened, and you have what you went for.
  • Every point above the TN buys one more detail inside it: something you left ready, an exit, somebody placed where you needed them.
  • Failure: the plan existed, but it went crooked and you find out now.
  • Fumble: the past gives you away; the fumble menu (5.2) decides how.

That way the flashback stops being a currency and becomes a scene with risk, which is what the table wanted it for. Author’s decision T117

Recipes: 15 (“Thieves in the Fog”), where flashbacks are mandatory for planning the heist.

Devil's bargain

Risk for power

Before an important roll, the Game Master offers a deal: the action is resolved with Best of 2d10 against TN 6 (as on the Epic card), whatever card is active. In exchange, come what may, the Game Master applies a guaranteed narrative consequence, chosen from the menu of the 4 costs (5.6). The player may turn the bargain down. Approved by the author T117

The original version spoke of “+1 die”. In NOGUM you do not add dice or stack bonuses: the power of the bargain is shifting the reading towards optimism for one roll. ED-R1

Recipes: none of the twenty uses it. It is designed for horror and dark drama.

Exploding damage

Chained criticals

When the attacker rolls a critical, on top of their +1 to damage (5.2), they roll the same reading again. If they roll another critical, they add another +1, and so on. The damage is still subject to the weapon's and the defender's tags. Approved by the author T120

It is the “unleashed pulp” version of the single damage maths. Don't confuse it with the Reaper of the core, which is a property of the weapon and does not depend on the dice.

Recipes: none of the twenty; it appears in the Rosetta Stone (frantic pulp action).

Inverse mystic grid

To know is to lose your mind

In this module, forbidden knowledge hurts. Every time a character learns or uses forbidden lore (Occultism, any skill in the Arcane sphere), they take Mental Dmg 1 Dodge: None, even if the roll is a success. A critical is a full revelation: Mental Dmg 2. The more you know, the closer you are to being out of the game. Approved by the author T120

Recipes: 12 (“The Deadly Library”: knowledge = Mental damage).

Destiny (Wyrd)

Your death is written

When creating the character, the player writes down how and when they will die (a narrative condition, not a date: “when I see the sea of my childhood again”). Until that moment comes, the character rolls their survival actions with the Epic card, even if the table is playing with another one. When the Wyrd is fulfilled, they switch to the Tragedy recipe until the end of the scene. It is per-character tone (chapter 7) in the service of destiny. Approved by the author T126

The condition is approved by the Game Master. It has to be able to happen in this campaign: “when I see the sea of my childhood again” does not work if the game is never leaving the valley. And the Game Master can trigger it when the story calls for it: destiny does not wait until the player feels like it. T176

Rune magic: magic is paid for in blood. Each rune marks boxes on the caster's Physical track (1 per minor rune, 2 per major rune). Approved by the author

Here death is legitimate because the player wrote it themselves: it is the agreed death that chapter 5 allows.

Recipes: 6 (“Vikings, Snow and Destiny”).

Sleeves (the body as equipment)

Your mind is software

The Physical track and the Grid level of the Physical sphere belong to the sleeve (the body), which is noted on the sheet as equipment, with its own stat block. The skills, the Mental track and the Spiritual track belong to the character. T126

  • Changing sleeves causes Spiritual Dmg 1 Dodge: None Soak: None. Here the Mental track is called Identity (ED-39: there is no Arcane, so there is no Spiritual bar). Emptying it never retires the sheet. What waits on the other side is therapy —mind-dancing, reconstruction, time— and you come back as a very different character, which is far better table material than a gap. Severe cases are not played as protagonists unless expressly agreed, and losing the sheet only happens in short games where that ending was agreed from the start. Author’s decision ED-R2
  • If the sleeve is taken out of the fight, the mind is uploaded to the cloud and wakes up in a new sleeve (if there is a backup and a connection). The jump costs the same damage to Identity.

ED-R2 The draft says that changing bodies “costs Sanity (Identity damage)”. In the core, sanity is the Mental track, but the consequence the author describes (forgetting who you are and becoming an NPC) is the collapse of the Spiritual track. It is applied to the Spiritual track, renamed Identity.

Recipes: 9 (“Backing Up Your Soul”).

Clones

Six lives

Each character starts with 6 clones, which are marked on the sheet. When they are taken out of the fight, the table may declare that clone dead: the next one arrives, with the same sheet and full health, usually bringing an unconvincing explanation. Once the sixth is spent, the character leaves the game. Draft

Here death is a running gag agreed by the table. It does not contradict the pillar “nobody dies because of one bad roll”: in this genre, dying is part of the humour.

Recipes: 19 (“The Hilarious Dystopia”).

The GM rolls in secret

Behind the screen

The Game Master makes all risky rolls behind the screen, the players' ones included, and announces only the narrative outcome. In the extreme variant, they may lie about what came up. Draft

It fits with the black box from chapter 7: the player declares and the Game Master narrates. Use it only if the table agrees to it in session zero: the gentlemen's agreement binds the Game Master too.

Recipes: 19 (“The Hilarious Dystopia”).

Transformation

Levelling up by screaming

During a fight, the character transforms and goes up (usually in the Physical sphere). Like any temporary modifier (4.4), the transformation always has the same end condition: it ends when the scene ends. No variants, no counters, no spending boxes per turn: what the genre wants is for it to last exactly as long as the fight. Author’s decision T116

Transforming is never free. The ability to transform is paid for like whatever it raises. If it raises a whole sphere, it is Grid: 5·x² Keys, x being the levels it rises. If it raises loose Disciplines, at will it is the full purchase, against everything (idea 5, 2): 2·x² per Discipline, and never more than the sphere’s Grid, 5·x². With important limitations —only under a full moon, no control, it costs you health…— it goes Limited (3.5): half, rounded up; per Discipline, x². One Grid level across the whole Physical sphere, from 1 to 2, at will, is 5 Keys; the werewolf who only turns under a full moon and cannot control it goes Limited and pays 3. T176 T183

If the Game Master also changes the damage zoom mid-fight, the transformation combines with Synchronisation.

Recipes: 17 (“The Martial Arts Tournament (Now With Screaming)”).

Synchronisation

It is a core rule (2.4): when piloting a mecha, wearing an exoskeleton or merging with something, the character inherits its level, and the Game Master can change the damage zoom mid-session (the fragile pilot on Lethal climbs into the robot on War, without changing sheets). It is listed in the chest only as a reminder that it exists. T36 T183

Recipes: 18 (“Giant Robots and Teenage Angst”). T176

Environmental damage

The world hurts you

A hostile environment (radiation, extreme cold, vacuum, gas) inflicts damage for each scene of exposure, with no attack roll, and the damage rises the longer you stay: Dmg 1 the first scene, Dmg 2 the second, The Reaper · Grid 2 the third —staying there takes you out of the fight, it never kills you—, always Dodge: None Soak: None. Grid 2 is the usual; vacuum or the heart of a reactor carry their own, higher. The counter resets the moment you get out. The right protection (suit, shelter, antidote) works as narrative permission: with it, the damage can be soaked or disappears. Approved by the author T127 T176

For the extreme case (“if the helmet cracks, everyone dies”), the core already has the special tag Absolute Damage.

Recipes: 10 (“Scrap, Petrol and Rust”).

Custom tracks (Honour, Reputation and others)

A track for what matters most

The core has three tracks (Physical, Mental, Spiritual) and allows a campaign to create a custom track or rename an existing one (5.5). They all work the same way: blocks, boxes, thresholds and consequences, and they come back whole when you are safe (5.4). T181 T183

  • Honour (custom track): takes Social Dmg N. Reaching 0 is final disgrace. Recipe 4.
  • Reputation / City (custom track): measures what the hero really has at stake. Recipe 16. T176
  • Thirst (renamed Mental track): the vampire’s craving, played as withdrawal. Recipe 13.
  • Shadow (renamed Spiritual track): the corruption evil leaves on those who fight it. Recipe 3.
  • Corruption (renamed Spiritual track) in grimdark; Humanity and Identity (renamed Mental track) in cyberpunk and digital minds, because there the loss is cognitive and not supernatural. Recipes 2, 8, 9.

The names come from the author and the recipes; the specific uses of each track are settled in the problem tables. T119

Bonds

The heart as a Key

Each character notes 2 or 3 bonds: people who matter to them, and always non-player characters —the sister, the neighbour, the old master—, never another PC. A bond is useful for two things: T176

  • As a Key (2.3). When the character acts to protect or support a bond present in the scene, they can affect a higher-level threat. Friendship is the narrative permission.
  • As a shield. They can divert damage onto a bond, which suffers in the fiction: an argument, an abandonment, a wound. Personal life is sacrificed to save the rest.

Which bar a bond can shield is decided by the universe. By default, the Mental one. In the gothic, the Spiritual one as well: holding on to somebody who loves you is exactly what holds the Beast back, which is why bonds are half a character sheet there. Author’s decision

Recipes: 14 (“Kids on bikes vs the Government”).

Watch out for the name clash: “Bonds” is also one of the four Disciplines proposed for Ritual (8.2). If both are confirmed, one of them will need renaming.

Problem tables

Several recipes in 8.4 point at these tables, so here they are. They are not a system, they are examples: rolls for when the Director wants the blow to leave something behind and cannot think what. None is compulsory, all of them can be read instead of rolled, and half of them should not be at your table: each one says which genre it is for.

The rule that covers all of them: a problem is not a number, it is something to do. If what comes up gives you nothing tonight, roll again or take the one next to it. Bars with a name of their own (and which one each falls under)

Almost every genre renames a bar: Corruption, Humanity, Shadow, Empathy, Honour, Blood. That is good, and it is what makes a sheet smell of its world. But before naming it you have to know which bar it hangs from, because that decides how it heals, what it penalises and what happens when it empties.

The genre gives the name; the cause gives the bar

  • Body if what wears you down is physical: wounds, exhaustion, hunger, poison, cold.
  • Mind if what wears you down is cognitive: sanity, stress, will, fear, pressure. What breaks is your head, and nobody else lives in there.
  • Spirit if what wears you down is supernatural: something gets in, something stays, something claims you. What breaks is whatever ties you to being yourself, and it usually has a will of its own.

The example that teaches the whole thing is Humanity, which turns up with the same name and almost the same effect in two genres and does not fall under the same bar:

GenreHow Humanity is lostBar
Gothic (vampires)There is a Beast inside you, and every time you give in it gains ground. It is someone who is not youSpirit

Same word, almost the same effect, different bar. And the consequences really do differ: the vampire’s cracks heal with rituals, redemption and arcs, the chrome job’s with rest, support and therapy. The catalogue of Signatures

Principles.

  • Make it up. The Signature is an open list. The catalogue is an aid, not a limit. T104
  • Broad. A Signature looks like a skill from other games: Firearms, Melee weapons. It is not a specific model or a tiny technique: better “Firearms” than “Sniper”, and better “Pistols” than “Colt 45”. T98 T100
  • Within its Discipline. Every Signature hangs logically from a Discipline and requires having bought that Discipline and its Domain.
  • Only in specific things. You can only be an Expert or Grand Master in something specific. The rest of the Discipline is still rolled at +2. T111
  • Golden rule. To be a Grand Master (+4) at something, you must be able to explain exactly what you are doing.
  • Look up by branch. The catalogue is organised so you only look at your own branch: within Mental → Erudition → Sciences you read a short list, not the full one. T104

The tasting menu

Twenty flavours. Each recipe emulates the lore of one of the best-known roleplaying games and explains which NOGUM pieces you need to move for the table to feel it. Principles: T119 T123 T127

  • Titles full of technical sarcasm. Each recipe has a parody name that nods to the trope or the rules of the original. This rulebook is for Game Masters who know their stuff.
  • Sheet as an image. In the final layout, each recipe is an at-a-glance visual card: the lore, the fix and the pieces.
  • Meta column. Each recipe has its own down-to-earth comments in the margin.
  • Listed by fame, not by theme. The twenty are the best-known games, not a forced split of four per block. T124

Each recipe has the lore (what the world is about) and the fix (what to tweak), both from the author's draft. Below them, when needed, an In canon line translates the fix into specific pieces of the rulebook.

The references no longer name anybody: each recipe is recognised by its parody title, its lore and its hint line. If you cannot place one at first glance, the recipe is badly written, not badly censored.

Every recipe carries its Grid ceilings (2.4): how far a character can grow in each sphere in that universe, however long the campaign runs. The starting level is set by the table; the ceiling is set by the genre, and it is what makes a world feel like that world. The Key is the agreed exception to the ceiling, and legendary NPCs can sit above it: a player ceiling is not a world ceiling.

Block A · Fantasy (swords and sorcery)

1. “DUNGEON HOMICIDE MANAGEMENT” · “CLASSES, RACES AND COMBOS”

Ref: The dungeon and the steppe

The lore: A band of misfits breaks into someone else's home, kills the occupants and walks off with the furniture.

The fix: High Optimism. Style Rule to clear rooms fast. Artillery magic (reliable).

In canon: Epic tone (the reference recipe from chapter 1: best of 2d10, TN 6, critical on any 10 and fumble on a 1). If you want clearing the room to come at a price now and then, Unleashed Pulp: the same dice, but with success at a cost and a wide fumble band. Damage zoom: Tactical (9 boxes). Goblins and skeletons below the PCs' level: Style Rule (4.5). Magic is just another skill (Arcane → Gift → Projection) with a weapon stat block, for example Dmg 2 Dodge: Partial.

Grid ceilings (Phy · Men · Soc · Tec · Arc): 3 · 2 · 3 · 2 · 4. The archmage reaches siege magic; the fighter, clearing a district.

2. “MUD, RATS AND HERESY” · “THEY HEALED MY ARM AND NOW I AM A PSYCHOPATH”

Ref: Mud-and-plague fantasy

The lore: The world is horrible, you are poor and you will probably die of dysentery before any orc gets to you.

The fix: Low Optimism. Diseases and Madness table switched on. Magic corrupts (Spiritual damage).

In canon: Nightmare (Worst of 2d10). Chest module: diseases and madness table. The Spiritual track is called Corruption; each use of the Arcane sphere deals Spiritual Dmg 1 to the caster Approved by the author. If Corruption empties, the character becomes an NPC.

3. “THE MELANCHOLY STROLL”

Ref: The epic of the shire

The lore: The journey matters more than the destination. We sing sad songs and smoke pipes while evil advances.

The fix: “Shadow” Spiritual track. Travel Clocks. Combat is secondary; spiritual endurance is key.

In canon: the draft’s “Shadow Mesh” is neither the Quadrant nor the Power Grid: it is the Spiritual track renamed Shadow. Shadow attacks carry Spiritual Dmg N Soak: Willpower. Ref: The court and the katana

The lore: Samurai for whom a breach of social protocol is worse than death.

The fix: Social damage (Honour) kills just as surely as Physical damage. If your Honour drops to 0, you must commit seppuku (delete your sheet).

In canon: custom Honour track (5.5 and the chest). Snubs and breaches of etiquette are weapons with Social Dmg N Soak: Reputation. 5. “SWASHBUCKLING MELODRAMA”

Ref: Pirates with panache

The lore: Pirates swinging from chandeliers. Physics doesn't exist, only panache.

The fix: Maximum Optimism. You don't roll to see whether you hit, you roll to see how good you look doing it.

In canon: Epic. The PCs start with Physical 2 on the Grid, and guards and thugs are level 1. Because of the power gap (4.5), nearly all opposition is handled with the Style Rule: it is taken for granted that you win, and Style decides how. Only nemeses (same level) force a roll to hit.

6. “VIKINGS, SNOW AND DESTINY”

The lore: It's cold, the chainmail is heavy and the Gods are giving you dirty looks. Magic means Runes paid for in blood.

The fix: Destiny (Wyrd) mechanic. You define your death when creating your PC; until that moment, you are hard to kill. Magic costs Physical Health points.

In canon: Destiny (Wyrd) chest module: Epic to survive until your moment comes and Tragedy when it does (Low Chaos: destiny is written). Mundane opposition uses Lethal (high lethality). Runes mark boxes on the Physical track. Recommended Grid ceiling: level 2.

Critique-and-improvement recipe: “High tech, low hope, wrong dice”

Some real adaptations emulated a lore with the wrong tool. These sheets say, bluntly, how it should have been done. T122 T123

“HIGH TECH, LOW HOPE, WRONG DICE”

Ref: the classic chrome-and-rent rulebooks · Alias: the city that charges you for breathing

The diagnosis: the genre promises one thing —that every job comes off almost right and the bill arrives later— and the engine delivers another: a lethal, realistic simulation where the plan either works or it does not, and if it does not, you die. With those rules the table learns fast that the profitable move is not to take risks, and a chrome game where nobody takes risks is a bookkeeping game. The flaw is not the lethality: it is that lethality without “yes, but” turns the genre into a scene-avoidance simulator.

The NOGUM fix: this calls for Black comedy, not Realist. The red die with success at a cost and wide bands gives exactly what the genre wants: the job comes off, and it comes off with a debt, a witness, a failing implant or a body that was not in the plan. Lethality is not removed, it is moved: it goes to the zoom (Lethal, one blow decides) and to the tags, not to the odds of hitting.

Golden rule, the bill: in this recipe the Director cannot say “nothing happens”. Every success at a cost leaves something written on the sheet or on the city, and three sessions in, the characters no longer decide by what they want but by what they owe. That is cyberpunk.

In canon: Black comedy, Lethal zoom, the Mental bar called Humanity (ED-39), the menu of the four costs (5.6) as a list of bills, and equipment with its three locks (4.3), which is where the genre lives: wanting the railgun and not being able to pay for it is the plot.

Grid ceilings (Phy · Men · Soc · Tec · Arc): 2 · 1 · 4 · 2 · —. Not the same as recipe 8 (2 · 1 · 1 · 1): body and head stay where they were, but here Social reaches 4 and Technical 2, because with the engine swapped what scales is debts, favours and machines, not chrome. T170

And the other adaptation made with the wrong tool is no longer here: the world on the back of chelonians is now recipe 20, with its diagnosis intact, because besides being a critique it is one of the twenty most played.

That rulebook gives itself away with its three six-sided dice and tables that all but demand square roots. The other critique-and-improve sheet is the cyberpunk one, right here. Two is enough: any more diagnosis sheets would turn the appendix into a settling of scores. T121

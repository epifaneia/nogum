NOGUM Accelerated · Immediate Immersion Protocol

Play now. Ask yourself why it works later.

Editorial note. This rulebook follows the author's final outline. The “6 game cartridges” and the “difficulty almost always 11” from an earlier version are replaced by four tone modes and a single TN (6). T76 T77 T135

This is a complete game, not a summary. It is also a giant advert: at the end of each rule you will find a question. If one of them gets under your skin, the Complete Game is waiting for you. If not, just play.

0 · Start-up

0.1 The promise

You will be playing in ten minutes, without reading two hundred pages. There is no theory and no explanation here: only rules that work. With them you can play in your favourite universe, whichever it is.

0.2 What you need

  • Two ten-sided dice of different colours: one red and one blue. One pair per player, or one pair for the whole table.
  • One character sheet per player (at the end of this rulebook).
  • A Game Master (GM) who has read these pages. The players do not need to read them.
  • Friends who want to tell a story together.

0.3 The black box

The players do not need to know the rules. The deal is simple:

You declare your action and roll. I tell you what happens.

The GM translates the numbers into story. Nobody at the table has to hear the word “modifier” if they don't want to.

Does it feel like the GM gets a lot of the work? They do. It's a feature, not a bug. T72

1.3 Your game mode

Cross your two answers on the table:

Optimism \ ChaosLittleNormalA lot
A lotOptimisticOptimisticOptimistic
NormalNeutralNeutralChaos ED-A1
LittleTerrorTerrorTerror

Optimism picks the mode and chaos decides one box. There are four, and each changes one thing and one thing only:

  • Optimistic changes the die: you roll two and keep the higher.
  • Neutral changes nothing. It is the reference.
  • Chaos changes what the result is made of: same die, wider bands and successes that charge a toll.
  • Terror changes what you can take: the same die as Neutral, but three health boxes instead of nine.

Look hard at the third one, because it is the strangest and the most important thing in this book: Chaos and Neutral bring exactly the same number of rolls home, 50 % with the sheet at zero and 70 % with the sheet at +2. They are identical up to +3; at +4 they drift slightly apart, because in Chaos a 2 on the red is still a fumble: 80 % against 90 %. Chaos does not make you fail more. It changes what happens to you is made of: where Neutral deals clean successes and clean failures, Chaos deals fumbles, criticals and wins that come with an invoice.

And the fourth is the other way to hurt. In Terror the world is not crueller: you are more fragile. You roll as in Neutral and you hit just as often. What changes is that a knife kills you. The Complete Game calls that fractal health and leaves it in your hands; here it comes preset, so you can watch it work without having to decide anything.

ED-A1. The author set the four modes, their readings, and that the 3 health boxes are anchored to Terror rather than being a switch. The crossing table is an editorial decision: high optimism = Optimistic with any chaos; normal optimism + a lot of chaos = Chaos; low optimism = Terror with any chaos.

Missing a tone? A world that is Swiss clockwork and cruel at the same time? A game of Russian roulette where a 9 is already a miracle? Dark and chaotic at once? These four cover roughly everything and are enough to start with. And they are not a separate invention: all four exist in the Complete Game —Optimistic is Epic, Neutral is First Gear, Chaos is Black comedy and Terror is First Gear with the short health track—. By the time they feel small, you already know four of the seven cards. T135

2 · Universal rules

2.1 The single roll

Always roll both dice, the red one and the blue one. What changes is how you read them:

ModeWhich die countsFumbleCriticalResult
OptimisticThe higher of the twoDouble 1A 10 on eitherTotal 6 or more: success
NeutralOnly the red one (the blue one doesn't count)1 on the red10 on the redTotal 6 or more: success
ChaosRed die only (the blue one does not count)A 1 or 2 on the redA 9 or 10 on the redTotal 5 or less: failure · 6 or 7: partial success · 8 or more: success ED-15
TerrorRed die only (the blue one does not count)A 1 on the redA 10 on the redTotal 6 or more: success · and only 3 health boxes (2.7)

How to roll

  1. Read the die the mode tells you to.
  2. Add your bonus (2.2). That is the total.
  3. Compare it with TN 6. Always the same one.
  4. Fumbles and criticals are read on the dice, before adding anything. A fumble is always a disaster and a critical is always something extraordinary, whatever your bonus.

Partial success (Chaos only). You pull it off, but you pay a price. The GM picks one:

  • Time: you manage it, but late; the guards are already in the corridor.
  • Resource: you manage it, but something breaks; the bat splinters.
  • Position: you manage it, but you are left exposed; you fall flat on your back on the other side of the wall.
  • Friendly fire: you manage it, but someone else gets caught in it; the explosion takes out the only radio.

Never “you pull it off, but you lose a health box”. That is laziness.

ED-15. The Chaos mode bands are exactly those of the Black comedy card in the Complete Game: red die, fumble on 1 or 2, critical on 9 or 10, and success at a cost taking the TN and the point above it (total 6 or 7). It never turns a failure into a success; it only charges a toll on what had already worked out. Consequence: in Chaos a professional (+2) works it out 70 % of the time —the same as in Neutral— but two out of every seven of those times paying a price. All four readings come from the Complete Game: Epic, First Gear, Black comedy, and First Gear with the short health track.

Same character (+2), same task. In Optimistic they pull it off 91 % of the time. In Neutral, 70 %. In Chaos, also 70 % —you read that right, the same number— but two out of every seven of those times paying a price, and with twice the fumbles and twice the criticals. In Terror, 70 % again: what changes is that two hits now put them out. You have not touched the sheet once: you have changed the atmosphere three times in three different ways.

2.2 Bonuses

Your bonus comes from your sheet and from the situation. Your sheet only gives five possible values:

BonusWho you are at this
−2Null. You haven't put a single point into that whole sphere.
−1Clumsy. You have the sphere, but that column is empty: you are on the wrong foot.
+1I get by. You have a broad skill that covers it.
+2It's my job. You have a specific skill that covers it.
+4Grand Master. Nobody does it better.

Difficulty. If the task is easy, +2. If it is hard, −2. If it is heroic, −4. Normal is 0. Difficulty is added to the bonus; the TN never moves.

Limits. Add up skill, difficulty and any other modifier. If the total drops below −2, the GM chooses: either roll at −2 or narrate the failure without rolling. If it goes above +4, the GM chooses: either roll at +4 or narrate the success without rolling. The final bonus is always between −2 and +4.

Don't roll for the sake of it. Roll only if every possible result is interesting. If failing adds nothing, it just happens.

2.3 Skills

Every character has five spheres: Physical, Mental, Social, Technical and Arcane (magic, the Force, psychic powers; if your world doesn't have them, ignore it). Spheres are not skills: they are the drawers you keep them in.

  • A column is each of the two broad skills of a sphere, with its specific skills underneath: in Physical, Dexterity is one column and Vigour the other.

  • In addition, every character has Style +2, a gift that sits outside the spheres (2.8).

2.4 The Grid: how big your feats are

The bonus says how well you do something. The Grid says at what scale you do it. Each character has one level per sphere. Ask yourself: if this gets out of hand, what makes the news?

LevelScaleExamples
1One personAny human, however expert: The consultant from Baker Street, a boxer, a hacker.
2A room, hundreds of peopleAn exceptional human, only if your universe allows it: the barbarian of the steppe, the perfect soldier. A rank-and-file soldier stands no chance against them.
3A building, a neighbourhoodBringing down a castle, cutting the power grid of a skyscraper.
4A big cityA mystic shield over the city, a monster razing districts.
5A countryChanging the geography. Only for campaign finales.
6CosmicNo rolling: it's scenery. The GM narrates it.

Each level multiplies your reach by a hundred: ×1 one person, ×100 a room, ×10,000 a building, ×1,000,000 a city, ×100,000,000 a country. When someone has a level on you they are not better than you: they are playing with two more zeros, and that is why there is no roll.

If you don't know which level to give, humans are level 1 in everything. What is measured is tools and scale, not intelligence or muscle: a brilliant chemist is still level 1.

The power gap

It works for everyone: players and adversaries alike. Compare the Grid level of whoever acts with the level of whatever they act against, in the sphere of the action. An NPC or a monster has a single level for everything, unless the GM says otherwise.

  • If you are at the same level, roll as normal: attack, defence, everything.
  • If you are above (even by one level), you don't roll to succeed: you succeed. If it matters how, roll Style (+2, 2.8). That also goes for a monster attacking characters below it: the blow lands without a roll, with its weapon's damage.
  • If you are below, you cannot affect what is above you —not even a scratch— without a Key. That includes defending yourself: against an attack that comes from above there is no defence roll, unless you have a Key that applies. With a Key you roll everything as normal, with your full bonus.

The Key. A Key is a narrative reason that lets you affect something of a higher level: a bazooka, the stone from his own world, a stolen admin password, a video of the bribe, twenty mercenaries firing at once. Each Key raises you one Grid level against its target. With a Key you roll as normal, with your full bonus and no penalties. Getting one is often an adventure in itself.

Keys are bought

  • At creation, the separate pot gives you 5 Keys, or +1 Grid level in one sphere. By default, every character starts at level 1 in everything; if you pick the +1, it can go in any sphere your universe's ceiling allows. It does not trade for skill points: everyone turns up with a trade.
  • You can start with more points if the table chooses it consciously, knowing it goes against the idea of this rulebook.
  • Each Key is one specific skill for ever (“Weapons against vampires”) or a whole sphere for one scene, if it is very specific. Never a Signature. You choose when you buy it, and you write down its target.
  • Grid and Keys trade: one Grid level in a sphere is worth 5 Keys, its eight specific skills for the price of five. If the story raises the table’s Grid and you would rather not rise, you take those 5 Keys.
  • One Key puts you one Grid level above. To climb further against the same thing, the count is squared: rising n levels costs n² Keys in total (1, 4, 9). With five in the pot, nobody starts more than two levels above.
  • During the campaign the Director hands out one Key per session. That tap is the game’s power dial: Keys also buy the equipment that breaks the Grid. Only that: normal equipment costs money and nothing else (2.9).
  • And what the Director gives you for a good idea —“it occurs to you to use the holy water”— is a lent Key: it lasts one scene; it goes on the sheet in pencil and is rubbed out when the scene ends.

Why can the vigilante millionaire stand up to the alien in tights and trunks with the same bonus as a nightclub bouncer? Because of the Key. The Complete Game has a whole chapter bringing the Grid down to earth, with pop-culture examples in all five spheres. NOGUM is waiting for you.

2.5 Initiative

When combat starts, each participant rolls with the active mode and adds their best Dexterity bonus (the broad skill or any of their specific skills under it). Act from highest to lowest total. On a tie, the GM decides.

If your character is going to spend their turn deducing a clue, hacking a terminal or intimidating the rival boss… does it make sense for their physical speed to decide the order? Want an initiative where you first choose your intent (violence, trick or specialty) and then how you carry it out? NOGUM is waiting for you. T135

2.6 Combat

Combat is an aggressive negotiation. Each attack is resolved in two steps.

Step 1 · Impact (the attacker rolls)

Roll your attack skill against TN 6. The defender's circumstances (cover, fog, size) change the difficulty. The defender's skills don't count here.

  • Fumble: you don't connect and something goes wrong (you disarm yourself, hit an ally…).
  • Failure: the blow goes astray.
  • Success: you connect. The weapon's damage is in the air.
  • Critical: you hit a vital spot: +1 damage.

Step 2 · Mitigation (the defender rolls)

If the weapon allows it (check its tags), the defender makes a single reaction: dodge (Dexterity → Acrobatics) or soak (Vigour → Fortitude), depending on which route the weapon leaves open.

The defender wins ties. You both roll against the same TN and compare the result level: fumble < failure < success at a cost < success < critical.

  • The defender matches or beats the attacker’s result level: no damage.
  • The attacker ends up above and the defender got a success or a success at a cost: weapon damage −1.
  • The defender fails: takes all the damage.
  • The defender fumbles: the attack goes up one result level on that scale, and then you compare.

Final damage = weapon damage (+1 if the attacker rolled a critical) − the defender's reduction, within the limits set by the tags.

Tags override the tie. With Dodge: Partial you eat at least 1 even when you match it, and with Dodge: None there is no reaction to roll.

In Chaos, a partial success in combat is a success at a cost: it connects or defends like a success, but in the comparison it counts as one result level lower, and the GM adds a cost from the menu (the attack connects, but you lose your weapon; you dodge, but you hit the floor). ED-15

2.7 Health

Every character has 9 health boxes, in three blocks of three. Each point of damage crosses off one box.

Except in Terror

In Terror mode there are not nine: there are 3 boxes, a single block. Fill it and you are out of the fight. With the damage in this book that means two serious hits take you off the scene, and that one is enough if the attacker rolls a critical and you don’t stop it.

It is not a switch you have to remember to turn on. It comes with the mode, and it is the one piece of this book that lets you look in on what the Complete Game calls fractal health: there, lethality is a dial and you turn it, with three, nine or twenty-seven boxes.

BlockBoxesWhen filled
First1–3−2 to actions the wound gets in the way of (a swollen eye, a broken hand).
Second4–6−4 (replaces the −2) and you lose complex actions (a useless leg, a haemorrhage).
Third7–9Out of the fight.
  • Out of the fight is not death. You are incapacitated and take no part. Whether you wake up is decided by the story or by your player, never by the dice. In NOGUM nobody dies because of a bad roll.
  • Not even the Reaper kills. Whoever it hits below its level is left out of the fight. Death, madness or transformation only happen if they are agreed in the story.
  • Boxes are cleared when combat ends, but the wounds remain: the penalty of the block you filled stays until you heal (hours or days for the first, days or weeks for the second).
  • Penalties respect the limits in 2.2: you never go below −2.

Or in a story where a glance into the abyss hurts as much as a gunshot? 2.8 Mooks and Style

The Style Rule

Every character has Style +2. When you face enemies below your level on the Grid, or mere mooks (the scenery of the scene), you don't roll to see if you win: it's a given. You roll with the active mode and add your Style to see how you win:

  • Fumble: you win, but you make a fool of yourself. If your character takes themselves very seriously, it may sting.
  • Failure (and, in Chaos, partial success): you win, but messily or without shining.
  • Success: you win in style, clean and spectacular.
  • Critical: legendary. Film-grade choreography, several go down at once and rumours are born.

Mooks don't get health boxes tracked.

Style suits anime, wuxia, space opera and action films. It is not recommended for very serious universes: in a realistic war, even the lowliest soldier rolls.

Why does the hero sweep aside twenty thugs and then sweat against a single one? In the Complete Game you'll see how the Grid, the Key and Style share the work, and how to make enemy sheets in ten seconds. NOGUM is waiting for you.

2.9 Equipment: what you can afford

Here normal equipment costs money and nothing else, and money is your bonus in Resources (Social → Hierarchy → Resources). No points, no Keys: if your drawer reaches, you have it. Only equipment that breaks the Grid —the kind that lets you affect something of a higher level— also costs Keys (2.4).

  • One step above, once per arc. You can get hold of something out of your league, but it is never free: a favour that will be called in, a debt, a trail, somebody who now knows your name.
  • Stolen or borrowed gear works for a whole scene. Afterwards you decide whether to keep it, and then it does have to fit in your drawer.
  • The weapon does not make you better, it does something else. What changes are its tags: how much damage it does, whether it can be dodged and whether armour helps (2.6).

In the complete rulebook equipment has a whole economy behind it: what makes you better is paid for with skill points and what lets you do the impossible is paid for with Keys, and there is a workshop for pricing anything you invent. Here money is enough. NOGUM is waiting.

Improvising an enemy in ten seconds. Ask yourself whether it matters. If it doesn't, it's scenery: Style. 4 · Want to break the game?

You now have everything you need to play in your own universe.

But you will have noticed the limits. Here the tone is fixed: if you start in Optimistic, you play in Optimistic all afternoon. What if you want the adventure to start as a bright fantasy and end as a nightmare? For the final boss scene to change physics mid-fight? For one particular character to live in their own tone?

In NOGUM you learn why each rule in this rulebook works, and with that:

  • Seven tone cards and the panel to shift them scene by scene.
  • Fractal Health: three hits, nine boxes or twenty-seven points, with the same sheet.
  • Intent-based initiative: violence, trick or specialty.
  • The mental and spiritual damage tracks, social combat and the consequence menu.
  • The full list of skills and the catalogue of Signatures.
  • The Rules Chest, the Multiverse Bazaar and twenty recipes to emulate the most famous universes in roleplaying.

Try it, tinker with it and, if you like it and it raises questions, come back. If you want to see examples of how to emulate universes with this engine, the Complete Game is waiting for you. T76 T135

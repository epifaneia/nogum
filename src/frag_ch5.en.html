Chapter 5 · Danger (Violence and consequences)

Combat carries special weight in every roleplaying rulebook, and NOGUM is no different. There are a few differences from normal rolls that deserve a chapter of their own.

NOGUM is a universal system, but always narrative. Combat is simple and narrative on purpose. There will be no optional modes to make it more granular or tactical: that change would not alter the tone or the universe, you would only be moving the game along the narrative–tactical axis, and this rulebook is philosophically anchored on the narrative side. It is recommended that every NPC who is going to interact has a sheet, or at least the few brushstrokes needed for the handful of rolls that will be required. Author’s combat notes

5.1 Initiative: who attacks first?

Getting rid of attributes raised a question: what is the most cinematic way to hand out turns in a dangerous situation? NOGUM starts from an idea that feels deeply intuitive: speed comes from mastery. The gunslinger draws fast because they have done it ten thousand times; the hacker types fast because they are a master. By default, every skill can be used to roll initiative.

If we are only deciding who goes first, what do you roll? Do you have to decide your action beforehand? One of NOGUM’s pillars is understanding roleplaying as fluid improvisation, so anything that invites slowing the game down is strongly discouraged. The action is not chosen in advance. The intention is.

The three guards

  1. The GM asks: “The situation explodes. What is your guard?”
  2. Each player declares one of these three (not exactly what they will do, but how they will tackle the problem) and rolls their best skill that fits it:
    Violence
    You go in head-on. You roll your highest-rated attack skill. When your turn comes, you can use a different skill, as long as it is still direct and destructive.
    Trick
    You have a bright idea: you deceive, hide, use the terrain to your advantage, build a gadget or pull one out of your magic pocket. You roll Stealth, Deception, Sleight of Hand… Flexibility is encouraged. On your turn you can switch to another skill, as long as it is still sideways and sly.
    Specialty
    You are going to use one specific skill: driving, hacking, healing. “I don’t know exactly what I’ll do, but I’ll do it with the computer.” There is no wiggle room here: whatever you do must fit that skill like a glove.
  3. Actions go from highest to lowest result. On your turn you specify what you do within your guard. Tie: whoever has the higher bonus goes first; if it is still tied, PCs before NPCs. Critical: you act first. Fumble: last. T176

The guard marker. Every sheet carries a marker with the three guards. It is not a rule as such, just a reminder: if the table forgets to choose before acting, the guard on the marker from last time applies. Author's decision, 17 Sep

What if I no longer want to do what I declared? You can switch, but you have lost all your momentum: you mentally prepared for one thing and now you are starting from scratch. Changing guard automatically sends you to the back of the queue for the current round. No additional penalties. T85, T129 ED-8

Slow skills. By default, speed is cinematic: the hacker types as fast as the gunslinger shoots. If that jars in your universe (a hacker needs minutes to launch an attack), that skill carries a negative aspect bought on the sheet: in combat it always goes last or, if it is too slow, cannot even be used within the timeframe of a conflict. Not every specialty is quick. T87

Example. Warrior: Violence with Sword +4 → gets 13 and goes first. Hacker: Specialty with Systems +2 → gets 9. Thief: Trick with Stealth +2 → gets 7. By the time it’s the hacker’s turn, the warrior has already smashed the lights he was planning to switch off, so, without leaving his specialty, he hacks the enemy comms instead.

No fixed initiative. NPCs declare a stance and roll too: it is an opposed roll using the skill of the chosen stance, and mooks carry their two or three basic numbers so it can be settled without a sheet. Author’s decision

5.2 The attack sequence: impact versus mitigation

First of all, the power gap (4.5). If attacker and defender are not at the same Grid level in the sphere of the attack, the one above hits without rolling, with their weapon damage, and the one below does nothing to them and does not defend, unless they have a Key that applies; with it, you play level. T170 T176 T183

To avoid slow arithmetic (subtracting dodges from attacks), NOGUM uses two steps. There is no opposed roll.

Step 1 · Impact (attacker)

The attacker declares their intention and rolls against the TN of the active Tone Card. None of the defender’s skills give bonuses here, but their circumstances do, as difficulty modifiers (4.1): cover, fog, being very small…

  • Fumble: the attack doesn’t connect. It may turn against the attacker; if that makes no narrative sense, it may hit an ally or cause some other adverse effect. End of sequence.
  • Failure: the attack goes wide, hits a wall or the target moves. End of sequence.
  • Success: the attack connects. The damage is “in the air”, travelling towards the target.
  • Critical: it connects in a vital spot: +1 damage. It is the only arithmetic in combat. T129

Step 2 · Mitigation (defender)

The defender can only try to avoid the damage if the weapon’s spec sheet allows it (5.3). They choose one permitted route, dodge or soak, and roll their defence skill against the TN of the active card.

The roll does not subtract points on its own. What counts is its result level —fumble, failure, success at a cost, success or critical—, which is compared with the attacker’s as the box below explains. T170

The defender wins ties ED-25

Both of you rolled against the same TN, so all you have to look at is who got further up. Totals and margins are never compared: result levels are.

FumbleThe attack goes up one step on the active card’s ladder, and then the result levels are compared. A failure becomes a success (or a success at a cost on the cards that carry it), and a success becomes a critical. If that makes it a critical, +1 damage, like any critical. The critical is the ceiling: it goes no higher. T176

Tags override the tie. A weapon with Dodge: Partial leaves you eating at least 1 point even when you match it, because it is an area, a burst or fire: it cannot be fully avoided. And with Dodge: None there is no reaction to roll at all. That is the difference between a duel and a gunfight.

Defence at a cost (on the cards that carry it): it reduces like a success, −1, and the GM also charges a price from the cost menu. You walk away alive, but you drop the weapon, lose the position or end up where you did not want to be.

Double fumble: the attacker’s fumble rules. The blow does not connect anyway —the defender’s only bumps it up to a failure— and both of them look ridiculous in front of whoever was watching.

The fumble menu

A fumble is an ugly failure: it does not work, and something else happens too. How much it hurts is up to the table and the tone, but so that you are not improvising from scratch on one roll in five on the hard cards, here are six doors:

  1. A route closes. The way you were using is no longer available: the lock jams, the contact stops trusting you, the bridge gives way.
  2. Something spends that does not come back. The last charge, the last vial, the favour they owed you.
  3. You leave a trace. Someone will know you were there, and not in the good way.
  4. The threat takes a step closer. The clock ticks on, the patrol turns the corner, the ritual reaches its next verse.
  5. What you are carrying breaks. The weapon, the tool, the radio. Not always for good.
  6. Someone notices you. And now you have a problem with a name.

In the predictable column the fumble closes a door: the path you tried is gone. In the chaotic one it opens a branch: something new turns up that nobody had put there. Same frequency, different size.

Optional rule · The monster does not tremble

The card says how the table feels with the dice in hand. But the adversary is not holding them. If a horror fumbles and misses like a frightened protagonist, the fear goes flat: on Nightmare, a monster at +2 misses half its attacks (51 %) and fumbles almost one in five (19 %).

A skilled Director can have an adversary with a name —the nemesis, the thing in the cellar— make all its rolls on First Gear: red die only, TN 6. At the same +2 it hits 70 % of the time and fumbles 10 %.

  • Always towards the centre. Never to the opposite extreme: a monster on Epic hits 91 % and almost never fumbles, and that feels unfair.
  • Named ones only. Mooks roll on the table’s card: a cultist dropping his dagger is horror too.
  • Out loud. “This one rolls on First Gear.” NOGUM exists to declare what used to be done in secret; changing it behind the screen breaks the deal.

Use it with great care. Oppression already brings frustration, and being frustrated against someone who does not even miss can multiply it. And with Lethal health (three blocks), a monster that hits 70 % instead of 49 % takes people out of the fight a good deal faster. Author’s decision · T169

Damage taken = weapon damage (+1 if the attack ends up critical, even through the defender’s fumble): nothing if the defender matches or beats their result level; −1 if the defender succeeded but stayed below; all of it if the defender failed. Always within the limits of the spec sheet. T176

Summary: first we see whether you get hit. Then, whether your armour or your reflexes save your life.

Usual defence skills: Dexterity → Acrobatics to dodge; Vigour → Fortitude (with whatever protection you are wearing) to soak. You always soak with the skill, armour or no armour: the body is the first protection. What decides whether it helps are the weapon’s tags —Soak: Max N and Soak: None, which is where “this goes through your skin” lives— and whatever bonus your protection adds. By default, normal. Author’s decision

Mental, social and spiritual defence, by default (the table can change it to suit the fiction): against the social, dodge with Empathy and soak with Hierarchy; against the mental, dodge with Alertness and soak with Fortitude; against the spiritual, Protection if you have Arcane, and Fortitude if you don’t. T176

Hazards with no attacker. A fall, fire, the environment: nobody rolls the attack. They count as a successful attack of their level, and you defend if the spec sheet allows it. If they are extreme, as a critical. The huge ones carry a Reaper of their level (5.3). They never kill: they take you out of the fight. T176

Horror example

The GM tells you: “The sniper fires. It’s The Reaper · Grid 2 Dodge: None Soak: None.”

Translation: if you are below level 2, there is no roll: you are out of the fight. You don’t roll defence. You don’t roll armour. Pray. T176

Anything without tags is not an attack

The three combat tags only make sense on something that does damage. A torch, a rope or a map carry no Dodge or Soak because they attack nobody; if someone swings the crowbar as a weapon, read it as Dodge: Full Soak: Full, which is the default value of anything that does not say otherwise.

And with the rule that defence wins ties (5.2), these three tags have become the game’s main balance surface: a Dodge: Full weapon is stopped almost every time and a Dodge: None one is never stopped. That is the difference between a long duel and a short gunfight, which is why they are worth choosing carefully when you invent a new weapon.

Full catalogue of weapons, protection, powers and vehicles: 8.3 The Multiverse Bazaar.

The Reaper: what is not a weapon

A weapon’s maximum damage is 2. That is the ceiling, and it is deliberate: 1 and 2 are the damage it makes sense to roll for. A weapon of war is told apart from a knife by its other three tags —no dodging, no soaking, long reach— not by a bigger number.

What lies above that is not a better weapon: it is an event. A siege gun, a car at seventy, a dragon’s breath, a bomb. We call that the Reaper, and it works like the Grid, not like damage:

The Reaper

Every Reaper carries a Grid level, and it is a Key to damage. If it reaches you and you are below that level, there is no damage roll and no soak that helps: you are out of the fight, in whatever way that track implies. Death, madness or transformation only come if they are agreed narratively (ED-12). Whether it can be dodged is what its tag says Dodge: the sniper you cannot see (Dodge: None) cannot be dodged; a grenade, because of its area, can barely be dodged (Dodge: Hard (−2)). Against anyone at its level, it deals Damage 2 with normal defence, and plays like any other blow. Against anyone above it, nothing without a Key: that is the power gap. And it never kills. T170 T176 T181

It is the same old sentence (4.5): below the level it is impossible, and the Key is the agreed exception. Here, what is impossible is staying on your feet.

What cannot be dodged is avoided. A Reaper with Dodge: None is not a combat problem, it is a fiction problem. T181 It is not solved with a better roll; it is solved by not being there, by cutting the fuse, by talking down whoever is holding it, or by climbing the Grid before it arrives. A Director who points a Reaper at a character who cannot take it is not proposing a fight: they are announcing that the character is leaving it. Let that show at the table, and let it be seen coming.

And it exists on all three tracks. The physical Reaper floors you. The mental one takes you out of the scene through your head, and also through your face: there is evidence, and tomorrow you are nobody, because social attacks go to the Mental track (ED-11). The spiritual one takes whatever you carry inside, whatever your world calls it. It does not empty the track: it crosses it out. And whatever comes after is agreed by the table, not by the Reaper.

In the Animated World the Reaper exists too, and it floors you too — for two panels. The anvil falls, the character goes flat, and in the next scene they are whole. What gets crossed out there is their dignity.

The fourth state is not called “dead”

That is no accident. In this rulebook’s philosophy, a protagonist’s death is never down to chance: players own their characters’ lives, and the rules can only take them out of the fight.

Out of the fight means incapacitated: you lose consciousness, or you stay conscious clutching your guts and screaming for help. The only non-negotiable point is that you cannot take part in the fight in any way: not physically, not with powers, not even by giving useful advice. You can scream or crawl, but you are removed from the action scene. Whether you wake up the next day is decided by the campaign’s tone or the player’s wishes. Never by the dice. Author’s combat notes

Out of the fight, you leave the scene. You come back when it ends, and your health follows the usual rule (5.4). T176 T181 T183

A campaign can rename a track or create its own (Honour for samurai, Reputation for superheroes, Empathy in cyberpunk, Blood for vampires).

The genre gives the name; the cause gives the bar

Almost every genre renames a bar, and that is fine: Corruption, Humanity, Shadow, Empathy, Honour, Blood. What cannot be chosen to taste is which bar it hangs from, because healing, penalties and what happens when it empties all depend on that. ED-39

Body if what wears you down is physical. Mind if it is cognitive: your head breaking, and nobody else lives in there. Spirit if it is supernatural: something gets in, something stays, something claims you, and it usually has a will of its own.

The example that teaches the whole thing is Humanity, which with the same name and almost the same effect falls under different bars: in the gothic there is a Beast inside you that gains ground every time you give in, and that is Spirit; in cyberpunk nobody is inside —cyberpsychosis is your own head ceasing to recognise other people—, and that is Mind. Which is why its cracks heal differently (table above). T181 T183

And if there is no Arcane in your world, there is no Spiritual bar: not empty, non-existent, exactly like the Sphere. Whatever would be Spirit elsewhere falls under Mind, and the table agrees whether that bar can retire a character. The full list of genre names, with their tables of what gets lost, is in the problem tables.

Words are weapons

There is no “combat system”; there is a conflict system. The Mental and Spiritual tracks have boxes just like the Physical one, so words also have calibre and lethality. T137–T138

  • Interrogation: five hours of aggressive questioning. The cop rolls Intimidation and, on a hit, deals Mental Dmg 1. When the suspect fills a block, they crack and confess.
  • Horror: watching an abyssal creature devour a friend is a passive attack Mental Dmg 2 Dodge: None, straight to the sanity.
  • Trial or royal court: a lawyer corners a witness by rolling Persuasion or Deception: Mental Dmg 2 (social attacks go to the Mental track, ED-11). If she empties their track, the witness makes a fool of themselves, loses all credibility and is dropped from the case.

And they are stopped like any blow, with the default defences of 5.2. T176 T183

A flaw is the scar of a filled block

Advantages and disadvantages are not bought from any pot (3.6): they happen. And they happen here. When a block of your Mental or Spiritual bar fills up and you later heal, the minus two goes away but something named stays behind. ED-35

That something is the flaw: the insomnia, the debt, the tic, the fear of water, the name you cannot say out loud. It carries no number; it carries consequences. And the day you play it against your own interest when it did not suit you, it is one of the seven things this table rewards.

The gift works the same way from the other side, when the fiction owes you one: the scar that makes you recognisable, the dying man’s favour, the strange calm of someone who has already been there.

Healing

  • Gritty or realistic game: threshold 1 consequences, hours to days; threshold 2, days to weeks; threshold 3, weeks to months. An implant, a potion or a magical bath can skip those timeframes.
  • Battle with no narrative weight for the character: light consequences, with a simple cure. No consequence disappears on its own: only the tracks come back, when you are safe (5.4). What a simple cure means is up to the universe: in the cartoon world, a change of panel; in a hyper-realist one, a whole session in the emergency room. T181 T183
  • Battle tied to the character’s personal story: physical or mental wounds leave permanent or hard-to-heal after-effects, which can only be healed through another significant arc, not through a long or expensive process.

The four-cost menu (partial success)

In modes with partial success (Unleashed Pulp, Black comedy and any high-chaos recipe), the GM should not fall back on “you manage it, but you lose a hit point”. Choose a cost: T137–T138

  1. Time: you manage it, but it takes too long. You open the safe, but the guards are already in the corridor. Roll initiative.
  2. Resource: you manage it, but you break something. You kill the zombie, but the bat splinters and you are left without a weapon.
  3. Position: you manage it, but you are left exposed. You jump the wall, slip and land on your back; the enemy has you in their sights.
  4. Friendly fire: you manage it, but you harm an ally or the group. The grenade kills the monster, but the blast wave destroys the only radio.

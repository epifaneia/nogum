Appendix A · The Engine Room

Here is the engine with the bonnet up: why each card reads the two dice the way it does, how much works out at each bonus, and what it costs to be somebody. To play, 1.3 is enough; this is the why.

Warning: this appendix is technical content. If probabilities bring you out in a rash, you can skip the whole thing and go back to the cards (1.4). The cards work just the same even if you do not know why. This is where you come to look at the engine with the bonnet up.

Every figure in this appendix comes from an exact enumeration of the possible combinations: 10 with one die and 100 with two. They are neither rounded nor simulated. They follow the rules in the book: the fumble and the critical are read on the number you use (the red die, the sum, the best or the worst of two), without bonuses, and take priority over the total ED-1C·t4. Everything else is resolved with reading + total bonus against the TN of the active card.

A.1 The balance point: one die, neutral chaos

What counts as “normal”? In real life, a flat distribution is madness: an expert surgeon who made a howling mistake one time in ten would be pure chaos. Even so, the hobby has spent decades rolling a single linear die (the d20 in the dungeon and the steppe, the d100 in the antediluvian octopuses), and the table feels it as “normal”. NOGUM takes that habit as its starting point. A linear die is Neutral Chaos T14. With 1d10, each face is worth 10%, and a Grand Master (+4) against TN 6 succeeds 90% of the time: they only fail on a natural 1. It is exciting, but not very professional.

A.2 Tools of order: adding dice

When you add dice together, the results pile up in the middle (the central limit theorem, the Gaussian bell curve). With 2d10 added, each extreme (2 or 20) comes up only 1% of the time, while the central value, 11, comes up 10%. Luck loses weight and competence gains it: that is order (Low Chaos).

  • An old-school simulator rolls 3d6 (3–18, mean 10.5). A 10 or an 11 comes up 25% of the time, and a 3 or an 18 barely 0.93%. It is the classic example of a reliable world.
  • The plus-and-minus dice system rolls 4dF (−4 to +4). The 0 comes up 23.46% of the time and the ±4 extremes only 2.47%: the sheet rules over the die.

A.3 Succumbing to chaos

To raise Chaos there are two levers that leave the mean untouched:

A.3.1 Widening the natural ranges
More faces of the die become a fumble or a critical. With 1d10, going from “1 and 10” to “1–2 and 9–10” doubles the extremes: from 20% to 40% of rolls. It only makes sense because the fumble and the critical are natural: the bonus does not touch them. This is what Black Comedy does on the red die. T5
A.3.2 Partial success
A band of results becomes “yes, but…”. It is the heart of the moves systems, which roll 2d6: on 6 or less you fail (41.67%), on 7–9 you get a success at a cost (41.67%) and on 10 or more, a full success (16.67%). This removes the dead end: something always happens. In the dungeon and the steppe the classic equivalent is the critical on a natural 20. This is the success at a cost of Unleashed Pulp and Black Comedy. T3

A.4 The levers of optimism and terror

Optimism: keep the BEST of 2. This is advantage in the dungeon and the steppe, 5th edition, and the bonus dice of the antediluvian octopuses, 7th edition (which keep the better tens die). With 1d20, advantage raises the mean from 10.5 to 13.825 and cuts the fumble from 5% to 0.25%. In NOGUM, with 2d10, the mean rises from 5.5 to 7.15, the chance of either die being a 10 goes from 10% to 19%, and the double 1 stays at 1%.

Terror: keep the WORST of 2. It is the exact mirror image. The mean drops to 3.85, any 1 ruins the roll (19%) and a miracle requires a double 10 (1%). Competence still counts, but panic can betray it.

A.5 The NOGUM solution: two dice of different colours

The whole system fits in two d10s of different colours, one red and one blue (the rule is in 1.3). You always roll both, and the active card says how to read them. There are four readings, and two tools that sit on top of them:

  1. Red only (1d10): Neutral Chaos.
  2. The sum (2–20): order.
  3. The best of the two: optimism.
  4. The worst of the two: terror.
  5. Widened ranges on the red die: high chaos (A.3.1).
  6. Partial success bands on the total: high chaos (A.3.2).

This is how each reading is distributed. The exact figures, value by value, are in Appendix B.

A.6 The anchoring: from −2 to +4

Every sheet runs from −2 (Null) to +4 (Grand Master): that range is the Anchor (2.1). Bonuses stack (skill, difficulty, equipment and effects), but you roll with a number inside the range: if the total goes past +4, the excess goes into attempting something harder and you roll with +4; below −2, the GM decides whether there is a roll and, if there is, you roll with −2 (2.5) C·a1C·a2. That is why the tables in this book can be calculated in full: seven rows per card, from Null (−2) to Grand Master (+4), with the coin toss (+0) in the middle, which no sheet carries as standard but is the total of many rolls. The charts in A.8 draw them, and Appendix B writes them out.

A.7 The five levers

A tone system is built by moving five things, and most arguments about dice get tangled because they mix three different effects. Here they are kept apart: the centre (how often it works out), the spread (how much the die rules over the sheet) and the spectacle (how often something memorable happens). A lever can move one, two or all three.

Lever 1 is diagonal, and that is the finding that puts everything else in order: keeping the best of two is optimistic and orderly at once, because it piles results up at the top. That is why the two-dice readings live in the corners of the ordered column and not in the middle.

And criticals and fumbles do not weigh the same. A good surprise amplifies: the critical goes where you were already going and nobody has to invent anything. A bad surprise branches: the fumble closes the door you were using and forces another one open that was not there. That is why the same lever adds predictability upwards and chaos downwards, and why the worst of two already carries the chaos without needing any further tool. ED-24

The bands apply to the number you use, not to the individual dice, and that is why they lean towards the tone of their cell on their own: with the best of two, widening them brings almost nothing but miracles (4 % fumbles against 36 % criticals) and with the worst almost nothing but disasters (36 % against 4 %). The same tool, a different effect depending on the tone, without a single extra rule.

Success at a cost takes the TN and the point above it. There, and not one point lower: a band one step down would be promoting a failure into a success, which is giving games away, and that is not what the tool it comes from does. Placed like this it does not change how often you get there, only how often you pay. ED-23

The TN stays put on purpose. One number for single-die rolls (6) and another for sums (11), and that is that. How hard or easy each roll is goes into the bonus, as a modifier: +2 easy, 0, −2 hard, −4 heroic (2.5) C·t3. Moving the TN would be the cleanest optimism lever there is, but it forces the table to remember a different number in every scene, and that is exactly the friction this game promises to take away. Only Tragedy touches it, as a declared exception, and it says so out loud (Appendix B) C·22oct.

One lever per column, and the row picks the direction. That is the whole architecture of the quadrant.

What a fumble does

It is not a rules lever: how much it hurts is decided by whoever is narrating, according to the tone and the table. What is fixed is what it is made of. A fumble is failure + complication. A success at a cost is success + complication. The complication can be the same and weigh the same in both cases; what changes is whether you also got what you wanted.

The row decides; the column charges

In each row, the chaotic card works out as often as its companion up to +3. Pulp gives the same 51 · 64 · 75 · 84 · 91 · 96 as the Epic, and at +4 it stays at 96 where the Epic reaches 99; Black Comedy the same 30 · 40 · 50 · 60 · 70 · 80 as First Gear, and at +4 it stays at 80 where First Gear reaches 90; and Nightmare the same 9 · 16 · 25 · 36 · 49 · 64 as Anguish, and at +4 the same 81. The only difference is the price, and at the very top the wide fumble band charges a few points. T170

And each row charges in its own coin: at the top the cost charges, at the bottom the fumble does, and in the middle both at once. That is why the chaotic column does not need the same tool in all three rows: it needs whichever one is cheap in each.

The figure that matters most to the GM is a different one: how often the story leaves the predictable. Adding up fumbles and costs, the die stops confirming what everyone could already see coming and forces a change of course. It comes to 1 % on the Epic and on Anguish, 3 % on the Realist, 10 % on First Gear, 19 % on Nightmare, 28 % on Pulp and 40 % on Black Comedy. That ladder separates the three columns without any overlap. Look at it before a long campaign for a concrete reason: a table takes plenty of surprise in one session and far less across twenty, because the unexpected tires whoever has to make it fit with yesterday.

Two edge effects that are decisions, not accidents. At +4, the cost almost disappears on the cards that carry it (from 32 % to 5 % on Pulp): the master no longer negotiates. And on Nightmare, at that same +4, clean failure disappears: whatever does not work out is always a disaster. Competence does not take the chaos away from you; it changes the coin you pay it in.

Do not read it as nine closed boxes. The two questions of the questionnaire place your game at a point on the map, and landing near an edge is the normal case: then you keep your cell’s reading and borrow only part of its neighbour —the band one step wider, say, without the cost. And if you land in a gap, you know what you have: that place is empty, and it is yours.

The +4 illusion

When you go from one die (1–10) to the sum (2–20), the +4 seems to “weigh less”, because the scale is twice as long. It does not. With 1d10 against TN 6, the Grand Master needs a 2 or more: 90%. With the sum against TN 11 they need a 7 or more: 85% T29T30. The bell curve concentrates results in the middle, so four points of bonus shift far more probability mass than their size suggests. Only five points are lost, and that is the grain of reality in Realist mode. The system changes the volatility, not your professional effectiveness.

Something similar happens with the coin toss (+0): 50% with one die and 55% with the sum ED-M.

The 99% principle. In Epic, the Grand Master succeeds at a standard task 99% of the time T21T22. That is not a bug: a Grand Master should have no trouble with the mundane. There is no “narrative inflation” here: the TN stays where it is, and a standard task is still standard. If failing would not be interesting, don't roll (0.6); failure being numerically rare is no reason not to roll. And if the player wants to show off, let them raise the difficulty themselves with “one hand behind the back”, for instance by ricocheting the bullet off the wall (2.5). Trade safety for glory.

A.10 The economy of the sheet: points, Keys and zeros

The cards say how you roll. This section says what it costs to be somebody (the pricing rules are in chapter 5), which is the other half of the game and is usually worse thought out in the rulebooks we read.

Why 12 and why 14

The pyramid has five Spheres, each with two Domains and eight Disciplines: ten slots per Sphere. If nobody at the table plays Arcane there are four Spheres in play, that is forty slots; if any player does, fifty.

Two more points for ten more slots keeps the scarcity almost identical. That is the whole mystery of the 14: it is not a reward for playing with magic, it is that the sheet grew and the rent had to be paid. That is why all fourteen go to everyone at the table as soon as one person plays Arcane, and why a world with magic the players cannot touch gives no extra points (5.2) C·22oct.

Breadth is cheap and freedom is expensive

The separate pot offers two coins at the same price, and they are not equivalent:

PurchaseCoversCostsPer Discipline

Freedom costs 60 % more: the Grid gives the eight Disciplines for five eighths of what they would cost in Keys (5.3). And there is a reason: the Grid is opened by the story, within the world’s ceilings (3.7), whereas you choose the Key and it points at what matters to you. There is also a pretty property that falls out of those numbers: climbing two levels in one single Discipline costs 4 Keys, and climbing one whole level across an entire Sphere costs 5 C·l4. Hyper-specialising costs almost the same as being big in general, so the system nudges you to spread out without forbidding the one-trick monster. T176

Two Grand Masters, and the third one is paid for by the card

A +4 costs four points: Domain 1, Discipline 1, Signature 2. But if it hangs from a Domain you have already paid for, only three. Covering the four Spheres so as not to be Null anywhere costs four, and on top of that base each Grand Master adds three: two Grand Masters with your back covered come to 4 + 3 + 3 = 10 points, with two to spare. The third would need 13, so with twelve points the sum does itself: two masteries with your back covered, or three masteries and a −2 hole somewhere (3 + 3 + 3 + 3 = 12). With Arcane and fourteen points it is another story: five Domains and three masteries come to 5 + 9 = 14, exactly the budget, and the third fits with no hole. T170

And this is where the engine and the economy touch, which is what this section exists to show. What that hole costs you is not decided by the sheet: it is decided by the card, because a −2 is not worth the same on all of them.

In the narrative reading the scale is whatever your world wants, or none at all, and the arithmetic of the price does not change C·r3. It is a ridiculously good deal for the player, and it is deliberate: the scene where a mortal kills a god has to be possible. What holds it back is not the price, it is the wallet.

The accordion: the long ladder of points

Keys have their square ladder; points have the accordion, and it is worth seeing both sets of numbers together because they grow in different ways.

And look at the last column, which is where the story is: the price per slot does not drop: 4 with a Signature, 4.4 with a Domain and 4.4 with a Sphere. Folding is not a bulk discount, it is simply the only way left to spend once you are already at +4, the top end of the Anchor (which runs from −2 to +4 on every sheet). That is why the accordion breaks nothing: it adds no numbers to the roll, it only removes gaps from the map. At the end of the road, a character with a folded Physical sphere and a freshly made one with a single Signature roll exactly the same when each does their own thing. The difference is that nobody can catch the first one outside their field any more.

The Key wallet is the only bottomless tap

Skill points saturate: no sheet goes past +4, there are fifty slots, and past a certain point you are only buying breadth. Keys do not saturate, because there is always another Discipline, another scene and another object in the Bazaar. At one Key per session per player (5.1) C·22oct, twenty sessions in each player has moved twenty Keys: what four Grid levels spread across four Spheres are worth (5.3), or four levels up in a single Discipline (sixteen Keys) with four to spare.

Hence the line that belongs on the first page of any long campaign: if you want the campaign to escalate, open the Key tap, not the points one.

The declared exception: when the TN does move

The TN does not move: difficulty goes into the bonus (1.3). Tragedy is the exception, declared and the only one in the book: the usual sum with the bar set at 14, said out loud. The same clockwork world, with hope out of reach C·22oct. How long it lasts is up to the table, with one piece of advice: we strongly recommend not staying long in the darkest tones. A scene or an ending, as in the Rules Chest’s Destiny module (9.1), rather than a whole campaign; but the table is made of adults, and it decides.

9.4 Rulebook Emulation

A roleplaying rulebook is, above all, a feeling. NOGUM does not copy it rule by rule: it emulates it by moving its own pieces (the tone card, the damage zoom, the bars, the maximum level in each Sphere) until the table recognises it. The recipes say what to move for each world; the case files show what a sheet built with them looks like.

The tasting menu

Twenty flavours. Each recipe emulates the lore of one of the best-known roleplaying games and explains which NOGUM pieces you need to move for the table to feel it. Principles: T119 T123 T127

  • Titles full of technical sarcasm. Each recipe has a parody name that nods to the trope or the rules of the original. This rulebook is for GMs who know their stuff.
  • Sheet as an image. In the final layout, each recipe is an at-a-glance visual card: the lore, the fix and the pieces.
  • Meta column. Each recipe has its own down-to-earth comments in the margin.
  • Listed by fame, not by theme. The twenty are the best-known games, not a forced split of four per block. T124

Each recipe has the lore (what the world is about) and the fix (what to tweak). Below them, when needed, an In NOGUM line translates the fix into specific pieces of the rulebook.

The references name nobody: each recipe is recognised by its parody title, its lore and its hint line. If you cannot place one at first glance, the recipe is badly written, not badly censored.

Every recipe carries its player maximum (3.7): how far a character can grow on the Grid, in each Sphere, in that universe, however long the campaign runs. The genre sets it, and it is what makes a world feel like that world. It is a proposal: the table confirms it at session zero with one question, is it possible in your world? Above it there may be the world maximum: the legendary NPCs, the thing that sleeps under the city. And all of this is Grid: Keys are best left without a ceiling. T184 C·r6

13. “HOMEOWNERS’ ASSOCIATION POLITICS (WITH FANGS)” · “THE ALBINOS AND THE DICE BAGS”

Ref: gothic horror

The lore: You are immortal and sexy, but you are still the city Prince’s errand boy.

The fix: Strict Hierarchical Grid (Generation). Blood bar as a stress resource.

In NOGUM, Generation is not a Grid ladder. What sets a vampire apart from a human is not strength: it is that they have Arcane, and a human does not. Their Grid depends on where you want to play (below). Spells —dominating, hiding, the senses of the blood— need a point in Arcane and are paid for with Keys (4.7), and we recommend they come dear: in a vampire game, growing is sweat. T188 If you want to tell a fledgling from a methuselah, do not raise the Grid: give them Keys —Fortitude, Stealth, Intimidation—, which is finer and does not break the scale. Thirst and Humanity are two different bars, and they are best not mixed. Thirst sits on the Mental bar, because it is craving: it works like withdrawal, not like a fuel tank. The fuller it gets, the worse you concentrate, the worse you judge risk and the cheaper giving in becomes; when it fills completely, frenzy sets in. Humanity is the Beast and sits on the Spiritual bar, because in there is somebody who is not you. The old rulebooks treated blood as a pool of points; here it is played as the thing that actually gives you play at the table: the craving. Suggested tone: Realist (predictable intrigue).

Critique-and-improvement recipe: “High tech, low hope, wrong dice”

Some real adaptations emulated a lore with the wrong tool. These sheets say, bluntly, how it should have been done. T122 T123

“HIGH TECH, LOW HOPE, WRONG DICE”

Ref: the classic chrome-and-rent rulebooks · Alias: the city that charges you for breathing

The diagnosis: the genre promises one thing —that every job comes off almost right and the bill arrives later— and the engine delivers another: a lethal, realistic simulation where the plan either works or it does not, and if it does not, you die. With those rules the table learns fast that the profitable move is not to take risks, and a chrome game where nobody takes risks is a bookkeeping game. The flaw is not the lethality: it is that lethality without “yes, but” turns the genre into a scene-avoidance simulator.

The NOGUM solution: this calls for Black Comedy, not Realist. The red die with success at a cost and wide bands gives exactly what the genre wants: the job comes off, and it comes off with a debt, a witness, a failing implant or a body that was not in the plan. Lethality is not removed, it is moved: it goes to the zoom (Lethal, one blow decides) and to the tags, not to the odds of hitting.

Golden rule, the bill: in this recipe the GM cannot say “nothing happens”. Every success at a cost leaves something written on the sheet or on the city, and three sessions in, the characters no longer decide by what they want but by what they owe. That is cyberpunk.

In NOGUM: Black Comedy, Lethal zoom, the Mental bar called Humanity (ED-39), the menu of the four costs (6.6) as a list of bills, and equipment with its three locks (Access, that the story allows it, and its price in both purses, 5.6), which is where the genre lives: wanting the railgun and not being able to pay for it is the plot.

Player maximum (Phy · Men · Soc · Tec · Arc): 2 · 1 · 1 · 2 · —. Nearly those of recipe 8 (2 · 1 · 1 · 1): body, head and dealings stay where they were, and only Technical goes up to 2, because with the engine swapped what scales is debts, favours and machines, not chrome. Debts and favours scale with Keys, not with Grid: there is no being who can stop any human from rolling to resist being seduced, convinced or intimidated (4.8). T170 C·22oct

The other adaptation made with the wrong tool, the world on the back of chelonians, is recipe 20, with its diagnosis, because besides being a critique it is one of the twenty most played.

That rulebook gives itself away with its three six-sided dice and tables that all but demand square roots. The other critique-and-improve sheet is the cyberpunk one, right here. Two is enough: any more diagnosis sheets would turn the toolbox into a settling of scores. T121 T124

They see you, but not her.
Her power does not hide her: it changes her. Whoever looks at her sees whoever they expect to see, and raises no alarm. She fools whoever is looking, one at a time, without making her bigger: that is why it is Stealth one level above, not two (4.3). C·24oct
Unleashed.
A cloak of invisibility denies one sense, and it can be taken from you. Hers fools them all: sight, hearing, a dog, a thermal camera and a DNA test. No conditions and no straps: triple, 3 Keys (5.5).
The voice is power too.
She changes her face and her vocal cords: her looks and her voice are her excuse. When she talks, she rolls Deception with her Signature, +4: wearing the face is easy; talking like that person is the hard part. And she carries another Key, Deception one level above: anyone at her level gets no roll against her act; at most, they tell her “you’re acting weird today”. To notice, they need a Key, at least knowing that one of their own is not who they say they are, and even then they roll against a Signature.
No Arcane.
She was born with her power and needs no ritual: it is a Key, not magic. If nobody else at the table plays Arcane, she shares out 12 points (5.2).
The scene: the tower.
The headquarters of a corporation armed to the teeth has Grid 3 security, and she is a 1 with level-2 Keys. So she steals the CEO’s face: her power does not raise her to 3, it gives her the opening. Being the owner of the building is a momentary Key: the situation hands it over and it is not paid for (4.4). Inside, she rolls with her full bonus. And what the scene gives you, it can take away at any moment, also at no cost: if the real CEO shows up, it is gone.
What she leaves empty.
She cannot fix anything (Artifice, clumsy) and knows nobody (Hierarchy, clumsy): nobody knows Nobody.

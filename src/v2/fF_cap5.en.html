Chapter 5 · What It Costs (the Tally)

Everything a character has is paid for with two currencies, and each one buys one thing. Points buy what makes you better: a bonus to a roll. Keys buy what lets you do what cannot be done at your level: a permit, a level above. Everything else follows from that: damage is not paid for, what lasts one scene is not paid for, and what is for good costs at least one. C·e7 C·e8

The previous chapters say what each piece is: the bonus (chapter 2), the Grid (chapter 3), the Key (chapter 4). Here we count what it is worth, with a single tally that works the same for a skill, a spell, a pistol or a giant robot. The Multiverse Bazaar (9.3) is that same tally already done, object by object, with its workshop of solved cases.

The tally fits on a napkin. The Adjustment, in the napkin’s margin.

5.1 Two purses

The creation budget, experience and the price of equipment come out of two independent purses. C·e3 ED-33

  • Skill points: the trade. They buy the pyramid (2.2) and any other bonus to a roll, wherever it comes from: the master armourer’s sword, the automatic sight.
  • Keys: the size. They buy what cannot be done at your level: a permit (4.6), a level above in a whole Discipline (4.1), whatever a piece of equipment has of a Key and, with the plot’s permission, Grid jumps.

They are not exchanged for each other: points do not buy Keys and Keys do not buy points. Each has its own pace, agreed at session zero. By default, one Key per session per head; points, at the pace the table chooses (5.8). C·e3 C·22oct

Whatever is bought —equipment, above all— also asks for a payment that comes out of no purse: Access, that it be within your reach. The three locks, in 5.6. C·e5 ED-36

5.2 What you start with

A new character starts with 12 skill points and a separate pot: 5 Keys or one Grid level. If any player takes Arcane, everyone shares out 14. With the pot on the Grid, the sheet starts at 1·1·1·1·2 —a 2 in one Sphere and 1 in the other four—, the recommended starting point, not a limit. C·a5 C·r5

Points: 12, or 14 for everyone

If any player in the game takes Arcane, they all get 14, the human who never touches it as well: nobody gets more points for having magic, and that is fairness at the table. If your world has magic but it is not within the players’ reach, it gives no points: it is 12. C·a5 C·22oct The two extra points pay for the ten skills (two Domains and eight Disciplines) that Arcane adds to everyone’s sheet, and scarcity stays the same; the sums, in A.10. ED-31

Twelve points force you to manage scarcity: if you want to stand out at something, you will almost certainly be Null somewhere. The price of Signatures is being Null: with twelve, two Grand Masters and your back covered come to ten, and a third in another Discipline means leaving a Sphere at −2. What that gap costs at the table is decided by the card: a −2 pulls off half of what it attempts in Epic and fewer than one in ten in Anguish (A.10). T170 That said, hand out as many points as you like: the rulebook is there to fit your campaign. Borrador, T107

The separate pot

The pot rule

On top of the points, every character gets a pot that does not mix: +1 Grid level in one Sphere, or 5 own Keys. It is not exchanged for points in either direction. C·e1 T176 T183

The reason is a table reason: if they could be exchanged, you would get the Grid hero with no trade, someone enormous who cannot do anything. With the twelve points spent on skills, everybody turns up with a trade underneath the power.

The choice is not neutral. The level covers a Sphere’s eight Disciplines for five Keys; freedom costs 60% more, but you choose it (5.3). Torn? Grid or Key? (3.1).

The starting point: 1·1·1·1·2

It is a human sheet in four Spheres, with a jump in the fifth. You can start higher or lower, depending on what you want to emulate. What does hold: the 2 only goes where the universe allows it (3.7); each recipe, the ready-to-play settings in chapter 9, proposes where (9.4) and the table confirms it. If you do not want that 2, you take the 5 Keys. There are worlds that allow no 2 at all —a realist one, a zombie one— and there everybody starts at 1·1·1·1·1 with their 5 Keys. C·r5 T170

Starting experience

Before the first session a table can hand out more points and more Keys, or fewer, through the same purses and at the same prices (5.8). The default budget is a comfortable place to start, not the minimum: it is designed for cyberpunk, where the world tends to be more powerful than in a zombie or cosmic-horror game. C·e4 It is also the way to start above 2: a 3 is two levels, twenty Keys (the pot’s five and fifteen more, 5.3), agreed at session zero, and by default the others get the equivalent (3.6). Make it a decision and not an oversight: more points give characters with fewer reasons to go out looking for Keys, and looking for them is a good part of the game. T170 T173 T176

If the campaign is about powers or gadgets, equipment eats into the points: raise the budget, knowing that only breadth grows (nobody goes past +4, 2.1) and that with twenty points nobody is the one who knows how to do something.

5.4 The tally

NOGUM does not write one rule for drones and another for cloaks: everything is bought with the same two currencies, and whatever the currencies cannot say is said by a tag, the Adjustment (5.5). ED-37 C·e5 C·e8 C·22oct

The tally, step by step

  1. Describe it in one sentence, as if you were telling the table about it.
  2. How long does it last? What is single use or lasts one scene is not paid for, and you are done (below).
  3. Tag it: Damage, Dodge and Soak (4.2) and, if it reaches another level, its Grid, like the Reaper (4.5); how they are written on a weapon’s stat block, in 6.3. Damage is the only thing that is not paid for: it comes with the object.
  4. Bonuses, in points. Every +1 to a roll of yours, 1 point, as in the pyramid. Whatever makes the other side’s defence harder, 1 point per −1: dodging does not get them fully clear (Dodge: Partial), 1; a −2 to their dodge (Dodge: Hard (−2)), 2; their armour only takes a little off the blow (Soak: Max 1), 1. The automatic sight or the +2 to hit, the same.
  5. Permits, in Keys. What no skill can do costs 1 Key (4.6), and between half and triple depending on its conditions (the Adjustment, 5.5); if a power does several things, each of them limited, the total can be divided, without going below 1. A spell is a permit (4.7). C·l9 C·l10
  6. Levels, in Keys. Climbing n levels in a whole Discipline, against everything, costs n²: 1, 4, 9 (4.3). Limited —against one single thing, or only for damage—, half, rounded up: at two levels, 2; at three, 5. At one level it costs the same as the complete one (4.3). You can climb up to the world maximum (3.7); the price depends on whom it can be used against (6.3). C·l4 C·22oct
  7. What it cancels, in Keys. A defence that at your level is not made harder but cancelled is a Key: the pistol does not make a human’s dodge hard, it takes it away. And affecting the next level of the Grid, even with damage, is a Key by right: the Reaper (4.5). One Key per trait, without charging twice for the same concept (5.6). C·e8 C·22oct
  8. A whole Sphere is not a Key: it is Grid, 5·n² (5.3). T183
  9. Adjust whatever the tally has failed to say (5.5). If it is for good, it costs at least 1.

Two tests that avoid almost every mistake

  • If a skill already does it, it is not a power: it is a bonus. “Sensing when you are being lied to” is Empathy; “shrugging off pain”, Fortitude. Buying it as a power means paying for it twice.
  • If it puts you above your level, it is not a permit: it is a level Key, and it is paid for squared. Seeing through a wall is a permit; being undetectable is Stealth one level above, Unleashed (4.3).

The drone

It carries a camera and sends the image to your goggles: +2 to whatever you look at through it. Two points, and not one Key, because there is already a Discipline for seeing. But the drone can be seen and it can be shot at: the day they bring it down, you lose the +2 and you have made noise. Limited: one point. Not a single drone rule: the drawback is charged by the fiction, and the system only decides that it is worth half.

How long it lasts: one use, now and then, for good

  • Single use, or one scene: not paid for. The grenade, the elixir, the scroll that burns up, the Key the fiction gives you for this fight. It is not part of your sheet, and that is why it is not paid for, because it lasts so little, even if its price were high. To have it again, you have to get it again in the fiction. C·e7 C·22oct
  • What never runs out is permanent. The quiver nobody counts, the bag of potions that is always full: it is equipment, and it pays its full price.
  • What has to be replenished now and then is Limited: if you can run out of it at the worst moment, half (5.5).

The minimum is one. Whatever is for good costs at least one Key, or one point, even if the tally came out lower. You can make a power as limited as you like; if you want it for good, you pay 1, and it is yours. C·e7

5.5 The Adjustment

Some powers refuse to be itemised. The tally says 1 Key and at the table the power solves the whole game; or it says 9 and it turns out the gadget only works if you are wearing it and you do not always have it to hand. That is what the Adjustment is for: you look at what the power does that the tally has not paid for, and you multiply. ED-37

TagWhat it meansPoints and Keys
LimitedWorth less than the tally says because it comes with strings: only while you wear it, once per scene, only in daylight, it is not always to hand÷2
CompleteThe tally is the whole truth×1
EnhancedIt does what it says and also something that is not in the tally×2
UnleashedThe tally falls far short: it has no conditions and not even the fiction takes it away from you×3

The Adjustment multiplies points and Keys, not Access. It rounds up, and the total for anything permanent does not go below 1 (5.4). And it is vague on purpose: the four rungs are a guide for pricing by rule of thumb, not a chart anyone has to defend in play. C·e7 C·l9

The drone in 5.4 is the Adjustment going down: two points, Limited, one. And the same the other way. Flight is a one-Key permit, and in a rooftop campaign it does not solve a scene: it solves a third of the campaign —the chases, the heights, the escapes—. The tally has not paid for that. Unleashed: three Keys. The tag is not a power dial: it is the corrector for what the itemising has failed to charge.

The Adjustment only pays for what the tally has not paid already

This is the easiest mistake to make, so it goes in bold. If you have already itemised the power —given it its Grid level, its permits and its bonuses—, do not charge for it again with the tag. The Adjustment lives exactly where the system does not reach, and it withdraws by itself the moment the system reaches.

So that two different tables do not price the same thing differently, one single calibration question: how many scenes does this solve that you have not already paid for? None, and with strings attached on top: Limited. What the tally says: Complete. A whole scene of its own: Enhanced. It changes how the campaign is played: Unleashed.

It is agreed when bought and written next to the power. If play proves it was mispriced, re-tag it and adjust the price: a subjective call that multiplies by three needs a way out. And if something asks for more than ×3, it is usually not a power: it is a level you have not spotted, or it is scenery, part of the world and not of your sheet.

The same word, three prices

The invisibility from 4.6 is the perfect example, because it is one word naming three different things depending on the table.

So how much of this can you pay for on day one? All of it. The Adjustment is not a luxury of late campaigns, because it multiplies the same twelve points and the same pot: a +3 Unleashed power is nine points out of twelve, and you have three left. It is a legal sheet and it is a hard sheet —you have bought something enormous and you have almost no trade underneath—, and that is how the system charges instead of forbidding. It is the same sentence as in 5.2: the price of Signatures is being Null, and the price of the impossible is having no trade.

5.6 Equipment

Equipment is paid for like everything else: points for the bonus, Keys for whatever it has of a Key. It adds a lock in front, Access, and one exception: damage, which comes with the weapon. C·e5 C·e8

A piece can give you a quality bonus (the master gunsmith’s rifle, +1: one point), a permit or a level above, whatever it has of a Key (Keys), and effects, the catch-all: the cursed weapon that corrupts you pays for what it gives you, and whatever only makes your life harder is already charged by the fiction.

The three locks

Getting something onto your sheet asks for three things, and the stat block of each Bazaar object says which ones (9.3): C·e5 ED-34

  1. Access: what your Resources let you have (Social → Hierarchy → Resources, below). Everyday things ask for 0, a serious weapon 1, military hardware 2, what should not exist 3. If you fall short it is not that it is expensive: it is out of your reach, and getting hold of it is a plot.
  2. The story allows it: that it makes sense for it to be in your hands here and now.
  3. The cost in both purses, with whatever Adjustment suits it (5.4). Most things ask for one currency, or none. Anything permanent that grants a permit costs at least 1 Key even if its Access is 0. T176

The third stops a catalogue pistol from handing out for free what another character pays for with their Keys. What it does not charge for is rarity: that is said by the world —it is under lock and key, it leaves a trail—, and it is Access. T175

Powers carry no Access: the Force, ki, superpowers describe what you are, and they are paid for in points and Keys. What the fiction gives you as setting —the family car— is scenery until the day you buy it as equipment. T176

On a sheet · “Rivet”: seven gadgets through the three locks (9.5)

Weapons

A weapon is the tally applied to something that deals damage. One line per rule; the solved cases (the katana, the railgun, the grenade launcher…) are in the Bazaar’s workshop (9.3). C·e8 C·22oct

  • Damage, free. An axe and a knife cost the same in the purses: nothing. Access is what separates them.
  • Whatever makes the other side’s defence harder, or makes you hit better, in points (5.4).
  • Whatever cancels a defence at your level, in Keys. A plain pistol cannot be dodged at level 1: it pays 1 Key. The same goes for whatever gets through armour at your level. Space-opera blasters can be dodged, by genre convention, and they do not pay that Key.
  • The Reaper, a Key Limited to damage: it covers damage, not hitting. In the hands of a level 1, a Reaper 2 costs 1 Key and a Reaper 3, 2; in the hands of a level 2, the Reaper 3 costs 1 (4.5).
  • One Key per trait, without charging twice for the same concept. Without a Reaper, being impossible to dodge (Dodge: None) and armour being no use (Soak: None) are two traits: 2 Keys. The Reaper already smashes the shield, so a Reaper with Soak: None does not pay that extra Key; if it cannot be dodged either, that is another trait: +1.

What inherits the scale

The mecha or the exoskeleton whose sheet you play with while you wear it, with its level across a whole Sphere, is Synchronisation (3.8), and it is paid for as Grid, 5·n²: Limited, half rounded up, only if it is not always available and the table decides that weighs enough; a suit you never take off pays in full, because it is your body. An ordinary vehicle inherits nothing: whatever it does above your level is a Key, like the tank in 4.8. T181 T184

What comes with you

An animal companion, a familiar or a helper who goes with you is paid from your wallets, like equipment, but with no Access: it is not bought in a shop, it is a bond (the trained hawk from the market does ask for Access). It carries a quick sheet (7.4) and is paid as the pair: C·26oct

  • What you both do is bought once, for the two of you, at the normal price: sensing, tracking, moving. Whoever is there rolls, and if you are both there, whoever has it better. If you split up, it stays with whoever paid for it.
  • What only it brings goes in full on its sheet: its claws, its nose, flying. Permits, in Keys; and if it operates above your level, whatever it takes you above, like the mutant pet in the Bazaar (9.3).
  • By default, it helps: its presence is a modifier to your roll (2.5), and it pays nothing. If it acts on its own, with its own roll, it does more than the tally says, and whatever it uses for that goes Enhanced (5.5). Sharing senses breaks nothing; acting twice a round does.
  • If you lose it, you get back what you paid, like equipment you let go (5.7). Whether another one comes is up to the story.

5.7 Giving back, lending, gifting

Equipment gives back its points and its Keys when you let it go. What is lent or stolen lasts one scene and costs nothing. The GM’s gifts are not paid for while the story asks for them. All three come from the same idea: you pay for what is part of your sheet, and only while it is. C·e6

Giving back

When you sell it, it breaks or you leave it, its points and its Keys become free again: you lose the object, not what you paid. The equipment Key lives in the object; the one that goes in no object goes with you. T181

Lent and stolen: one scene

Stolen gear works for one scene

What is stolen, lent or obtained in the fiction is used for a whole scene without paying anything, whoever uses it and even if nobody does; passing it around resets nothing. When the scene ends, either you pay for it and it is yours, or you leave it. That way looting is a decision and not an escalation.

You steal the C4 at the bank and keep passing it around: when you leave the bank, the C4 is gone, or you pay for it.

Handing over between players

What is single use is simply declared: you give the silver bullet to whoever shoots best. What is permanent is handed over: the owner releases it, gets back what they paid, and the other pays for it out of their own purse. And one line that settles almost everything: what you carry in your hand can be lent; what you carry in your head cannot. The holy water changes owner; knowing the true name does not. T176

The GM’s gifts

The robot the agency assigns you for the whole campaign, the group’s ship, the mentor’s relic: they are not paid for while the story asks for them, because the gift is part of the premise. The day it is truly yours, it is paid for. The same goes for the Keys the fiction gives, the borrowed and the momentary one (4.4): they are not yours, and they are not paid for. T184

5.8 Progression

Growing means filling both purses: points make you wider, Keys make you bigger, and the Grid is raised by the story. Each thing has its owner and its pace. C·e3 C·r8

The permission belongs to the story; the price, to the rule

The Grid is not bought without the plot’s permission, but it has a price (5.3). That is fairness in both directions: T176 T184

  • The story raises the Grid and you do not want to rise: you collect the jump in Keys, five for staying one level behind and fifteen more if the table rises again. They are yours and you spend them wherever you like, with the table’s complicity.
  • You want to rise sooner, or where the others do not: you save up and pay for the jump as soon as the story gives permission.

By default nobody is left behind (3.6); the GM can stagger it or hand out small rewards, and what matters is that it does not feel unfair. The normal thing is still to agree on it: the tally is there for when it needs to be fair. T173 T181

The Key purse is the campaign’s power dial

Points only make you wider: nobody goes past +4 (2.1). Keys open up equipment and raise levels, and that does not saturate. That is why the default pace, one per session per head, is generous even if it does not look it: the pistol, the permit, the level above all come out of that pocket. C·22oct

The recommendation: raise Keys before points. Maxed-out points erase the roles within the group; Keys make you more capable at your own thing. Opening both taps at once without thinking it through is not recommended.

Question 2 · Who grows?

Everyone equally, or whoever earns it in the story. Handing out purely on merit makes the strong stronger; the healthy shape is for merit to add on top of a base: everyone gets what the pace says, and whoever had a big moment takes +1 extra.

What gets rewarded

  1. Resolving the arc’s central conflict.
  2. Playing a flaw against your own interest when it was inconvenient.
  3. Finding a Key, or giving one to someone else.
  4. Paying a heavy cost for the group.
  5. Closing a thread of the character’s personal story.
  6. Failing memorably and owning the consequences.
  7. Surviving a night on a harder card than the campaign’s.

Experience does not break the maths

Experience makes nobody taller: only wider (2.1). The characters widen, the GM raises the world, and the challenge comes back on its own. If they live at +2 and +4 in their own field and the cards lose the clean failure, the answer is not to cut points: it is to move the Grid, more likely raising the players than lowering the enemy (3.1). When there is nothing left to raise, points have their inward ladder, the accordion (2.4); Keys climb outwards.

Advantages and flaws: bought, and they happen

Advantages and flaws are permanent traits that are not skills: night vision, the implant, the phobia. They arrive by two roads. C·22oct

Bought like everything else

An advantage is paid for with the usual tally (5.4), with its Adjustment. A flaw goes the other way round: it gives you points for something else, by the same rule of thumb. The [Slow] tag is the example: the marked skill is no use for initiative, the roll that decides who acts first in a conflict (6.1), and if it is so slow that it does not fit into a conflict, it cannot even be used in one; it can be taken as a flaw that gives points. There is no catalogue: it is an engine for GMs to build with. T87

Whether you start with advantages or flaws is decided at session zero; in principle, total freedom with your points and your 5 Keys. C·22oct

And they happen. A flaw is the scar of serious damage. A character’s health is kept in bars —physical, mental, spiritual—, split into blocks (6.4). When a block of your Mental or Spiritual bar fills up and you later heal, the −2 it brought goes away, but something with a name stays behind: the insomnia, the debt, the fear of water. The advantage arrives the same way, when the fiction owes you one. The thresholds, in 6.5. ED-35

The idea: fast growth, a bonus for good narrative play and fixed experience at campaign milestones. Milestones work as an incentive; experience, to manage expectations. T37

5.9 Clarifications

Almost everything is settled by the sentence at the start: bonus, points; Key, Keys; damage and anything for one scene, free; anything permanent, at least one. The weapon cases, solved, are in the Bazaar’s workshop (9.3). T179

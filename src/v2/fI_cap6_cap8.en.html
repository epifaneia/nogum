Chapter 6 · Danger

Danger is played with the same roll as everything else. The attacker rolls to hit, the defender rolls to get out of it, and whoever gets further up wins; a tie goes to the defender. Weapons say which defence they cancel, just like a Key. And what is at stake is health (of the body, of the head and of the spirit), which comes back when you are safe, and the wounds it leaves, which stay. C·c1 C·c3 C·c6

Combat carries special weight in every roleplaying rulebook, and its differences from normal rolls deserve a chapter of their own. Combat is simple on purpose, and it comes with no optional modes to make it more tactical: that change would not alter the tone or the universe, it would only move the game along the narrative–tactical axis, and this rulebook is anchored on the narrative side. It is recommended that every non-player character (NPCs, the ones the GM runs) who is going to interact has a sheet (chapter 7), or at least the few brushstrokes needed for the handful of rolls that will be required. 6.1 Initiative: who attacks first?

What is the most cinematic way to hand out turns? NOGUM starts from an intuitive idea: speed comes from mastery. The gunslinger draws fast because they have done it ten thousand times; the hacker types fast because they are a master. By default, every skill can be used to roll initiative.

And since roleplaying is fluid improvisation, the game is not held up by choosing the action in advance. The intention is chosen.

The three guards

  1. The GM asks: “The situation explodes. What guard are you going in with?”
  2. Each player declares one of these three (not exactly what they will do, but how they will tackle the problem) and rolls their best skill that fits it:
    Violence
    You go in head-on. You roll your highest-rated attack skill. When your turn comes, you can use a different skill, as long as it is still direct and destructive.
    Trick
    You have a bright idea: you deceive, hide, use the terrain to your advantage, build a gadget or pull one out of your magic pocket. You roll Stealth, Deception, Sleight of Hand… Flexibility is encouraged. On your turn you can switch to another skill, as long as it is still sideways and sly.
    Speciality
    You are going to use one specific skill: driving, hacking, healing. “I don’t know exactly what I’ll do, but I’ll do it with the computer.” There is no wiggle room here: whatever you do must fit that skill like a glove.
  3. Actions go from highest to lowest result. On your turn you specify what you do within your guard. Tie: whoever has the higher bonus goes first; if it is still tied, protagonists before NPCs. Critical: you act first. Fumble: last. T176

The guard marker. Every sheet carries a marker with the three guards. It is not a rule as such, just a reminder: if the table forgets to choose before acting, the guard on the marker from last time applies. Decisión del autor, 17 sep

What if I no longer want to do what I declared? You can switch, but you have lost all your momentum: you prepared for one thing and now you are starting from scratch. Changing guard sends you to the back of the queue for the current round, with no further penalty. T85, T129 ED-8

Slow skills. By default, the hacker types as fast as the gunslinger shoots. If that jars in your universe, that skill carries the [Slow] tag on the sheet, which can be taken as a flaw that gives points for something else (5.8). C·22oct A skill marked that way cannot be used to roll initiative: either you go in with another guard, or you go last. And if it is so slow that it does not fit within the time of a conflict, it cannot even be used in one. T87 Decisión del autor

Example. Warrior: Violence with Sword +4 → gets 13 and goes first. Hacker: Speciality with Systems +2 → gets 9. Thief: Trick with Stealth +2 → gets 7. By the time it’s the hacker’s turn, the warrior has already smashed the lights he was planning to switch off, so, without leaving his speciality, he hacks the enemy comms instead.

NPCs roll too. They declare a guard like everyone else: it is an opposed roll with the skill of the chosen guard, and quick sheets settle it with their Sphere at +2 (7.4). Scenery does not roll: it acts when the scene calls for it (7.5). Decisión del autor

6.2 The attack sequence: impact versus mitigation

First of all, the power gap (3.1). If attacker and defender are not at the same Grid level in the Sphere of the attack, the one below has no right to roll, and the blow of the one above is not rolled: the defeat is narrated (if the one above is a protagonist and wants to see how they win, they roll their Style). A Key changes that: with it, this sequence is played, with the full bonus, in whatever the Key covers (4.2). What each weapon cancels is stated in its tags (6.3). T170 T176 T183 C·l2 C·r10 C·22oct

To avoid slow arithmetic (subtracting dodges from attacks), NOGUM uses two steps. Totals are not pitted against each other: each side rolls against the TN, and you compare which step of the card each roll landed on (fumble, failure, success at a cost, success or critical). That step is its result level. C·c1

Step 1 · Impact (attacker)

The attacker declares their intention and rolls against the TN of the active tone card. None of the defender’s skills give bonuses here, but their circumstances do, as difficulty modifiers (2.5): cover, fog, being very small…

  • Fumble: the attack doesn’t connect. It may turn against the attacker; if that makes no narrative sense, it may hit an ally or cause some other adverse effect. End of sequence.
  • Failure: the attack goes wide, hits a wall or the target moves. End of sequence.
  • Success: the attack connects. The damage is “in the air”, travelling towards the target.
  • Critical: it connects in a vital spot: +1 damage. It is the only arithmetic in combat. T129

Step 2 · Mitigation (defender)

The defender only rolls if the attack has connected: nobody throws themselves at the bullet that was not going to hit them. If the attacker fumbles or fails, there is no defence roll. C·22oct And they can try to avoid the damage only if the weapon’s spec sheet allows it (6.3). They choose one permitted route, dodge or soak, and roll their defence skill against the TN of the active card.

The roll does not subtract points on its own. What counts is its result level, which is compared with the attacker’s as the box below explains. T170

Defence wins ties ED-25

Both of you rolled against the same TN, so all you have to look at is who got further up. Totals and margins are not compared: result levels are.

Tags override the tie. Every weapon states on its spec sheet which defences it allows (6.3). A weapon with Dodge: Partial leaves you eating at least 1 point even if you match it, because it is an area, a burst or fire: it cannot be fully dodged. And with Dodge: None there is not even a dodge to roll: all that is left is soaking, if the weapon allows it. C·c3

Defence at a cost (on the cards that carry it): it reduces like a success, −1, and the GM also charges a price from the cost menu. You walk away alive, but you drop the weapon, lose the position or end up where you did not want to be.

The fumble menu

A fumble is an ugly failure: it does not work, and something else happens too. How much it hurts is up to the table and the tone; so that you are not improvising from scratch on one roll in five on the hard cards, here are six doors:

  1. A route closes. The lock jams, the contact stops trusting you, the bridge gives way.
  2. Something gets used up and does not come back. The last charge, the last vial, the favour they owed you.
  3. You leave a trace. Someone will know you were there, and not in the good way.
  4. The threat takes a step closer. The clock ticks on, the patrol turns the corner, the ritual reaches its next verse.
  5. What you are carrying breaks. The weapon, the tool, the radio. Not necessarily for good.
  6. Someone notices you. And now you have a problem with a name.

In the predictable column the fumble closes a door: the path you tried is gone. In the chaotic one it opens a branch: something new turns up that nobody had put there. Same menu, different size.

Optional rule · The monster does not tremble

The card says how the table feels with the dice in hand, but the adversary is not holding them. If a horror fumbles and misses like a frightened protagonist, the fear goes flat: on Nightmare, a monster at +2 misses half its attacks (51 %) and fumbles almost one in five (19 %).

A GM who knows the craft can have an adversary with a name —the nemesis, the thing in the cellar— make all its rolls on First Gear: red die only, TN 6. At the same +2 it hits 70 % of the time and fumbles 10 %.

  • Towards the centre. Not to the opposite extreme: a monster on Epic hits 91 % and hardly ever fumbles, and that feels unfair.
  • Named ones only. Quick sheets roll on the table’s card: a cultist dropping his dagger is horror too.
  • Out loud. “This one rolls on First Gear.” NOGUM exists to declare what used to be done in secret.

Use it with great care. Oppression already brings frustration, and an enemy who does not even miss multiplies it; and if every blow takes a third of your health (Lethal health, 6.4), it takes people out of the fight a good deal faster. Decisión del autor · T169

Summary: first we see whether you get hit. Then, whether your armour or your reflexes save your life. T176

What decides whether it helps are the weapon’s tags —Soak: Max N, protection only removes N, and Soak: None, it removes nothing (6.3)— and whatever bonuses the protection you are wearing adds. Anything without tags is not an attack

Combat tags only make sense on something that does damage. A torch, a rope or a map do not carry them because they attack nobody; if someone uses the crowbar as a weapon, read it as Dodge: Full Soak: Full, which is the default value of anything that does not say otherwise.

And because defence wins ties (6.2), these tags are the game’s main balance surface: a Dodge: Full weapon is stopped almost every time, and a Dodge: None one is not stopped. That is the difference between a long duel and a short gunfight, and it is why whatever a tag takes away from the other side’s defence has to be paid for (5.4). C·e8

6.4 Fractal health (the Russian doll)

Health in NOGUM works the same way in every game: there are four states of health (Healthy, Wounded, Critical and Out of the fight). But lethality is one of the most powerful ways to set the tone, and the solution is modular once again: the four states do not change, but how many hits it takes to reach each one does. It is a matter of how closely you look at health, and that level of detail we call zoom. Borrador, T35

That four-state scale is a bar, and a character does not have just one: Physical is health, and Mental and Spiritual work the same way (6.5).

Healing

  • Gritty or realistic game: threshold 1 consequences, hours to days; threshold 2, days to weeks; threshold 3, weeks to months. An implant, a potion or a magical bath can skip those timeframes.
  • Battle with no narrative weight for the character: light consequences, with a simple cure, but none of them disappears on its own. What a simple cure means is up to the universe: in the animated world, a change of panel; in a hyper-realist one, a whole session in A&E. T181 T183
  • Battle tied to the character’s personal story: physical or mental wounds leave permanent or hard-to-heal after-effects, which can only be healed through another significant arc, not through a long or expensive process.

The four-cost menu (success at a cost)

On the cards with success at a cost (1.3: Unleashed Pulp and Black Comedy), the GM should avoid falling back on “you manage it, but you lose a hit point”. Choose a cost: T137–T138

  1. Time: you manage it, but it takes too long. You open the safe, but the guards are already in the corridor. Roll initiative.
  2. Resource: you manage it, but you break something. You kill the zombie, but the bat splinters and you are left without a weapon.
  3. Position: you manage it, but you are left exposed. You jump the wall, slip and land on your back; the enemy has you in their sights.
  4. Friendly fire: you manage it, but you harm an ally or the group. The explosion kills the monster, but it also takes out the only radio.

6.7 A scene, from start to finish

This is how everything above sounds at the table. Inés, a court vampire (her sheet is built step by step in 7.2), has Grid 2 in Physical, Brawling +2 and Style +2. Tomás, a human hunter, has Grid 1 in everything, Brawling +2 and one Key: his hunter’s training. He knows how to get close to a vampire without it sensing his hostility (4.1). And he carries a stake. Tonight they go into the house of the Margrave, a vampire with Grid 2 in Physical and Acrobatics +2. At the door, a human doorman, Grid 1. C·23oct

The Margrave’s house

The card. The court is played on Realist (1.4): both d10s are rolled and added, TN 11; fumble if the sum is 2–3, critical if it is 19–20, and no success at a cost.

Initiative. Inés and the Margrave roll: both are Grid 2 in Physical. Tomás would have no right to roll: the Margrave hears a heart beating on the other side of the wall, senses hostility before he sees it, and would see a human coming from miles away (3.1). But years of hunter’s training are his Key, and with it he rolls like anyone else (6.1). Inés gets 15; Tomás, 13; the Margrave, 12.

The gap. Inés against the doorman: both in Physical, and she is one level above. The doorman has no right to roll, neither to stop her nor to touch her, and Inés does not roll to hit, because she hits (3.1). She only wants to know how, so she rolls Style (3.5): 6 and 5, eleven, plus two, 13. It beats the TN: success, you look magnificent. Inés folds him against the door frame without spilling her glass.

A single card. The Margrave’s body has no weak points: bullets go in and out. Hurting him takes a permit, and Tomás carries one: the stake (Damage 1 Dodge: Hard (−2), from the Bazaar). It is a Limited Key: it only works in the heart (4.3). Tomás aims, and aiming up close, face to face, costs him −1. The impact: 8 and 4, twelve, plus two, minus one, 13. Success: the stake connects (6.2). The Margrave dodges with Acrobatics +2, −2 for the stake: 7 and 5, twelve. Success too, and ties go to the defender. The dodge fails, but not entirely: the stake goes in below the collarbone, not into the heart. No heart, no Key; no Key, no damage. The Margrave wears the stake the way you or I would wear a hairpin.

What the Keys do not cover. It is the Margrave’s turn. He pulls the stake out without hurrying and goes for Tomás. Tomás staked everything on a single card: his training got him close without being sensed, and the stake let him go for the heart. Neither lets him defend himself or keep fighting. So there is no roll, neither to hit nor for damage: the defeat is narrated. The Margrave lifts him by the neck and throws him against the fireplace, and Tomás is out of the fight (6.4).

On a par. That leaves Inés. She and the Margrave are both at Grid 2 in Physical: from here on the dice are rolled as normal, blow by blow, as between two humans.

6.8 Clarifications

The areas that have been hardest to pin down. Each case applies a rule from the chapter; it adds none. What happens when attacker and defender are not at the same level lives in 3.1 and 4.2, and the scene in 6.7 shows it at the table.

Can a weapon deal Damage 3? With a critical or with the defender’s fumble, yes: Damage 2 plus the +1. And with both at once, Damage 4, because they add up. As a tag, no: whatever lies above 2 is the Reaper (4.5).

If I match the attacker with a dodge, do I always get away with it? Only if the weapon allows it: with Dodge: Partial you eat at least 1 point (6.2).

We have won a fight. Do the bars come back? Only if the GM declares that you are safe. Consequences do not come back with the bar: they are healed (6.4).

The hero rolls on one card and the monster on another. How are they compared? By result levels, each read on its own card (8.3).

Chapter 8 · The Mixing Desk

The rules are the knobs on a desk, and the GM moves them so that the table feels what it agreed in session zero. This chapter brings no new rules: it brings craft. How to diagnose the tone, where to start from, how fast to move it and what to do when what the dice say is not what the table feels. C·t6

In NOGUM, the GM is not an impartial physics simulator: they are the sound engineer at a mixing desk. Their job is not to apply the rules blindly, but to choose which rules apply in order to achieve the emotion they are after. If the previous chapters were the science, this one is the art. And art is about feelings, not maths: breathe.

The black box

This system is demanding for the GM, and only for the GM. The player does not even need to read the rulebook. T65

The player declares (narrative) → the GM checks the card, the Grid and the spec sheet (hidden mechanics) → the GM narrates the consequence

“You declare the action and roll. I’ll tell you what happens.” The GM is encouraged to interpret everything and speak in narrative terms. The curious player can peek behind the curtain as far as they like.

It asks a lot of the GM? That is a virtue, not a flaw. Other games are a point-and-shoot camera: anyone can take a decent photo, but they all look alike. NOGUM is an SLR on manual. You can mess it up, sure, but it is the only way to take exactly the photo you have in your head. T72

8.1 How to identify the tone (the diagnosis)

Before starting a campaign, or even a scene, ask yourself two questions to calibrate the axes. Do not ask “what are we playing?”, but “how does the world feel?”.

8.2 Default tone (the safety net)

What if you are not sure? What if your players just want to “play an RPG” without philosophising about narrative? NOGUM recommends a standard setup that works for the vast majority of adventures.

“Classic Adventure” mode (First Gear)

  • Neutral optimism. We only pay attention to one die. There is no noticeable curve. It is as simple as it gets.
  • Medium chaos. No curves and no extra rules: the search for tone goes unnoticed. Ideal for “I don’t want to stop and think about tone”.
  • Health on the Tactical zoom (9 boxes, 6.4). We want them to take damage, but not to drop from a single blow.

Tip: if you get stuck, put the desk in this mode. It is the autopilot. There is no rush: start exploring whenever you feel like it.

8.3 The three speeds of tone

Tone moves by campaign, arc, session, scene or character, within what was agreed in session zero (1.5). Here is the how. NOGUM’s modularity can be intimidating: do not try to mix live from day one. NOGUM grows with you.

SpeedHowExampleFor whom
1 · Campaign tone (static)You decide the tone in session zero, before making the sheets, and it is not touched.“We’re going to play a space horror campaign: Anguish card and health on the Lethal zoom.” The whole campaign, from the bar to the final boss, uses those rules.Recommended for starting out. Consistent and easy, although it can become monotonous.
2 · Per arc or per session (episodic)You change the tone when the setting, the chapter or the session changes.After five sessions of horror in the trenches, a gala party in the capital: “Today is an episode of intrigue and downtime: Realist card and mental damage.”Once the group is comfortable. The right not to switch

If you do not like modularity, this rulebook is still your home. Do not switch mid-game if you do not need to. It is perfectly valid to play a whole campaign in Epic, or always adding the dice together. NOGUM helps you find your tone: you set it up once at the start and, if you do not touch the knobs, it runs like clockwork. 8.4 Ludonarrative dissonance (the invisible enemy)

The greatest danger in roleplaying is when what the system says contradicts what the table feels. NOGUM gives you tools to fix it.

Symptom A · Comfortable laughter in horror
Problem: you narrate a horrific scene and the players crack jokes and roll the dice cheerfully. Mind the difference: nervous laughter is not the problem, it is horror working —whoever is having a bad time laughs—. The symptom is comfortable laughter, from someone who knows nothing is going to happen to them. Diagnosis: the system is too kind and they feel safe. Solution: if session zero allowed going that low, lower the optimism and announce the switch to the worst of 2d10. When a professional (+2) sees their success rate drop from 91 % to 49 % (Appendix B), the laughter will stop. Mechanical fear breeds narrative fear. ED-14
Symptom B · Boredom in combat
Problem: they have been slugging it out with the boss for an hour, have only knocked off half its health, and are looking at their phones. Diagnosis: you used the War zoom (27 points) when you did not need to, or chaos is too low and the same thing keeps happening. Solution: raise the chaos and the lethality. Switch to a smaller zoom so that the next blow, whoever lands it, ends the fight.
Symptom C · The table is tired (the human factor)
Problem: you have a dense, serious political investigation ready (low chaos), but it is Friday night, it has been a hard week and all they want is beer and explosions. Diagnosis: the tone of the universe clashes with the mood at the table. Golden rule: the table rules. Do not force drama if they are asking for comedy. Turn the dials, within what was agreed: make the investigation a car chase (raise the chaos) and the debate a bar brawl. NOGUM lets you save the session by adapting the game’s physics to your friends’ energy.

What is roleplaying? (According to game shops)

It looks like an obvious question, but we decided to put it at the start of the manifesto to remove any barrier to entry, and also as a chance to define how we understand this hobby.

Roleplaying is a ludic-narrative activity in which a group of friends gathers around a table to create a story together. There are so many roleplaying rulebooks that almost any list of their defining features will run into an exception somewhere.

Usually the table is clearly split between the players and the Game Master (in this manual, the GM): the person who writes the skeleton of the story and the players who will navigate it. Some games blur that difference.

It is also very common to have character sheets, which describe everything the characters can do, and rules that define when an action succeeds. A degree of chance is added to make events uncertain, so that the narration is not an imposition but a request for permission.

The rules, the dice, the hierarchy with the Game Master… every piece has a different flavour depending on which parts of the process of narrative creation the game designer finds most interesting. We are no exception. We chose which parts of the narrative matter most to us, and this manual has a very strong personality built around highlighting those elements.

What is roleplaying? (For NOGUM)

NOGUM was written to be a universal rulebook, but it is anything but naive in pursuing that goal. We have designed a manual that fits any narrative universe (from a country-house detective novel with ten suspects to a space opera of galactic empires). But it does not fit every way of roleplaying. Quite the opposite: the manual has a very clear personality stamped on it. Below we describe which points are non-negotiable if you want to enjoy a game with this manual. If any of them clashes head-on with your philosophy of play, it may be time to look for other options that resonate more with you.

The most famous dichotomy is the one separating tactical, simulationist games (old school) from narrative games. This game is clearly narrative. But that definition is a little vague. Let’s bring it down to earth:

The non-negotiable pillars

Story over dice
The rules exist to improve the narrative. If they go against the grain, they are ignored (chapter 0). We borrow the maxim: “roll only if every possible result is interesting” (0.6). T97 Your health is just a number: no protagonist is going to die because of a bad roll. Their death is narrative, and it is agreed (0.7).
Story over fairness
The above is about the rules, but it does not mean they can always be bent in the players’ favour. Sometimes it is the exact opposite: a dirty universe hits the players in ways the rule does not cover, and an epic universe makes concessions the rule does not cover either. This game is a gentlemen’s agreement. The players accept the ups and downs of the story, and the GM manages them always with everyone’s fun in mind, never using their position to make anyone uncomfortable. It takes sportsmanship to accept in advance what you are signing up for, backbone to keep the commitment when the results don’t go your way, and maturity to talk it through and steer the experience together. You can negotiate whatever you like to make the game more fun or the narrative more powerful, but never to demand “fairer” rules or more respect for the dice, because that is not what this manual is about. What you can argue is that the way the rules have been ignored is not fun or does not help the story.
Emotion as the pulse of the narrative
When we say everything is subordinate to the narrative, we mean something very specific: emotion. It is fun to point out that a game of the opera of stars should not have a Western revolver, but a laser pistol. But what really matters is whether the game feels like an epic, pulpy space opera, whether you can breathe the fun and optimism of the genre at every step. For this manual, the emotion of a genre must show in the way you narrate, in the rules… and in the GM’s liberties.
The pace must flow
Roleplaying, as this system understands it, is improvisation. Any quick solution to a problem will be better at the table than a far more nuanced, brainy and balanced one that forces the pace to break. The narrator must know this, allow and encourage quick decisions, and accept that a hasty decision is not always the wisest. We came here to play, not to sit an exam. An idea that is “brilliant for something improvised” must be rewarded in the game as a brilliant idea in that universe. An idea brought ready-made from home, or the result of 30 minutes of debate that interrupt the session, must be treated with all the ruthless rigour the GM can muster. Non-play is discouraged.
Grown-up table, liquid rules
Players who are adults (mentally, not on their ID card) and who collaborate with the GM instead of contesting every rule, because everyone’s goal is a coherent narrative, not a mathematically fair duel. The rules are liquid: they change according to what the scene asks for, and they are anchored by definition to a statistic that makes rolling dice meaningful. We are not afraid of combos: what a character can do is written, on every sheet, as a bonus from −2 to +4 (2.5), so no juggling done at home breaks the scale, and what counts as abusing the rules is agreed before you start, in the conversation we call session zero (0.4). What is this manual?

This book is not a finished product: it is a question. Or rather, it is the answer to a discomfort that has followed me for years as a designer and storyteller.

Over the last decade we have seen the rise of “universal narrative” systems. Games like the aspect-driven narrative systems or the philosophy of the move-based systems promised us the master key: a single ruleset to play any story. And they lied to us. Or at least they told us a half-truth.

These systems are universal in form (you can use them to fire a laser or swing a sword), but they are incredibly rigid in substance. Without you noticing, they bring their own tone from home.

The default-tone trap

Try playing a purist game of the antediluvian octopuses with an aspect-driven narrative system. You can’t. The system is mathematically designed so that characters are competent, proactive and able to overcome fate. However horrible the monsters you describe, the mechanics push towards pulp and adventure.

The opposite example, and the one that gave birth to this project, is the black comedy systems. Their “success at a cost” mechanic is brilliant for generating gritty drama and constant consequences. But try using it for a Silver Age superhero chronicle.

Picture the scene: you are a Sun God, a paragon of virtue and infinite power, stopping a mere bank robber. Silver Age fiction demands perfection: it is a formality, a clean display of superiority. But the system makes you roll, and the statistics say “partial success”. Suddenly the Sun God trips. Or the robber breaks his nose. Or a building collapses by mistake.

If we were playing the series about rotten superheroes, the one where the poster-boy paladins are worse than the villains, that result would be perfect. But in a classic heroic fantasy, the system had just destroyed the immersion. The rule was working against the narration.

Deconstructing tone

This manual tries to distil the elements that give a system its emotion. It was built to squeeze every one we found and isolate it, so that it only applies in a universe that shares the emotion that rule heightens. The manual offers a complete game (if you want the quick, ready-to-play, somewhat simplified rules, the accelerated version with all the recipes is at the end). But its main reason for existing is to give the GM tools to design their own rules and to start seeing their games from a new angle: tone.

Our first finding was that dice probability imprints very different moods. It is not just a matter of more or fewer successes, but also of how predictable the rolls are. If you have a high chance of success, it seems obvious that your result is predictable; but the nuance between optimism when rolling and certainty is not obvious at all. Let’s describe both.

  1. The Optimism axis: does the universe conspire to make you shine or to crush you?
  2. The Chaos axis: do actions have predictable consequences, or does absurd randomness reign?

Picture a fun but heroic universe, like that of the adventurer with the hat and the whip. The adventurer is never in real danger, but his actions sometimes cause unexpected tangles that take the plot to unforeseen and entertaining places: trouble you know he will get out of, but that adds an interesting twist. That universe calls for rules with high optimism and high chaos.

Now picture the novels of the antediluvian octopuses: an existential terror that chills the blood. There are no wild twists, tangles or unpredictability here. Only the suffocating atmosphere that constantly reminds you how fragile a human being is among deities that exist beyond time and space. When you hold a book of arcane magic, perhaps you should brace for the unexpected; the rest of the time, your steps will be predictably oppressive. Low chaos and low optimism.

This manual squeezes those two sensations and creates rules that increase the optimism or chaos felt in each setting.

We also explore how lethality completely changes the way characters face challenges, and how to adapt the total amount of health to the tone of the game. And the importance of numerically anchoring the world so that the roll never loses its meaning: NOGUM supports very different power levels with a numerical system that always stays coherent.

Finally, the manual aims to keep things simple and to avoid at all costs letting the rules steal the spotlight from the game. It gives the GM absolute freedom with an agile, intuitive modular scheme that demands minimal cognitive effort to navigate the tables. Not to make the game simple (this manual is not), but so that the GM can focus their efforts on what matters: discerning the tone and working out which set of rules the atmosphere calls for, not how to apply them.

The happy accident

This manual was first conceived as a modular toolkit that would let every GM design their campaigns with the right tools, whatever the tone of their universe. But by designing rule changes to be so simple and fluid, we made a revolutionary accidental discovery. Not only can you adapt the tone for each campaign: you can play an entire session with a different tone from the rest of the campaign (comic relief, a relaxed interlude between dark arcs). And the capacity for change is such that an advanced GM can adjust the tone in every scene… and even give different tones to each character at the same table. T7

These are the questions this manual asks. I found my answers; perhaps yours will be better. But at the very least, we hope we have helped you ask your games questions you had never asked them before.

The Six Ideas

The whole manual comes out of six ideas, one per chapter, from 0 to 5. The first two break reality; the next four anchor it. T179

Why start counting at zero? Because the first one is not a rule: it is the table. The other five work for it. T184

With the table and the tone you can turn a serious story into an absurd comedy or a lethal nightmare just by changing how the dice are read. But if the world is that volatile, what stops the character from dissolving? You need an anchor, and you need to separate, once and for all, how good you are from how big your deeds are, who they reach and what they cost you.

Six ideas that explain everything

  1. The Table Rules. The rules are tools for keeping tone and progress under control, and they are set aside when they get in the way of what the plot asks for. NOGUM is for adults: it puts up no firewalls against anyone who wants to abuse the rules. That is talked through before you start, at session zero, and the table can say “that doesn’t count”. (Chapter 0) T184 C·m1
  2. Tone is physics. NOGUM does not simulate the physics of objects but the physics of the script: which side the universe is on, and at what price. Both are read in the dice, with two d10s and seven cards that say how to read them, so the same character can change worlds without touching a single number. (Chapter 1) C·t1 C·t2
  3. The Anchor: what you know. The Anchor is your chance of success. What a character knows is written as a bonus that runs from −2 to +4, on every sheet, in any genre and in session one hundred; that is why the odds stay where they were designed. (Chapter 2) C·a1
  4. The Grid: what you are. The Grid tells you the size of the challenges you face. A brilliant boxer from a realistic world and a superpowered alien who screams and does martial arts can both have +4 in Brawling. The Grid says that one rolls to break a row of bricks, and the other to destroy a planet with a beam that shoots from his fingers. And it warns you of something else: the boxer stands no chance against the being from another world. He does not even have the right to roll. (Chapter 3) C·r1 C·r2
  5. The Key: who you can touch. A human being has no right to touch the alien… right? Well, unless that human has a contingency plan for everything and a green stone that takes the invader’s powers away for a few moments. That narrative permission to do things that sit at another level is what we call a Key. (Chapter 4) C·l1
  6. A single tally: what it costs. A character is built with points, and those points pay for the three previous ideas, in two purses: skill points, which buy what you know, and Keys, which buy what lets you touch what sits above you. Five Keys can be exchanged for a whole Grid level; what makes it whole, we will see in its chapter. Everything else you carry, from gear to powers, is paid for from the same tally and in the same two purses. (Chapter 5) C·e1 C·e3

Almost every question about the manual is settled by one of these six. If a rule seems strange to you, come back here: the rule comes from the idea, not the other way round. The hard cases, one by one, are in the clarifications at the end of each chapter and in the frequently asked questions.

Chapter 0 · The Table Rules

The rules are tools for keeping tone and progress under control: use them while they serve you, and ignore them when they stop you from doing something the plot asks for. Who decides what the plot asks for is the table, and it decides before playing, by talking. C·m1

Before writing the campaign, before choosing the module, before making a single sheet, the table sits down to talk. That conversation is session zero, and in NOGUM it is not a formality: it is where the gentlemen’s agreement of the introduction gets signed. Everything that comes after —the tone, the numbers, the pace at which you grow— comes out of here. T184 C·m2

The rules are vague on purpose: they leave gaps for the table to fill with judgement. If someone at the table is after something else —a tactical duel, an exact simulation—, this is the moment to find out, not session five.

Session zero has four conversations: the red lines (0.1), what is going to be felt (0.2), who proposes (0.3) and what doesn’t count (0.4). Two principles are agreed here: when to roll (0.6) and how a protagonist dies (0.7). And the numbers come out of all that (0.8), and so do the rules the rulebook does not have (0.9).

0.1 Everyone’s red lines

Nothing is imposed that makes anyone feel bad

Nothing that could make someone at the table genuinely feel bad goes into the game. It is not a vote, nor a list of forbidden topics: each person has their own red lines, and one is enough for it to be respected. It needs no justification, and it is not up for debate.

Lines are not only what is usually called strong content. They can be:

  • explicit sex or violence;
  • a specific subject that hits close to home for someone, or a phobia: spiders, enclosed spaces, deep water;
  • an oppressive atmosphere, or a campaign where nothing is ever going to go well;
  • or something as seemingly harmless as a long philosophical exploration of the meaning of your actions, which some find fascinating and others unbearably tedious.

They are all worth the same. That you love something does not oblige anyone to live through it.

Some lines are not said in front of everyone. They can be told privately to the GM, and that is enough: nobody has to explain to the table where each change comes from.

Some show up late. There are things you do not know will get to you until they come up. When that happens, they are removed, without asking permission and without explanations, and the game goes on.

What weighs is asked for first. Oppressing, frustrating, a plot that gives nobody a break: all of that can be a great game, but it is asked at session zero. And if it crosses a line for someone, it does not happen, even if everyone else wants it. Nobody drags anybody into an experience they do not want to live.

0.2 What we are going to feel

The second conversation is the one that looks most like choosing a genre, but it is not about genres: it is about emotions. Saying “horror” is not enough: you need to know which horror.

  • What is going to be felt at the table. Anguish, euphoria, laughter, melancholy, the vertigo of the epic.
  • What you want to explore, and what bores you. “I want existential dread explored.” “I want epic, and don’t bore me with family dramas.”
  • Which areas of the plot are respected. A past a player does not want touched, a relationship that does not break, a place that is sacred to the story.
  • What solutions the genre asks for. Are things solved here by investigating, fighting, talking, running? Does the fairground trick count? In a pulp game, the shark repellent pulled from the utility belt is a worthy scene and a good laugh. In a campaign that asked you to find out how to hurt the final enemy, getting rid of them with a cheap trick is something else.

With this the GM already knows which card to pick (chapter 1) and how far to take it. What is agreed is a range, not each scene: if the campaign can reach Nightmare, the darkest of the cards (1.4), the GM moves within what was agreed without asking every time (1.5, 8.3). Whatever goes beyond that goes back to the table.

0.3 Who proposes

It depends on the table and on how flexible its GM is. Usually the GM arrives with an idea —a world, a tone, a couple of images— and the table adjusts it. It can be the other way round, and the conversation can start from a blank page.

What you are after is consensus, always bearing in mind who does the hard work. The GM is going to write, prepare and sustain the campaign for months, and will not write a story well if they do not feel like telling it, however much the table asks for it. Their appetite counts as much as everyone else’s red lines. The players do not impose a campaign on the GM, and the GM does not impose on anyone what they do not want to live through.

0.4 “That doesn’t count”

This manual does not devote a line to firewalls against foul play: it is designed for adults, and what stops the cheap combo is the table. When someone says “that doesn’t count”, they are not attacking anyone nor arguing a rule: they are reminding everyone of what you agreed at session zero.

Adding up bonuses is allowed. Bonuses stack, as far as they can and at the table’s discretion, and anything above +4 is rolled at +4 (2.5), so sheets keep living between −2 and +4 and adding up breaks nothing. The cheap combo is abusing the rules: a Key —that permit to touch what sits at another level— left blank when the sheet is made and filled in, once the game has started, with exactly whatever solves each problem (4.4), and anything like it. We are not afraid of the cheating player, because here the goal is not to kill better: whatever is not narrative is agreed at session zero. C·m1 C·22oct

That is why that conversation has to exist, however short. Without it, “that doesn’t count” is one person’s opinion against another’s. With it, it is everyone’s word. It is said without drama, the scene is redone if needed, and the game goes on.

0.5 Going back to session zero

The agreement is not a closed contract. Anyone can ask to stop and go over it, and it is worth doing when an arc changes, when the tone changes or when someone has been left out of the story. Session zero is where that conversation already began.

0.6 Roll only if failing is interesting

You only roll if every possible result is interesting. Not positive, not desirable: interesting, at least as a possibility that gives merit and meaning to success. T97 C·m4

The question before every roll

Before picking up the dice: what happens if it fails? If the answer moves the story —a path that closes, a price, an enemy who finds out—, you roll. If failing leads nowhere, you don’t roll: it works, and play goes on.

The question is whether failing is interesting, not whether it is likely. Failure being numerically trivial is no reason to skip the roll: bonuses stay between −2 and +4 so that it is not, and a +4 also fails now and then, on every card. If what you add up goes above +4, you roll at +4 (2.5). C·a2

Picking a lock with no hurry and nobody watching: if it fails, you try again, and you don’t roll. Picking it with the patrol rounding the corner: you roll, because failing changes the scene. The lock is the same; what has changed is what failing costs.

When you can no longer fail, you roll to see how. There are scenes where nobody doubts you are going to win —the nameless extras, the mooks, the enemy who has ended up a Grid level below you and no longer has the right to roll—, but the how does matter. That is what Style is for: a +2 you roll when you can no longer fail (3.5).

0.7 Death is agreed

No protagonist dies from a roll. A roll can take them out of the fight; death is agreed, with arc-level reasons. C·m3

Players own their lives

The rules can only take a protagonist out of the fight. Out of the fight means being incapacitated and leaving the scene (6.4); whether you wake up the next day is decided by the tone of the campaign or the player’s will, not by the dice. T176

A protagonist’s death is narrative, not the product of luck. The same goes for whatever, in other games, takes a character away without killing them: madness, transformation, or passing into the GM’s hands as a monster, villain or avatar. They only arrive if they are agreed (6.5). ED-12

Weapons don’t carry it written on them either. However big it is, a blow takes you out of the fight (4.5). What size does do is make things hard for the table.

It is talked through at session zero: whether a protagonist can die in this campaign, and how that is decided. Usually death comes when the story asks for it and the player accepts it: the sacrifice, the end of the arc, the farewell everyone saw coming. If a table prefers something else —a night of horror where the dice do kill—, that is also an agreement, and it is made before playing, with everyone on board. In some horror universes it is a key piece, especially in one-session games. C·22oct

The empire’s battle moon has just blown up your planet. So how exactly am I supposed to say it didn’t kill you? By agreeing on it, and with a good argument. C·l8

0.8 And the numbers come out of that

Only after all of the above do you touch the mechanics, and almost everything follows on its own. Each thing is explained in its place; this is the list, so you forget none:

  • The tone: which card is used (chapter 1), how many blows a character can take before falling (6.4) and how far the campaign can go (8.3).
  • The Grid ceilings: what level a character can start at, what is the biggest thing that exists in your world and how far the players will be able to go. It is looked at separately for the physical, the mental, the social, the technical and the arcane, with one question: is it possible in your world? (3.7). C·r6
  • How the Grid reads: whether one more level really is a hundred times more power, as in worlds that resemble the real one, or whether it only says who the story is about (3.2). T188
  • What you start with: how many skill points each character has, a separate extra chosen between one more Grid level or five Keys (the pot), and the starting Grid level, which can be raised or lowered according to what you want to emulate (5.2). C·a5 C·r5 C·e4
  • Advantages and flaws: the traits that are not skills, such as night vision or a phobia; whether you start with any (5.8). C·22oct
  • The pace of the two purses: how many points and how many Keys are earned over the campaign, and who grows (5.1, 5.8). C·e3
  • What can be lost: besides physical health, mental, spiritual or whatever the genre asks for, and which of those losses can take a character out of the story (6.5).
  • The optional rules you want to use, from the Rules Chest in chapter 9 (9.1).

0.9 Your table’s own rules

Sooner or later the fiction asks for something the rulebook does not have. Then you write a rule, the way you write a sheet: what it does, when it applies and how it is rolled. If it involves chance, it is read with your card’s die, like any roll (1.3). If it grants power, it is paid for on the scale it touches, in points or in Keys (5.3): being the table’s own does not make it free. C·29oct

And it is written down, so the next session says the same: on the sheet, if it belongs to one character, or on the table’s list, if it applies to everyone. The App keeps a place for them, “Table rules”, and shows them to its assistant. An example: “Viper”’s Humanity pays a d10 divided by three every time she transforms, a rule her table invented (9.5).

Chapter 1 · Tone (the physics of emotion)

Tone is the physics of your world: which side the universe is on, and at what price. NOGUM puts it in the dice, which is why the same sheet, read with another card, lives in another story. C·t1 C·t2

Now we get down to business. This chapter gives shape to the concept from the introduction: how dice statistics influence the emotion of the roll, and how that mood when the dice are thrown influences the feel of the whole table.

In the real world, gravity is a constant: 9.8 m/s². If you drop an apple, it falls. Always. In fiction, gravity is an opinion. If an action hero jumps off a building, he lands on an awning and survives. If a supporting character in a horror film does the same, he breaks his neck.

The physics hasn’t changed: the universe’s intention has. NOGUM does not simulate the physics of objects; it simulates the physics of the script. To that end, before you touch a single die, I am handing you control of the two invisible engines that govern that reality.

1.1 The two axes of reality

Imagine you have a mixing desk in front of you with two main faders. By moving them, you change how it “feels” to exist in your world.

Y axis · Optimism (the Will)

It answers the question: how much does the universe want the protagonists to triumph?

  • High optimism (The Chosen One). The universe is your accomplice. Coincidences are happy ones: the guard looks the other way just as you pass, enemy shots only graze shoulder pads. Example: the one with the hat and the whip leaping onto a moving lorry. He knows he won’t miss the jump; the only tension is seeing how great it looks.
  • Neutral optimism (Indifference). The universe is deaf and mute. It doesn’t hate you, but it won’t save you. Things are fair. Example: real life, or a serious historical novel.
  • Low optimism (Hostility). The universe is an active enemy. Murphy’s law rules: if something can go wrong, it will go worse. The floor creaks just as you try to be stealthy. Example: the last survivor on the ship trying to escape; everything she touches breaks, jams or betrays her.

X axis · Chaos (Predictability)

It answers the question: how stable is the relationship between cause and effect?

  • Low chaos (Order / Predictable). Reality is a Swiss watch. If you are good at something, you do it well almost every time. There are no absurd surprises and consequences are logical. Example: the consultant from Baker Street. Everything has a deductive explanation; if you shoot, the bullet flies straight.
  • Medium chaos (Standard). There is room for plot twists and surprise, but within a coherent frame.
  • High chaos (the unpredictable). Reality is liquid and the improbable happens constantly. A shot can ricochet three times and hit you in the foot, or you can hit an impossible target with your eyes closed. Example: the motor-mouthed mercenary or Alice in Wonderland: physical logic bows to the joke or the madness of the moment.

Pessimism is not chaos

Things going wrong and things going weird look alike, but they are not the same. The “why does the plan fail?” test T60:

  • Low optimism: the heist fails because the enemy is smart, competent and cruel (one guard too many, a state-of-the-art alarm). Feeling: oppression. Things go consistently wrong.
  • High chaos: the heist fails because of something absurd (a cat jumps on the panel, a sneeze, an expired explosive that goes “pff”). Feeling: loss of control. Things go unexpectedly weird.

And real life is neutral optimism with low chaos: fair and predictable, not chaotic. T4

1.3 The roll

The whole mechanic fits on one line: you read the dice as the card says, add what your character brings (their bonus) and compare it with the TN, the target number you need to reach. The card supplies the rest. C·t1

The roll

Dice reading (as the tone card says) + total bonus ≥ the card’s TN → success

How to read any card

  1. Roll both d10s, one red and one blue, even when only the red one counts. The card tells you which number you use: the red one only, the sum, the higher or the lower.
  2. Look at that number before adding anything: if it falls in the fumble band, it is a fumble; if it falls in the critical band, it is a critical. Each card marks which dice results are its fumble band (you fail, and something gets complicated as well) and which are its critical band (you get it, and further than you were aiming for): below. The bands take priority over everything else. ED-1 ED-18
  3. If there is no fumble and no critical, add your total bonus —what you can do (if several things on your sheet apply, the highest counts), how hard the task is, gear and whatever helps or hinders you— and compare it with the card’s TN. All of that, added up, lives between −2 and +4: it is the Anchor (2.1, 2.5). C·a1 C·a2
  4. On two cards, Unleashed Pulp and Black Comedy, just making it costs you: if the total equals the TN or beats it by one, you get there and the GM picks the price: that is success at a cost. ED-23

Two dice, four readings

The active card says how the two dice are read:

  • Red only (1–10): a flat die, where every face is worth the same. It is the hobby’s habit, neutral chaos.
  • The sum (2–20): results pile up in the middle, luck loses weight and competence gains it. It is order.
  • The best of the two: the universe pushes upwards. It is optimism.
  • The worst of the two: any 1 can ruin the roll. It is terror.

The chaotic column of the quadrant widens the fumble band on top of those readings (on Black Comedy, the critical band too), and two of its cards add success at a cost. T14

The TN does not move

The TN is 6, and 11 when the dice are added. What changes from one task to another is the difficulty, and the difficulty is a modifier on the roll: +2 easy, 0 standard, −2 hard, −4 heroic (2.5). Nobody has to remember a different bar in every scene. The only exception is declared: Tragedy, which plays on the Realist card and sets the TN at 14 (1.4). C·t3 C·22oct

Fumbles, criticals and success at a cost

Every card brings its own fumble and critical bands, narrow, normal or wide, with the exact numbers written down (1.4). C·t4

A fumble is failure and complication; a success at a cost is success and complication. The complication can be the same and weigh the same in both cases: what changes is whether you also got what you wanted, and how much it hurts is decided by whoever is narrating, according to the tone and the table. A critical goes where you were already going, and further: nobody has to invent anything (in combat it also adds one to the damage: 6.2).

The price of success at a cost can be time, a resource, position or friendly fire (6.6). You come out ahead just as often as without it: what changes is how many of those times you pay. C·t5 ED-23

And before all of this, the question from 0.6: if failing is not interesting, you don’t roll.

1.4 The Tone Cards (the Control Panel)

Seven cards, one per square of the quadrant. Each one is the whole rule of the roll while it is on the table. C·t2

It works out as often as on First Gear up to +3 (at +4, 80 % against 90 %), but nothing is safe and nothing is free: fumble, cost and critical all sit near 20 % with almost any sheet. It is not a ball growing towards something: it is a leaf in the wind fighting to land anywhere at all and never managing it. Each single toll looks like something silly, and three scenes later nobody can say when it went wrong. It is the one that asks most of the GM: four rolls in ten bring something to invent.

And it is called comedy on purpose. A world like this frustrates from two directions: because nothing comes off clean, and because what lies underneath is usually ugly. The card carries one of them, the heavier one. The darkness does not come from the die: it comes from a life that ends in a few blows (Lethal health, 6.4) and from whatever background you have put underneath. That is why a grim cyberpunk game and a comedy of errors use the same card without contradicting each other.

The same roll as Anguish: it works out exactly as often. What changes is that on eighteen rolls in a hundred the failure starts to speak. And at +4 something striking happens: clean failure disappears, so the grand master no longer fails: they fumble.

A warning: chaos frustrates

The three cards in the chaotic column only work if the table has agreed that what you are chasing is the story, not success, because much of the time you will not get there. Nightmare is built for one dirty night or a short scene inside a campaign; a long campaign on it wears people out. Say so before you start and nobody will feel punished.

Where the oppressive row comes from

It is not a theoretical corner of the map: it is the emulation of a family of games that exists and gets played. Look at what Anguish does —9 % at −2, 25 % at 0, 49 % at +2 and 81 % at +4— and compare it with the antediluvian octopuses, where an untrained skill sits around 5–20 %, an ordinary investigator around 25–35 % and a specialist in their field around 70–80 %. It is the same distribution. What is stored in the character sheet there is stored in the card here, which is why you can step in and out of that tone without rewriting a character. The difference is the spread: there the specialist has their corner where they do land things, and here the roll is equally hard for everyone. That is why it works better in short form —one night, one act, one scene—, just like those one-night horror games where the candles go out one by one or blocks come out of a tower until it falls.

A recipe apart: Tragedy

Tragedy is the declared exception to the TN that does not move (1.3), and it is a recipe, not a card: the Realist’s sum with the bar set at 14, declared out loud. The same clockwork world, with hope out of reach. As with the other darkest tones, we strongly recommend not using it for long. Its table is in Appendix B. C·22oct

1.5 Tone moves

Tone is not chosen once and for all. It can change per campaign, per arc, per session, per scene or per character, within the range agreed at session zero (0.2). C·t6 T7

  • Per campaign: decided at session zero and left alone. It is the simplest option, and the recommended one to start with.
  • Per arc or per session: the setting changes and the card changes; a comic interlude between two dark arcs.
  • Per scene: the card follows the music and the tension, and changes as you cross the boss’s door.
  • Per character: at the same table, the chosen hero rolls on the Epic and their cursed companion on Nightmare.

If you do not know where you are, First Gear is the safety net (8.2). And nobody is obliged to move anything: a whole campaign on the same card is as NOGUM as any other. How to change without breaking immersion, and what to do when two cards clash or the rule and the story pull in different directions, is in chapter 8 (8.3, 8.4).

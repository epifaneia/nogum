Chapter 4 · Who You Can Touch (the Key)

A Key is a whole Discipline that, against any target, puts you one or more Grid levels above your own. What rules is what the Key says: it cancels what it says it cancels. C·l1 C·l2 C·l3

The Grid says what you are across a whole Sphere (chapter 3). The Key says who you can touch with one of its Disciplines. Everything else in this chapter comes out of that sentence, and what each thing costs lives in chapter 5.

4.1 What a Key is

In the fiction, a Key is any explanation that lets you interact with a higher level of the Grid: an object, a piece of information, a tactic, an ally. Without it, you have no right to roll against whoever stands above you (3.1). With it, you roll with your sheet and with the game’s tone, with no penalty at all: you are still the best at what you do. T28

The Key, in three lines

  • It covers a whole Discipline. Athletics, Acrobatics, Stealth, Sleight of Hand: the entire Discipline, with all its Signatures inside (2.3). C·l3
  • Against any target. A Stealth Key works against the guard, against the camera and against the dog. Against one single thing, it is a different Key, and a cheaper one: the Limited Key (4.3). C·l1 C·l4
  • It puts you one or more levels above your own, by paying several Keys (4.3). In that roll you act as if that were your Grid, unless the Key is limited. C·l2

At the table: you are level 1 and you use your Weapons Key against someone at level 1. The Key puts you at 2, and they have no right to roll to stop it. Against anything; that is what you paid for. If how you pull it off matters, you roll Style (3.5). Against someone at level 2, the Key gives you the roll you did not have before, with your full bonus (4.2). C·l2 C·r10

The Key does not change what you are. Outside that Discipline you are still at your Grid, and your bonus is still your bonus, from −2 to +4 (2.1): the Key raises your level, not your trade. The Grid puts you above across a whole Sphere; the Key, in one of its Disciplines. That is why covering a Sphere by hand takes eight Keys, one per Discipline, and why the Grid, which is the whole bundle, comes cheaper: five (5.3).

Three Keys in the fiction

  • The barbarian of the steppe can fight hundreds of “normal” men at once, and a rank-and-file soldier has no right, on paper, to so much as scratch him. But he is mortal: after the battle he drinks mead and falls into a sleep as deep as the bowels of the Earth. A rank-and-file soldier who catches him in his bed has the Key, and can harm someone who, without it, would be out of his reach.
  • The vigilante millionaire versus the alien in tights and briefs. A mortal against a god, but the alien has the most famous weak spot in comics: the stone from his own world. If the millionaire manages to use it, they face each other as equals. It is the Key that unlocks the feat of fighting a god.
  • A hacker is a mortal, but their computer lets them black out an entire building: level 3 feats (4.8). If they summon every hacker in a large anonymous collective and coordinate an attack on the country’s financial centres, that attack is a Key to level 5. The effects are far greater, but the roll is the usual one, with the full bonus.

All three are Keys given by the fiction, the kind that last one scene (4.4). The ones you buy work the same way: what changes is whose they are and how long they last.

4.2 It cancels what it says it cancels

By default, a Key does what the Grid would do: whoever is below its level has no right to roll. But what rules is what the Key says. If it says it cancels something, that thing is no longer rolled; what it does not mention, it does not touch, and that is still rolled. A Key is, deep down, a permit granted by the table, and it is granted on the terms in which it is written. C·l2 C·22oct

The grenade

A grenade is a level 2 Key: the damage of a whole room (4.8). That is what it says, and nothing more.

  • There is no roll to take it. Against whoever is at level 1, neither body nor armour is any use: that is cancelled.
  • Dodging it is hard, but it can be done. The Key does not rule it out so flatly: there is a dodge roll.
  • Against the next level, it gives a roll there was not before. A level 2 beast with plenty of natural armour can be hurt by the grenade. Your knuckleduster does not even tickle it.

And since it is single use, the grenade you throw and use up is not paid for, even though it is a level 2 Key (5.4). C·l2 C·22oct

Dodge, soak and damage

With weapons, what is cancelled is stated explicitly, and there are three things to cancel: the dodge, getting out of the way so it does not hit you; the soak, or the block, taking the blow with your body, your armour or your shield; and the damage, how much whatever reaches you does to you. Every weapon carries them written as three tags, Dodge, Soak and Damage, which apply this same rule; how they are played comes with combat (6.3). The last two are closely related, but they are not the same: some weapons cannot be soaked because their damage is massive, like the grenade; others go through armour and deal less damage, like armour-piercing rounds or the monofilament katana. That is why a higher-level Key does not, on its own, leave the one below defenceless: it takes away the defence it names, and the other one is rolled. C·22oct

Every defence a weapon cancels is a trait, and every trait, a Key, without charging twice for the same concept. A plain pistol does not make dodging it hard: at level 1, a bullet cannot be dodged. It cancels the dodge, and pays one. The solved cases are in the Bazaar’s workshop, the catalogue of objects and powers in chapter 9 (9.3). C·e8 C·22oct

Upwards: with the permit, the full bonus

The counter-intuitive part goes the other way. Against whoever is above you, with the Key you roll with your full bonus, with no penalty and no added difficulty because they stand higher. Nothing is protected twice: the Grid was already their protection, and the Key is exactly what lifts it. C·r10

The Key covers what it covers. The Key, or the Keys, say what kind of opportunity you are buying: a weapon that wounds them even if they protect themselves, or an opening in the scene —an ambush, a surprise—, which is a single opportunity. In what it covers, you stand level and roll normally (6.2). In what it does not cover, they are still above you: when their turn comes, their blow lands on you as on anyone below, without a roll, unless your Key also covers your defence. T176 T183 C·22oct

To defend yourself against something above you, the Key has to cover the Discipline you defend with —Acrobatics to dodge, Fortitude to take it—, or be a momentary Key given by the fiction (4.4). With a Weapons Key you hit the vampire; its swipe you dodge with another. And a sword gives you no cover against the dragon’s breath: surviving it is Fortitude, and you bought Weapons. T176 T183

4.3 Climbing more than one level

One Key puts you one level above. To climb further, they stack, and the ladder is square: climbing n levels costs n² Keys. That is the full price: the whole Discipline, against everything. C·l4 ED-32 T183

The reach is the tangible reading’s, two zeros per level (3.2); in the narrative reading, each rung means whatever it means on your scale. With the five Keys you can start with (5.2), one Discipline gets to two levels above —four Keys— with one to spare; the third rung, which is nine, is earned in play, or with whatever extra the table agrees to hand out before the first session (5.2). The Grid climbs the same ladder, with the whole Sphere: 5·n² Keys, against the 8·n² it would cost to cover it Discipline by Discipline (5.3).

The Limited Key

Against one single thing —vampires, machines, this monster—, or only for part of what the Discipline does —only the damage—, the Key is Limited, and cheaper: half (5.5). Climbing two levels against one single thing costs 2 instead of 4. C·l4

At one level, limiting it saves nothing. You can write it as “Weapons against vampires”, and it will work against vampires and not against the werewolf. But anything you keep for good costs at least one Key (5.4), so it costs you the same as the whole Weapons Key, which works against everything. If you are going to be charged the same, limiting it is not recommended. Keys against one single thing are usually the kind the plot lends, like holy water (4.4). C·22oct

If you want faster growth and Grids that are less out of reach, the Rules Chest carries a flatter ladder, 3n−2, as an optional rule (9.1). C·22oct

What matters at the table is whose it is and how long it lasts. There are three kinds. C·l6

Own Key
The one you buy: with the five Keys you can start with (5.2) or with the Key purse, which by default gives one per session per head (5.1). It is permanent. It is written on the sheet with its Discipline and its level when you buy it, or the first time you use it (blank, below). Nobody can take it from you. If it comes in an object, it lives in the object, and when you let go of it you get back what you paid, to spend on something else (5.7). T176 T185 C·l6
Borrowed Key
Given by the GM for the plot or to reward ingenuity: “it occurs to you to use the holy water”. It is not paid for, and it reaches as far as the fiction says; it is not improved with Keys. It goes on the sheet, but in pencil: it lives in the scene and leaves with it. ED-28
Momentary Key
Set up by the fiction: twenty mercenaries at once, the hacker club, your sister, your contact in the police. They are your excuse and your ability, not buttons: on your own you hack a room, with your club a building, but the club has to be there, and your contact may be on holiday. It lasts one scene and raises whatever the fiction says. It is not growing: when the scene ends you go back to your level, and whatever you want to keep for good is earned with experience (5.8). And the fiction holds it up: if tomorrow there are three of you mercenaries, the Key is gone. T184 T185

The Key is as big as the scene says. It can be Limited or far more powerful, and its price runs from half to triple (5.5). The borrowed and the momentary Key last one scene and are narrative, so they have no cost and need no further definition. With that distinction the GM can keep handing out ideas without inflating the character, and whoever paid for theirs knows it is theirs; the ones you buy climb the ladder (4.3). C·l5

Blank

An own Key can be kept unwritten and decided the first time it is used. It is the shark repellent from 0.2: in a pulp game, having it come out of the belt just in time is a worthy scene; if you use it to get rid of the campaign’s enemy, who asked you to find out how to hurt them, it may be the cheap combo that 0.4 talks about, and anyone can say “that doesn’t count”. C·l7 T184

As open as the fiction

That what the Key says is what rules also means that a Key can say almost anything the table knows how to read, not just “one level more”. Majestic Presence, a power from the Bazaar (9.3), says that nobody of a lower level can attack you without first passing a Mental roll. It does not contradict the Grid: it does not take the roll away, it puts a door in front of it. And for whoever is below, passing that roll can be their Key. C·22oct

The Key, from start to finish

It is born own, borrowed or momentary, and it climbs the ladder, or whatever the fiction says. It has no ceiling: ceilings belong to the Grid, and Keys are better left without one (3.7). Turning down a jump on the Grid gives you Keys, yours to spend wherever you like (5.8). And it goes according to its kind: the own Key stays, the equipment one is recovered when you let go of the object (5.7), and the borrowed and the momentary one leave with their scene. 4.5 The Reaper

The Reaper is a Key to damage. If it reaches you and you are below its level, you are out of the fight, with no right to roll. C·l8

A weapon’s damage is written with a small number, which goes up to 2, and that is deliberate: what tells a weapon of war apart from a knife is its other tags, not a bigger number (6.3). C·c2 What lies above that is not a better weapon: it is an event. A siege gun, a car at seventy, a dragon’s breath, a bomb. We call that the Reaper, and it works like the Grid, not like damage:

The Reaper

Every Reaper carries a Grid level. If it reaches you and you are below that level, there is no damage to count and no body or armour that helps: you are out of the fight, in whatever way suits what has reached you (below). Whether it can be dodged is what its tag says, the dodge one, Dodge: the sniper you cannot see (Dodge: None) cannot be dodged; a grenade, because of its area, can barely be dodged (Dodge: Hard (−2)). Against anyone at its level, it deals a weapon’s highest damage, Damage 2, with normal defence, and plays like any other blow. Against anyone above it, nothing without a Key. T170 T176 T181 C·l8

It is the Grid’s own sentence (3.1): below the level, no right to roll, and the Key is the agreed exception. Here, what gets no roll is staying on your feet.

Why it is a Key. Without a Key, you deal damage at your own height. The Reaper reaches the next level of the Grid, and that is exactly what Keys do. Even though it is damage, it is also a permit, and that is why the Reaper above your level is paid for, although damage, on its own, is not (5.4). It is a Key Limited to damage (4.3), and its levels go up the ladder: a Reaper 2 in the hands of a level 1 raises them one; a Reaper 3, two. What it costs in each case is in the Bazaar’s workshop (9.3). C·22oct T183

It covers damage, not hitting: the bazooka. The bazooka is a Key to damage, and that is where it stops. If you hit the tank, the tank feels it even if you are level 1; if it hits you, it blows you apart, because that cannot be taken on the chest. But it has to hit: the missile can miss, and the target can take cover. A homing missile with your face in its memory would be something else: it covers damage and hitting, and it cannot be stopped or dodged. To survive it you need another Key, and it can be a narrative one: your hacker friend cuts its guidance for a second, and in that second you can dodge. C·22oct

What cannot be dodged is avoided. A Reaper that cannot be dodged (Dodge: None) is not a combat problem, it is a fiction problem. T181 It is solved by not being there, by cutting the fuse, by talking down whoever fires it, or by climbing the Grid before it arrives. A GM who points a Reaper at a character who cannot take it is not proposing a fight: they are announcing that the character is leaving it. Let that show at the table, and let it be seen coming.

And it exists on all three bars. A character’s health is kept in bars —the physical, the mental, the spiritual— (6.4), and the Reaper can arrive through any of them (6.5). The physical one floors you. The mental one takes you out of the scene through your head, and also through your face: there is evidence, and tomorrow you are nobody, because the social goes to the Mental bar ED-11. The spiritual one takes whatever you carry inside, whatever your world calls it. It does not empty the bar: it crosses it out. And whatever comes after is agreed by the table, not by the Reaper.

In a cartoon world the Reaper exists too, and it floors you too: for two panels. The anvil falls, the character goes flat, and in the next scene they are whole. What gets crossed out there is their dignity.

4.6 Permits

The sheet is a closed list (2.3); the world is not. What no skill can do —flying, seeing through walls, breathing where you cannot— is a permit, and a permit is a matter of Keys: by default, one. C·l9 ED-37

The test is a question: does some Discipline already do it? Seeing better is a bonus, not a permit, because there is already a Discipline for seeing (5.4). Seeing through a wall is something no Discipline does: that is a permit. If something grants a permit or puts you above your level, it has Keys, whether it comes from a shop, a workshop or a 3D printer; and if it is for good, it costs at least one, however cheap it is in the shop (5.6).

Invisibility shows the whole thing. No Discipline turns you invisible: it is a permit, a Key, and its price goes up or down with its straps, the conditions that tie it down: a cloak you can take off is not worth the same as turning invisible whenever you like. We will see it with numbers when we talk about the Adjustment (5.5). But being undetectable by any means is no longer a permit: it makes you beat everything that keeps watch, and that is Stealth two levels above, against everything, 2² = 4 Keys (4.3). T179 C·l4

The rest is vague on purpose: depending on its conditions, a permit can cost anywhere between half and triple, more or less by rule of thumb (5.5), and if a power does several things in a limited way, the total can be divided, but it does not go below 1. The tally is in 5.4, and the example powers, in the Bazaar (9.3). C·l9

4.7 Spells

A point in Arcane is the requirement; the spell is the permit, and it is paid for with Keys. C·l10

The Discipline is bought like any other: throwing fire is learnt just like wielding a sword (Arcane → Gift → Projection), with points (2.2). That is how well you do it. What the spell does that no skill can —turning invisible, opening a door to somewhere else, speaking with spirits— is its permit, and it goes in Keys, like any other (4.6). T54

How many depends on what you are emulating. Limited, at half price, if the rulebook you emulate makes magic hard —mana, components, faith, hours of ritual—; whole if it is at will; in between, the table decides (5.5). C·l10

Having Arcane is already a jump, but it does not make you level 2: a spell on one person is level 1 (3.3). If the spell reaches higher —a whole room, a building—, it is also a level Key, up the ladder (4.3). And if it is damage, it is damage: the fireball that sweeps a room is a Reaper 2 (4.5), and what is paid for is the Key, not the damage. C·22oct

4.9 Clarifications

Is it Grid or a Key? If you are big across the whole Sphere, Grid; if in one Discipline, a Key. The test, with both its faces, is in 3.1.

Can I put the Key on my Signature? There is no need: the Key covers the whole Discipline, and your Signature goes inside it. With a Weapons Key, your Pistols rise too. If we sliced it any finer, a Sphere would stop being worth eight Keys and the tally that exchanges Keys for Grid would stop adding up (5.3).

I am level 2 and the enemy is a level 1 human with a sheet. It is a power jump: your blow goes in without a roll, and Style says how; he, against you, needs a Key. Having a sheet means he is not scenery (3.5), but it does not stop him being below you. T181 C·22oct

Does the werewolf pay for its wolf form? For the ability to transform, yes, because it is theirs for good; for a momentary transformation lent by the plot, no (4.4). How it is counted, in 9.1. C·22oct

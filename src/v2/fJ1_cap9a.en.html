Chapter 9 · The Toolbox

This chapter brings no new rules: it brings the usual ones, already assembled. Modules, tables, lists, equipment and recipes, built only from the pieces in chapters 0 to 8. It is not read straight through: it is looked up.

So far you have read the theory: why the dice have emotions, why every sheet lives between −2 and +4 and why health is a Russian doll. This is the toolbox. It is also where powers, which the core leaves vague on purpose, are brought down to earth with examples (4.6, 5.5).

The order is no accident. It goes from the general to the specific and, finally, to the application:

  1. 9.1 Rules Chest: optional modules borrowed from other rulebooks (the general), with their problem tables alongside.
  2. 9.2 Skills list: the complete pyramid and the catalogue of Signatures (the specific).
  3. 9.3 The Multiverse Bazaar: the equipment lists, with their stat block (the specific).
  4. 9.4 Rulebook Emulation: twenty recipes that emulate famous universes, and the sample case files (the application).

The rules and the lists come before the emulations for a reason: no smoke and mirrors. The twenty recipes in 9.4 have been built solely from pieces that appear in this rulebook: tone cards, damage zoom, Grid, Keys, tags and the modules in the chest. If a recipe needs something, that something is in these pages. T117

And a warning of principle: here we emulate lore, not engines. You will not find recipes for playing “in the style of” another universal system. We emulate worlds, stories and feelings; we do not emulate the emulators. T125

Why is there no recipe for “how to play it like the aspect-driven narrative games” or “like an old-school simulator”? Because they already have their own rulebooks, and because NOGUM is not just another universal system: it is the machine that explains why each of them sounds the way it does. If you are coming from one of them, at the end of 9.4 there is a Rosetta Stone so you don’t get lost.

The Multiverse Bazaar (9.3) has its own section.

9.1 Rules Chest

The chest is a catch-all: every rule from other rulebooks that might bring something to the table, from narrative clocks to flashback rolls. None of them is mandatory. They are pieces the GM takes out of the chest when the story calls for them and puts back when they stop being useful. T117

Each module states what it does, which 9.4 recipes it appears in and what its status is. The concept of each module is named by the author in their recipes; the specific figures are the assistant’s proposals, which the author has not yet validated, and they are flagged as such.

Each module says what it does, which 9.4 recipes it appears in, how much attention it asks of the table and when it gets in the way.

House rule for the chest: if pulling out a module means explaining it for ten minutes, it wasn’t meant for this table. Put it back. The chest serves the story, not the other way round.

Progress clocks

Progress clock

Draw a circle split into 4, 6 or 8 segments depending on how long the goal is (an infiltration, a chase, the end of the world). Each success on a roll that pushes towards the goal fills 1 segment; each critical, 2. Each failure on a risky roll fills a segment of the opposing clock (the villains’ one: the alarm, the ritual, the tide). Whoever fills their clock first wins. Aprobado por el autor T117

  • Travel clock. It measures the road, not the fight: each leg of the journey fills segments and each setback (storm, ambush, despair) costs them. It is the heart of recipe 3. T127
  • Supply clock. It replaces bookkeeping of arrows and rations. It only comes out when scarcity is the theme of the scene (lost in the desert); the rest of the time, the heroes have packed sandwiches. Aprobado por el autor T119

The core already uses a clock without calling it one: the arterial bleeding at threshold 2 (6.6) is a 3-turn clock.

Recipes: 3 (“The Melancholy Stroll”), 15 (“Thieves in the Fog”). T176

Flashbacks

Retroactive planning

A player can interrupt the present to narrate a scene from the past that solves the current problem (“I don’t have the key… but yesterday I bribed the guard”). The flashback is not paid for: it is rolled.

  • Roll whatever skill the plan calls for against the active card’s TN.
  • Success: the flashback happened, and you have what you went for.
  • Every point above the TN buys one more detail you can slip inside it: something you left ready, an exit, somebody placed exactly where you needed them.
  • Failure: the plan existed, but it went crooked and you find out now.
  • Fumble: the past gives you away; the fumble menu (6.2) decides how.

That way the flashback is a scene with risk, which is what the table wants it for. Decisión del autor

Recipes: 15 (“Thieves in the Fog”), where flashbacks are mandatory for planning the heist.

Devil’s bargain

Risk for power

Before an important roll, the GM offers a deal: the action is resolved with Best of 2d10 against TN 6 (as on the Epic card), whatever card is active. In exchange, come what may, the GM applies a guaranteed narrative consequence, chosen from the menu of the 4 costs (6.6). The player may turn the bargain down. Aprobado por el autor T117

In NOGUM you do not add dice: the power of the bargain is shifting the reading towards optimism for one roll. ED-R1

Recipes: none of the twenty uses it. It is designed for horror and dark drama.

Exploding damage

Chained criticals

When the attacker rolls a critical, on top of their +1 to damage (6.2), they roll the same reading again. If they roll another critical, they add another +1, and so on. The damage is still subject to the weapon’s and the defender’s tags. Aprobado por el autor T120

It is the “unleashed pulp” version of the single damage maths. Don’t confuse it with the Reaper of the core (4.5), which is a property of the weapon and does not depend on the dice.

Recipes: none of the twenty; it appears in the Rosetta Stone (frantic pulp action).

Inverse mystic Grid

To know is to lose your mind

In this module, forbidden knowledge hurts. Every time a character learns or uses forbidden lore (Occultism, any skill in the Arcane Sphere), they take Mental Damage 1 Dodge: None, even if the roll is a success. A critical is a full revelation: Mental Damage 2. The more you know, the closer you are to being out of the game. Aprobado por el autor T120

Recipes: 12 (“The Deadly Library”: knowledge = Mental damage).

Destiny (Wyrd)

Your death is written

When creating the character, the player writes down how and when they will die (a narrative condition, not a date: “when I see the sea of my childhood again”). Until that moment comes, the character rolls their survival actions with the Epic card, even if the table is playing with another one. When the Wyrd is fulfilled, they switch to the Tragedy recipe (1.4) until the end of the scene. It is per-character tone (1.5 and chapter 8) in the service of destiny. Aprobado por el autor T126

The condition is approved by the GM. It has to be able to happen in this campaign: “when I see the sea of my childhood again” does not work if the game is never leaving the valley. And the GM can trigger it when the story calls for it: destiny does not wait until the player feels like it. T176

Rune magic: magic is paid for in blood. Each rune marks boxes on the caster’s Physical bar (1 per minor rune, 2 per major rune). Aprobado por el autor

Here death is legitimate because the player wrote it themselves: it is the agreed death of 0.7. And Tragedy arrives in the dose we recommend for the darkest tones: a short while, one scene. It is advice, not a rule: the table is grown-up and decides (1.4). C·22oct

Recipes: 6 (“Vikings, Snow and Destiny”).

Sleeves (the body as equipment)

Your mind is software

The Physical bar and the Grid level of the Physical Sphere belong to the sleeve (the body), which is noted on the sheet as equipment, with its own stat block. The skills, the Mental bar and the Spiritual bar belong to the character. T126

  • Changing sleeves causes Mental Damage 1 Dodge: None Soak: None. Here the Mental bar is called Identity (there is no Arcane, so there is no Spiritual bar). Emptying it does not retire the sheet. What waits on the other side is therapy —mind-dancing, reconstruction, time— and you come back as a very different character, which is far better table material than a gap. Severe cases are not played as protagonists unless expressly agreed, and losing the sheet only happens in short games where that ending was agreed from the start (0.7). Decisión del autor ED-R2
  • If the sleeve is taken out of the fight, the mind is uploaded to the cloud and wakes up in a new sleeve (if there is a backup and a connection). The jump costs the same damage to Identity.

Recipes: 9 (“Backing Up Your Soul”).

Clones

Six lives

Each character starts with 6 clones, which are marked on the sheet. When they are taken out of the fight, the table may declare that clone dead: the next one arrives, with the same sheet and full health, usually bringing an unconvincing explanation. Once the sixth is spent, the character leaves the game. Borrador

Here death is a running gag agreed by the table. It does not contradict the pillar “no protagonist dies because of a roll” (0.7): in this genre, dying is part of the humour.

Recipes: 19 (“The Hilarious Dystopia”).

The GM rolls in secret

Behind the screen

The GM makes all risky rolls behind the screen, the players’ ones included, and announces only the narrative outcome. In the extreme variant, they may lie about what came up. Borrador

It fits with the black box of chapter 8: the player declares and the GM narrates. Use it only if the table agrees to it in session zero: the gentlemen’s agreement binds the GM too.

Recipes: 19 (“The Hilarious Dystopia”).

Dice tables

Four tables that switch on depending on the genre. The recipes name them, and they are written, as examples by genre, in Problem tables. What follows is what each one is for.

  • Grievous wounds. It expands the threshold 2 consequences (6.6) with a 1d10 table of specific injuries.
  • Diseases and madness. A table of ailments (physical and mental) for grimy worlds. Recipe 2.
  • Equipment failures. For worlds where nothing works: it widens the fumble range (tool A.3.1 of the Engine Room) for equipment only and, when it triggers, you roll on the table. Recipe 10.
  • Mutations. A table of random mutations for body horror and post-apocalypse. None of the twenty recipes uses it. T119

Transformation

Levelling up by screaming

During a fight, the character transforms and goes up (usually in the Physical Sphere). Like any temporary modifier (2.5), the transformation has its end condition: it ends when the scene ends, because what the genre wants is for it to last as long as the fight does. Decisión del autor

Transforming is not free. What you pay for is the ability to transform, which is yours for good; a momentary transformation lent to you by the plot is not paid for (4.4). The ability is paid for like whatever it raises. If it raises a whole Sphere, it is Grid: 5·n² Keys, with n the number of levels it rises (5.3). If it raises individual Disciplines, at will it is the complete Key, against everything: n² per Discipline (4.3); if it raises so many that the total goes past 5·n², the Sphere’s Grid works out cheaper. Whether it is Limited depends on its conditions. With significant limitations —only under a full moon, out of control, it costs you health…— it is Limited (5.5): half, rounded up, and at least 1 Key. Ending with the scene does not make it cheaper: if you transform at will and whenever you like, it is the complete one, and the end condition is no trick for paying less. C·22oct A single Discipline rising one level costs the same Limited as complete, so there, as advice, it is not worth telling them apart (4.3). One Grid level across the whole Physical Sphere, from 1 to 2, at will, is 5 Keys; the werewolf who only transforms under a full moon and cannot control it is Limited and pays 3. T176 T183

If the GM also changes the damage zoom mid-fight, the transformation combines with Synchronisation (3.8).

Recipes: 17 (“The Martial Arts Tournament (Now With Screaming)”).

Synchronisation

It is a core rule and lives in 3.8: whoever pilots a mecha, wears an exoskeleton or fuses plays with the mecha’s sheet, at its level, for as long as it lasts. It only appears in the chest as a reminder that it exists. T36 T183

Recipes: 18 (“Giant Robots and Teenage Angst”). T176

Environmental damage

The world hurts you

A hostile environment (radiation, extreme cold, vacuum, gas) inflicts damage for each scene of exposure, with no attack roll, and the damage rises the longer you stay: Damage 1 the first scene, Damage 2 the second, The Reaper · Grid 2 the third —anyone below that level is taken out of the fight, with no right to roll (4.5)—, and all of it with Dodge: None Soak: None. Grid 2 is the usual; vacuum or the heart of a reactor carry their own, higher. The counter resets the moment you get out. The right protection (suit, shelter, antidote) is what saves you: with it, the damage can be soaked or disappears. Decisión del autor T176

The extreme case (the helmet that cracks in vacuum) is a higher-level Reaper (4.5).

Recipes: 10 (“Scrap, Petrol and Rust”).

Custom bars (Honour, Reputation and others)

A bar for what matters most

The core has three bars (Physical, Mental, Spiritual) and allows a campaign to create a custom bar or rename an existing one (6.5). They all work the same way: blocks, boxes, thresholds and consequences, and they come back whole when you are safe (6.4). T181 T183

  • Honour (custom bar): takes Social Damage N. Filling it completely is final disgrace. Recipe 4.
  • Reputation / City (custom bar): measures what the hero really has at stake. Recipe 16. T176
  • Thirst (renamed Mental bar): the vampire’s craving, played as withdrawal. Recipe 13.
  • Shadow (renamed Spiritual bar): the corruption evil leaves on those who fight it. Recipe 3.
  • Corruption (renamed Spiritual bar) in grimdark; Humanity and Identity (renamed Mental bar) in cyberpunk and digital minds, because there the loss is cognitive and not supernatural. Recipes 2, 8, 9.

The names come from the recipes, and the specific uses of each bar are in the problem tables. T119

Campaign bar. A custom bar can be campaign-long: it has its own zoom (usually War: 27 points, the slow loss in 6.4) and does not empty when you are safe; it recovers with whatever the fiction says: therapy, a cure, taking the chrome out. Its thresholds leave consequences like any other. Recipe 8. C·28oct

Bonds

The heart as a Key

Each character notes 2 or 3 bonds: people who matter to them, who are non-player characters —the sister, the neighbour, the old master—, not another PC. A bond is useful for two things: T176

  • As a Key (4.4). When the character acts to protect or support a bond present in the scene, they can affect a higher-level threat. Friendship is the narrative permission.
  • As a shield. They can divert damage onto a bond, which suffers in the fiction: an argument, an abandonment, a wound. Personal life is sacrificed to save the rest.

Which bar a bond can shield is decided by the universe. By default, the Mental one. In the gothic, the Spiritual one as well: holding on to somebody who loves you is exactly what holds the Beast back, which is why bonds are half a character sheet there. Decisión del autor

Recipes: 14 (“Kids on bikes vs the Government”).

Watch out for the name clash: “Bonds” is also one of the four Ritual Disciplines (9.2). Context tells them apart —the Discipline is rolled, the bond is written on the sheet— but if they clash at your table, call the ones of the heart “Ties”.

The flat ladder

Optional rule · Growing in instalments

Faster growth, Grids less out of reach. By default, rising n levels with Keys costs n² (4.3). But this is a universal rulebook, and at some point somebody will want to become a god. If you don’t want the price to skyrocket, use 3n−2: 1, 4, 7, 10, 13. The Grid comes down with it, so that it stays the cheapest way to buy a whole Sphere (5/8 of covering it with Keys): 5·(3n−2), that is 5, 20, 35, 50, 65, instead of 5·n². C·22oct

Menu of the 4 costs

Success at a cost and its menu (time, resource, position, friendly fire) are already in the core: the idea, in 1.3, and the menu, in 6.6. Only Unleashed Pulp and Black Comedy carry it, and the recipes played on them take it for granted.

The Style Rule and mook handling are also taken for granted: they are core, and live in 3.5.

Fatigue

Defence gets tired

From the third round of a fight, and every three rounds, −1 to defence rolls (dodge and soak), cumulative. It is recovered once safe, like the bars (6.4). It adds to wound penalties and respects the Anchor’s floor (2.5). C·24oct

Between equals, ties go to the defender and the fight is a long choreography (6.2). Fatigue does not shorten it at first: it makes it converge. Around the tenth round, two fighters who started at +4 defend at +1, and blows start getting through.

Recipes: 17 (“The Martial Arts Tournament (Now with Screaming)”).

Problem tables

Several recipes in 9.4 point to these tables, so here they are. They are not a system, they are examples: rolls for when the GM wants the blow to leave something behind and cannot think what. None is compulsory, all of them can be read instead of rolled, and half of them should not be at your table: each one says which genre it is for.

The rule that covers all of them: a problem is not a number, it is something to do. If what comes up gives you nothing tonight, roll again or take the one next to it. And since NOGUM only uses d10s, all of them are rolled on the red die: if the number is above the table’s last row, roll again. T170

Bars with a name of their own (and which one each falls under)

Almost every genre renames a bar: Corruption, Humanity, Shadow, Empathy, Honour, Blood. That is good, and it is what makes a sheet smell of its world. But before naming it you have to know which bar it hangs from, because that decides how it heals, what it penalises and what happens when it empties: the body, if what wears you down is physical; the mind, if it is cognitive; the spirit, if it is supernatural. The rule, with the example of Humanity (Spirit in the gothic, Mind in cyberpunk), is in 6.5; and if there is no Arcane in your world, there is no Spiritual bar, and what would be Spirit falls to Mind. Cascading safety net

The rungs of the pyramid do not add up: you use the highest one that applies (what does stack, in 2.5). Signature +4, Discipline +2, Domain +1; if you only have something in the Sphere’s other Domain, −1 (Clumsy), and if the Sphere is empty, −2 (Null), because Null is per Sphere, not per skill. The bomb example, step by step, in 2.2. T106 T108

The catalogue of Signatures

Principles.

  • Make it up. The Signature is an open list. The catalogue is an aid, not a limit. T104
  • Broad. A Signature looks like a skill from other games: Firearms, Melee weapons. It is not a specific model or a tiny technique: better “Firearms” than “Sniper”, and better “Pistols” than “.45 revolver”. T98 T100
  • Within its Discipline. Every Signature hangs logically from a Discipline and requires having bought that Discipline and its Domain.
  • Only in specific things. You can only be a Grand Master in something specific. The rest of the Discipline is still rolled at +2. T111
  • Golden rule. To be a Grand Master (+4) at something, you must be able to explain exactly what you are doing.
  • Look up by branch. The catalogue is organised so you only look at your own branch: within Mental → Erudition → Sciences you read a short list, not the full one. T104

The 200 Signatures

About five Signatures per Discipline, sorted by branch, so that nobody has to invent one from scratch in session zero. T142 T143 It is not a closed list: the Signature is yours and you can write whatever you want, as long as you can explain exactly what you are doing. These are here because looking at five examples from your own branch is quicker than looking at a blank page, and because they teach the right size: Firearms, not sniper.

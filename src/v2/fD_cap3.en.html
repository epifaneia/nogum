Chapter 3 · What You Are (the Grid)

The Anchor says how good you are; the Grid, what you are: the scale you play at in each Sphere. When the story is no longer interested in someone making things hard for you, a level separates you, and the one below loses permission even to try, unless the narrative grants them a Key. C·r1

What separates the punch that shatters an island from the one that shatters a jaw is not in the bonus (2.1): it is here. What lets you touch what stands above you is the Key, and it has its own chapter (4).

3.1 What a level means

There comes a moment in every campaign when fighting a certain enemy stops being interesting. The thugs at the docks no longer scare anyone: the table knows it is going to win, and rolling every blow is a wasted evening. That is the moment to sum up that decision by putting a level between you, and most likely by raising the players (3.6), not by lowering the enemy. C·r1

With that level, the kind of advantage changes: it is no longer a better bonus, more health or a better weapon. The weaker one does not roll worse or with a penalty: they do not roll (4.1). C·r1 T189

The power jump rule

It applies to any actor, protagonist or adversary. Compare the Grid level of whoever acts with the level of what they act against, in the Sphere of the action. One level of difference is enough. T57

  • Same level: roll normally, with your sheet’s bonus (2.5) and, if it is an attack, with the full sequence in 6.2.
  • The one above does not roll to succeed: they succeed (level 2 hero against a level 1 thug). If it matters how, they roll Style (3.5).
  • The one below has no right to a roll against the one above (a level 1 human against the serum soldier, level 2). Your blows do nothing to them, and against what they do to you —the monster attacking whoever stands below it— there are no rolls, neither to hit nor for damage: the defeat is narrated. C·22oct Suggested narration: “It’s like punching a mountain. It doesn’t even flinch.”
  • What the one below can do without a Key: run, look for the Key or change plan. C·r1
  • With a Key, the one below rises in what the Key covers and rolls with their full bonus; whatever it does not cover stays as it was (4.2). Numerical superiority can be that Key, one that lasts only that moment (4.4). C·r10 T176 T180 T183

The level makes nobody better at what they know (3.4): it says who has the right to try against whom. T170

Grid or Key? Two faces

The usual question is whether your character is big across the whole Sphere or only at one thing. And it has two faces, one for each way of reading the Grid (3.2): C·r7

  • The tangible one, for worlds that resemble the real one. If your character is superior across the whole Sphere, a hundred times more than those below, it is time to raise their Grid a level.
  • The narrative one, which looks at what interests the story. It may simply be that those fights are no longer interesting, even without such an obvious physical superiority.

In both, what is big across the whole Sphere is Grid. If it is just one thing —their fans, their computer, vampires, holy water—, it is a Key (4.1). The temptation is to give high Grid to everything that sounds huge in the fiction; it almost never is, because fame, talent or the special gadget point at something specific.

3.2 The two readings

What one level of difference means depends on the tone, and there are two answers: C·r2

  • Narrative. The one above is the subject of the story and the one below is not. It says nothing about strength, armour or the world: it says where the camera is.
  • Tangible. The one above really can do a hundred times more than the one below. It is the reading of worlds that want to look like the real one.

The tone decides which one is read, and it is agreed at session zero. It is the book’s thesis applied to scale: emotion dictates physics.

The narrative reading

It is the more intuitive one, because it is the one you already know from stories. Can the dwarf with the axe lift two tons? Nobody knows, and nobody cares. What we do know is that, when he throws himself at a hundred orcs keeping count out loud, nobody in the room fears for him. It is not his dodging, nor his chain mail: he is a hero, and the story does not want the horde to get a roll against him. The same goes for the archer whom the guards cannot hit, or the barbarian of the steppe among hundreds of soldiers: humans, or nearly, who are not the subject of the scene.

In this reading, the Grid does not have to be tied to a real scale. You can have your own scale, but the ×100 probably will not fit: the spiky-haired warrior of anime climbs in far bigger leaps, and the dwarf with the axe is superior without any clear numerical superiority. Fit the scale to your world, or ignore it if it adds nothing. C·r3

The test, here, is this: is the story interested in whoever is below getting a roll? Whoever must be able to hurt a hero like that —the hunter, the one who has trained for exactly that— brings their Key, which is, deep down, asking the table for permission: the ancient vampire does not fall to a fledgling because the dice say so, but when the story allows it. Both readings, in two sheets of the same vampire, are in the case files of chapter 9, ready-made sheets to copy.

The tangible reading: two zeros per level

Universes that aim to emulate the real world needed a number, and we chose one: each level multiplies reach by a hundred across a whole Sphere. For three reasons: it leaves no doubt about superiority, it is very visual, and it grows at the same pace as geographic scales (a person, a room, a building, a city, a country). Everything that follows in this section is about that reading, and only that one. ED-29

If numbers mean something in your world, ×100 is the best choice. We do not recommend changing it: a smaller jump blurs the difference between one level and the next, and between a character at −2 and another at +4. The best boxer in the world stands no chance against a mob of a hundred couch-bound teenagers. Against ten? Maybe. And the maybe gets in the way. C·22oct

To assign a level, do not look at stats. Look at the stage and ask yourself: if this gets completely out of hand, what makes the news?

One level is two zeros

Every rung of the Grid multiplies your reach by a hundred: how many people you get to, how big a problem you touch, how much world you move at once. ED-29

LevelReachThe image
1×1One person
2×100A full room
3×10,000A building, a neighbourhood
4×1,000,000A city
5×100,000,000A country
6×10,000,000,000A planet (the cosmic, 3.7)

With the number in front, this chapter’s hard rule stops looking arbitrary: when someone has a level on you, they are not “better than you”, they are playing with two more zeros. That is why there is no roll. And the Key becomes more dramatic: with it you fight a hundred times above your weight across a whole Discipline (4.1). C·l1

It is reach, not literal truth. A Physical 2 does not lift a hundred times more weight or run a hundred times faster: they face a hundred times more world at once. If someone asks “so how much does that weigh?”, the answer is “whatever the scene needs”.

Shrinking the scale fixes nothing. A tenfold jump is exactly what already separates a −2 from a +4: the pyramid buys that, not the Grid. If ×100 gets out of hand in your campaign, the answer is to leave everybody at Grid 1 and play with skill points, and for whatever lies in between, Keys.

3.3 The Grid in examples

A Grid being does anything in its Sphere at that scale: Orpheus’ song awakens any emotion, not just one. Whatever is huge at a single thing is a Key, and has its own table in 4.8. This one is for beings, and every cell carries its reason, so you do not need to catch the reference. C·22oct

Each row measures one thing: in Physical, how many people you take on at once; in Mental, how much data you cross-reference at once; in Social, how far your blast radius reaches; in Technical, what you build or repair on your own; in Arcane, the supernatural area you touch.

3.4 Sphere by Sphere

The Grid is not one number: it is five. It works like a zoom lens that tells the GM where the camera is in each Sphere. When it is closed (level 1), we see the dirty details of the street and a knife wound is a deadly problem. When it opens (level 5), we see empires and gods; a knife wound is irrelevant, here people throw mountains.

This character is a cyberpunk rocker: an ordinary human, with everything at level 1. Their fame does not raise their Grid, because against fame a human usually keeps their roll to resist: it gives them Keys in their Discipline. With four, which is what it costs to stand two rungs above (4.3), they bring thousands of fans out onto the streets, as a level 3 would. In principle the Key counts in full, without trimming it down to a few (what chapter 4 calls a Limited Key, 4.3): cyberpunk is a connected world, and one call moves their fans. If in your world they are only known in the slums, that is another Key; it is up to the table. What they do not do is step outside their Discipline: they do not win over a crowd that has no idea who they are. A 4 in Social is no longer a rocker: it is Orpheus (3.3). C·22oct

The realistic human Grid is 1 across the board. The real world can affect whole countries —a biological weapon, a political speech—, but in very narrow fields: those are Keys (4.8). In a neon city, usually the only one who goes past 1 is the super chrome job, with a 2 in Physical. If a Grid jump sounds like comics, it is because a Grid jump is comics. C·r4 C·22oct

Ones are everywhere

However big the game, almost all of a character’s Spheres are at 1. A super usually has one or two Spheres above 1, and exceptionally three. The alien in tights and briefs is a 5 in Physical and a 1 in Social. T176 T183 C·22oct

Does the hacker who disables the defences of an entire building have a 3 in Technical? No. A 3 in Technical builds or repairs anything the size of a building. The hacker is level 1, and their computer is a level 3 Key, two above (4.3).

Being is not knowing

  • There is no level 0. Level 1 is an ordinary human. The bonus (−2 to +4) is what you know; the Grid is what you are. You can be at level 1 and be the best in the world in that Sphere. T91
  • Ontology beats competence. The best fighter in the world has no right to a roll against the weakest enhanced human (level 2): the perfect soldier will wipe the floor with you in the contact sport of your choice, even if he has never practised it.
  • The Grid doesn’t measure brains or muscle: it measures starting tools and scale. It is not about doing amazing things (the bonus gives you that), but things on another level, which would require an entire team the size of that level. T133
  • To go past 1 in a Sphere you have to be big across the whole Sphere. Whoever only excels at chemistry has a +4 in Chemistry, like the teacher in the table (3.3), and, if their thing reaches further, a Key.
  • Physically, characters are “naked”: a human body against something much bigger. Technology lets ordinary humans jump that barrier: with a Key (4.8) or, if it raises the whole of Physical, by playing with the sheet of a suit or a mecha while you wear it, which is what we call Synchronisation (3.8). T92

3.5 Style

When you can no longer fail, you don’t roll to see whether: you roll to see how. That is Style. C·a6

Every protagonist’s sheet, and those of the few GM characters made in the same detail (7.1), has a Style section outside the pyramid, with a fixed value of +2. It is a gift from the system and a NOGUM hallmark: it does not change with the sheet or with the genre, and it is not bought. It does not contradict 0.6: here failing is no longer possible, and what is at stake is how you win. If that does not matter either, you don’t roll. T113

The Style Rule (handling mooks)

It kicks in when you face enemies below your Grid level or who are scenery: mooks with no narrative importance, declared by the GM, with no sheet and no roll (7.5). If an enemy can hurt you, it is not scenery: it has a quick sheet, the one the GM makes in ten seconds (7.4), and rolls normally. T176 C·22oct

  1. The premise: there is no doubt you are going to win. You are a level 3 swordmaster and they are level 1 tavern thugs.
  2. The action: it is taken for granted that you hit. Each Style roll takes down one; a critical, several. No hit points are subtracted from the mooks. T176
  3. The Style roll: you roll once with the active tone card (1.4) + Style (+2), to see how you win. Style does not cost: on the cards with success at a cost, that result reads here as a plain success. ED-26
ResultWhat happens
FumbleYou get there, but you look ridiculous. You win anyway: that was never in doubt. What you drop is the pose.
FailureYou get there in the most forgettable, unremarkable way. It works, that is all, and maybe nobody was even watching.
SuccessYou look magnificent. Effective and clean, with pirouettes and astonished faces.
CriticalShow off. You describe the scene, and make it unforgettable: movie choreography, several enemies in one fluid action, rumours born that night. If it fits what is happening, the GM may also hand you a temporary advantage.

A Style critical does not heal. Recovering health with a flourish makes no sense; stress can be negotiated at the table, but not automatically, and the specific effect is decided by the GM together with the player. Decisión del autor

Tone warning

This rule does not suit every universe: few things feel more out of place in a cosmic horror game. The author advises against it in very serious universes. T113

  • Where it shines: anime, wuxia, space opera, action films.
  • Where it breaks immersion: realistic horror, war drama, survival. In the war film about the beach landing, killing an extra costs sweat and fear, and even the last enemy soldier rolls dice: there is no scenery there.

That is why Style sits apart on the sheet, like a sixth Sphere that does not depend on the pyramid.

3.6 Who raises it

The story opens the Grid. It is not bought with skill points, and if one character rises, by default the others receive the equivalent: their own jump or its value in Keys (5.8). C·r8

Grid level rises with the campaign, through narrative milestones, arc changes or time skips, and the GM decides it: often because fighting a certain level has stopped being interesting (3.1). But every jump is worth an exact number of Keys (5.3), and that makes it exchangeable: whoever does not want to rise collects its value, and whoever wants to adjust their rise has the means. The permission belongs to the story; the price, to the rule. T63–T64 Decisión del autor, 17 sep T176 T181 T183

The Grid changes the universe, not your competence. Levelling up changes the scale at which you operate and what the world lets you touch; it does not make you more skilled. That is the job of the pyramid (2.2), and of advantages and flaws (5.8).

The Grid you start with is in 5.2. C·r5

3.7 The ceilings

There are several ceilings, and it is worth keeping them apart: the starting maximum, the world maximum and the player maximum. All three are Grid ceilings; Keys are better left without one. C·r6

Starting maximum
The highest a sheet can start with. By default, a 2: the recommended start is everything at 1 except one Sphere at 2, where the universe allows it (5.2).
World maximum
The highest that exists in that universe, whether anyone plays it or not (the cosmic, below).
Player maximum
The highest a player can come to have across a whole Sphere, however long the campaign lasts. It is the one the recipes carry, the ready-made settings of chapter 9, Sphere by Sphere (9.4).

They do not have to match. In a cyberpunk game, usually no player reaches Mental 2, however long the campaign runs: there is no wits-times-a-hundred. What there are, are chrome jobs on the edge of psychosis, who reach Physical 2, and there the genre ends. A vampire world lets you climb very high, and even so a new sheet usually starts low. ED-30 T188 C·r6

The player maximum, more than tone or equipment, is what makes a universe feel like that universe. Each recipe (9.4) carries its own, Sphere by Sphere, as a proposal, and it is answered at session zero: is it possible in your world? T176 T184

Keys, better without a ceiling

Ceilings speak of whole Spheres; Keys do not. They can always surprise you with a plan that affects a huge number of people, and in universes with the internet, aeroplanes or weapons of mass destruction there are plenty of latent Keys. In cyberpunk there is no wits-times-a-hundred, but there are Keys: the computer genius has the exploit that works against that AI, and a forensic lab of dozens of people, to which you send samples, encrypted clues and compounds, is Investigation times a hundred (4.8). C·r6 T184

If the word “ceiling” muddles things, don’t worry: it is not that important a concept. What matters is the question. C·r6

The cosmic

The cosmic is level 6: the level of planets, in the tangible reading. Most universes do not have it, and at most tables it is unplayable: primordial gods and world-devouring entities are usually the world maximum (there are cosmic creatures, and generally nobody plays one), and they are touched with Keys. But nothing prevents a cosmic game in which each player runs a world-devourer, with the same rules; and in universes with no ceiling, a Grid rise can get there: think of the spiky-haired warrior who never stops powering up. C·22oct T92 T134

3.8 Synchronisation

Whoever pilots a mecha, wears an exoskeleton or a suit, or fuses with an entity plays with the mecha’s sheet (or the suit’s, or the entity’s), at its level, for as long as the bond lasts. If there is a sheet, it replaces yours. An ordinary vehicle inherits nothing. C·r9 C·22oct

It is the rule of anime’s giant robots and of heroes who transform and combine. While the bond lasts, the protagonist is the mecha: you play with its sheet, and the GM can change mid-session even how many blows health can take, what chapter 6 calls the zoom (6.4). In the recipe of giant robots and teenage angst (recipe 18, 9.4), outside the robot the pilot is level 1 with the shortest health, Lethal, and the blows of the drama land on the Mental bar (6.5); inside, they play with the robot’s sheet, level 4, and health switches to the longest, War. T36 T176 T183

An ordinary vehicle inherits nothing. They can shoot the vehicle, which has its own health, or you: if bullets get in through an ordinary window, they take health off you wherever you are; an armoured one may have to be damaged first. The scene decides, and common sense. T181

Synchronisation is Grid, and it is paid for as Grid (5.3); its price is in 5.6. T183

It’s a mecha: far too cool not to give it the spotlight it deserves. C·22oct

3.9 Clarifications

Which level is compared? The one for the Sphere of the action. The vampire’s presence (Social 2) spellbinds the room, but it is no help in an arm-wrestle if their Physical is 1 (3.1). T57

Can I put the 2 you start with (5.2) in Social without a single point in Social? If your universe allows a 2 in Social, you can, and you will win without rolling against any human. But the moment you peer into the world above —another vampire, the hive queen— you are at −2, because you cannot do anything in that Sphere (2.2). No need to forbid it: it is a sheet that wastes its best ability. Being is not knowing (3.4).
